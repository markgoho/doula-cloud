# What a platform must get a Practice's agreement to at signup, and what form counts

Research for [What a platform must get a Practice's agreement to at signup, and what form counts (#1490)](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1490), on the map [`/signup` to First Value (#1487)](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1487). Checked 27 September 2026.

## Method

Three kinds of evidence, and they are labeled inline: **read first-party, fetched directly** — the actual page or opinion text was retrieved and quoted from it (Stripe's own docs, `nysenate.gov`, `uscode.house.gov`, `leginfo.legislature.ca.gov`, and the FindLaw mirrors of *Specht* and *Meyer*, both fetched and quoted below from the opinion's own words); **read first-party, quoted from a search-engine summary of the primary source** — the statute or opinion is the source, but the exact sentence was returned by a search rather than a direct fetch of the document (this applies to *Nicosia*, *Berkson*, and the SHIELD Act and CalOPPA sections, and each is flagged where it happens); and **verified live**, meaning already established in this repository from a Sandbox walk rather than read about now. Section "What could not be confirmed" lists which sources fall into the second category and why the direct fetch failed. Today `/signup` (`app/src/routes/(signed-out)/signup/+page.svelte`) captures no agreement of any kind — no checkbox, no linked terms, no privacy notice — confirmed by reading the file directly.

Earlier records already answered adjacent questions and are not redone here. [The Stripe-facing website content, and where it lives (#390)](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390) and [`docs/research/stripe-website-review-mechanics.md`](stripe-website-review-mechanics.md) settled what `doula.cloud` itself must publish for Stripe's platform-activation review — a different requirement from this ticket's, which is what a *Practice* agrees to when *she* signs up for the product. [What a pilot Practice needs on its own Stripe account, and at acceptance (#421)](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/421) and [Publish the pilot terms, unlisted, and link them where a Practice accepts (#444)](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/444) settled the *pilot promotion's* terms and where they are shown — a promotion under Stripe's own rule, not the general terms of service this ticket is about. [ADR-0007](../adr/0007-connect-account-state-is-two-capabilities-and-a-requirements-list.md) and [`docs/research/stripe-platform-production-requirements.md`](stripe-platform-production-requirements.md) already establish the direct-charge shape, the `full` service agreement, and that Stripe's hosted onboarding captures the connected account's own acceptance of the Stripe Connected Account Agreement. This ticket is the first to ask what Doula Cloud itself must get a Practice to agree to, in its own words, at `/signup` — before any Stripe Connect flow ever starts, and regardless of whether a Practice ever connects Stripe at all, because she stores a Client's health-adjacent records the moment she creates an Engagement, not the moment she connects a bank account.

[#375](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/375) ruled *drafting* Doula Cloud's actual terms of service, privacy policy, HIPAA posture and pilot agreements out of scope for that map, calling it "product-legal work needing a lawyer, on its own map." This ticket does not redraft that ruling or write that legal text. It answers a narrower, answerable question: what *form* an agreement must take to bind a Practice, and what the signup screen must capture about it — mechanism, not prose. The Practice-facing legal text itself is separate work, chartered elsewhere, that this determination assumes will exist before `/signup` links to it.

## 1. What Stripe requires, and where in the flow

**Stripe requires two separate agreements, at two separate moments, and Doula Cloud owes work at only one of them.**

**The Stripe Connected Account Agreement is between Stripe and the Practice, captured by Stripe, later.** [Service agreement types](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/service-agreement-types) states that a `full` service agreement — the one required for `card_payments` — "creates a service relationship between Stripe and the connected account holder," and "Stripe handles the service agreement acceptance if you use Stripe-hosted onboarding or Embedded onboarding." Doula Cloud uses Stripe-hosted onboarding ([ADR-0007](../adr/0007-connect-account-state-is-two-capabilities-and-a-requirements-list.md)), so this acceptance needs no code of Doula Cloud's own. The Sandbox walk on [#421](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/421) verified this directly on a real v2 account's `currently_due` list: `identity.attestations.terms_of_service.account.{date,ip}` is one of the fifteen entries Stripe's hosted flow itself clears, and the walked account's tenth step is literally "Review and submit against the Connected Account Agreement and Acquirer Disclosure." That moment is later than `/signup` — it happens whenever the Owner starts the Connect flow, which the map ([#1487](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1487)) assigns to a different, not-yet-built screen ([#442](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/442)), not to signup itself.

**The actual "must" on Doula Cloud's own terms of service is fee disclosure, not the Stripe agreement's placement — Stripe only offers the ToS route as one convenient way to satisfy a narrower obligation.** [Update service agreement acceptances](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/updating-service-agreements) frames including Stripe's agreement in the platform's own terms as an option, not a requirement — "You *can* make accepting Stripe's service agreement easy for connected accounts by including it in your terms of service" — and that whole section of the page is scoped to accounts with no Stripe-hosted Dashboard access, which is not Doula Cloud's shape (ADR-0007 gives every Practice `dashboard: full`). The genuine "must" was already found on this map: `docs/research/stripe-platform-production-requirements.md`, section 3, quoting the Connected Account Agreement directly — "User should read User's Platform Provider Agreement carefully to understand the nature of the Platform Services and the Activity," and "Stripe does not control and is not responsible for fees imposed by a Stripe Connect Platform, which **should be made clear to User in User's Platform Provider Agreement**." That document — a "Platform Provider Agreement" disclosing what Doula Cloud charges — is what the CAA actually obligates Doula Cloud to have, and it is squarely the "product-legal work" [#375](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/375) put out of scope for drafting. Stripe's model sentence naming the Connected Account Agreement (quoted in `updating-service-agreements`) remains the recommended, but optional, way to fold that disclosure into a single terms-of-service document rather than a separate one.

**Doula Cloud must also disclose Stripe's Privacy Policy, and that duty is not conditioned on hosted onboarding.** The same page, under "Disclosing how Stripe processes connected account data": "While providing your connected accounts with Connect services, Stripe processes their data as explained in Stripe's Privacy Policy... You must disclose that to your accounts by providing them with a link to that policy." Unlike the service-agreement acceptance, this sentence is not qualified by onboarding type — it is a platform disclosure duty regardless of who captures the click. It is satisfiable either in Doula Cloud's own terms of service (alongside the Stripe service-agreement sentence above) or on the later connect-specific screen; either placement is defensible, and the map's own note that "the screens inside Stripe's Connect flow" are out of this map's scope while "the moment and the words that ask her to connect" are in scope ([#1487](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1487)) puts the natural home on the connect screen (#442) rather than duplicating it at general signup.

**The Acquirer Disclosure is handled for Doula Cloud, conditionally.** [Update service agreement acceptances](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/updating-service-agreements) requires the platform to advise connected accounts of Stripe's acquirer "in a clear and conspicuous manner," but adds: "If your accounts provide acceptance of Stripe's service agreement through a Stripe onboarding product, it includes that disclosure." Hosted onboarding is a Stripe onboarding product, and the Sandbox walk already observed the "Acquirer Disclosure" step inside it. Nothing for Doula Cloud to build.

**None of this is conditioned on general signup at all — it is conditioned on the Stripe Connect flow, which a Practice may never start.** A Practice can create a Practice, invite staff, and store a Client's health-adjacent record entirely without connecting Stripe. The four questions this ticket exists to answer are about that broader, Stripe-independent moment: signup itself, which every Practice hits, whether or not she ever takes a payment.

## 2. What form of online agreement New York and federal law treat as enforceable

**The statutes say electronic form is not the problem; they say almost nothing about screen design.** The federal E-SIGN Act, [15 U.S.C. § 7001(a)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A15+section%3A7001+edition%3Aprelim%29): "a signature, contract, or other record relating to \[a\] transaction may not be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because it is in electronic form," and a contract "may not be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because an electronic signature or electronic record was used in its formation." New York's own Electronic Signatures and Records Act, State Technology Law [§ 304(2)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/STT/304): "An electronic signature may be used by a person in lieu of a signature affixed by hand. The use of an electronic signature shall have the same validity and effect as the use of a signature affixed by hand." Neither statute specifies a checkbox, a signature pad, or any particular UI. What actually decides enforceability — whether a court finds a contract was formed at all — is ordinary contract-formation law: notice and assent, which is where the case law does the real work.

**E-SIGN's heavier consumer-consent regime, § 7001(c), is not triggered by ordinary signup.** § 7001(c) applies only "if a statute, regulation, or other rule of law requires that information relating to a transaction... be provided or made available to a consumer in writing" — it governs *statutorily mandated written disclosures* (the kind of thing seen in lending or certain insurance contexts), not the act of forming a contract by clicking to accept terms. Signing up for practice-management software does not fall under any such external writing mandate, so `/signup` does not need E-SIGN's separate "confirm you can access records electronically" consent flow. § 7001(a)'s plain rule — electronic form is not disqualifying — is the only piece of E-SIGN in play here.

**The Second Circuit's own taxonomy, read from the opinions themselves, is what actually decides the design question.** Three shapes, ranked from least to most defensible:

- **Browsewrap fails.** *Specht v. Netscape Communications Corp.*, 306 F.3d 17 (2d Cir. 2002), read directly from the [FindLaw mirror of the opinion](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/1002960.html): once a user clicked the visible "Download" button, "these plaintiffs encountered no further information about the plug-in program or the existence of license terms governing its use" — the terms sat only on a hidden, scrollable portion below the button they had already clicked. The court held that "a reasonably prudent offeree in plaintiffs' position would not have known or learned, prior to acting on the invitation to download, of the reference to SmartDownload's license terms hidden below the 'Download' button," so "plaintiffs' bare act of downloading the software did not unambiguously manifest assent to the arbitration provision." The lesson for `/signup`: a link to terms that a Practice never has to see before the button she actually clicks fires the create-Practice action is not enough.
- **Clickwrap and Meyer's "sign-in-wrap" both succeed, and a checkbox is not the deciding fact.** *Meyer v. Uber Technologies, Inc.*, 868 F.3d 66 (2d Cir. 2017), read directly from the [FindLaw mirror of the opinion](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/1871227.html) — Justia's copy of the same opinion refused this session's fetch (403) — enforced an arbitration clause where the registration screen carried the sentence "By creating an Uber account, you agree to the TERMS OF SERVICE & PRIVACY POLICY" directly below the registration button, with "TERMS OF SERVICE & PRIVACY POLICY" as a conspicuous, blue, underlined hyperlink — **no checkbox at all.** The court's test is two-pronged: **reasonably conspicuous notice** and **unambiguous manifestation of assent.** On notice, the opinion turns on spatial and temporal coupling: "The text, including the hyperlinks... appears directly below the buttons for registration. The entire screen is visible at once, and the user does not need to scroll," notice given "simultaneously to enrollment," with "dark print" contrasting "the bright white background" despite a small font. On assent: "A reasonable user would know that by clicking the registration button, he was agreeing to the terms and conditions accessible via the hyperlink, whether he clicked on the hyperlink or not" — the button itself is the assent mechanism, and Specht's browsewrap is distinguished exactly because Specht's page required no affirmative click coupled to the terms at all, while Uber's did.
- **A coupling failure is enough to put the question to a jury rather than settle it as a matter of law — quoted here from a search-engine return of the opinion, not a direct fetch (both attempted fetches of the opinion itself were refused; see "What could not be confirmed").** *Nicosia v. Amazon.com, Inc.*, 834 F.3d 220 (2d Cir. 2016): the message "By placing your order, you agree to Amazon's... conditions of use" "was not bold, capitalized, or conspicuous in light of the whole webpage," and the court held "reasonable minds could disagree" on whether the checkout page gave "reasonably conspicuous notice." The procedural posture matters as much as the fact: this was a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, and because Amazon could not show conspicuous notice *as a matter of law*, the claim survived to be litigated rather than being resolved on the pleadings — the opposite of what happened in Meyer, where the design was clean enough for the Second Circuit to decide the question itself. *Berkson v. Gogo LLC*, 97 F. Supp. 3d 359 (E.D.N.Y. 2015), also quoted from a search-engine return of the opinion, coined "sign-in-wrap" for exactly this middle category and held Gogo's version unenforceable because nothing on the page gave the user "a compelling reason to examine" terms that were neither clicked-through nor visually coupled to the sign-in action.

**The line these four cases draw together is coupling, not the presence of a checkbox.** A checkbox is one way to force coupling (the user cannot proceed without an affirmative click tied to the exact sentence); Meyer shows that a sentence positioned directly beneath, and visible at the same time as, the button that submits the form does the identical legal work without one. What fails, in every case above, is a design where the terms notice and the action that supposedly manifests assent to it are not read by the court as the same moment — separated by scrolling, by page location, or by an unclear tie between "click here to finish" and "this is what clicking means."

## 3. What a privacy policy must be shown or agreed to at account creation

**No New York statute requires a privacy policy to be posted or agreed to at account creation; New York's data-privacy statute regulates safeguards, not disclosure.** The SHIELD Act, General Business Law § 899-bb — quoted here from a search-engine return citing [FindLaw's mirror](https://codes.findlaw.com/ny/general-business-law/gbs-sect-899-bb/) and cross-checked against [IRMI's practitioner summary of the same section](https://www.irmi.com/articles/expert-commentary/the-new-york-shield-act-reasonable-security-requirement), not fetched directly (`nysenate.gov`'s own page for this section returned "entry could not be found") — requires anyone holding a New York resident's private information "to develop, implement and maintain reasonable safeguards to protect the security, confidentiality and integrity of that private information": administrative, technical and physical safeguards, with a lighter standard for a business under 50 employees. It is a security-program duty, and it says nothing about posting a privacy notice or capturing a click at signup. Recorded as a gap deliberately: SHIELD tells Doula Cloud to have a safeguards *program*, not to show a document at `/signup`.

**California's statute does require a posted privacy policy, and it reaches Doula Cloud because Practices are sold nationally.** The California Online Privacy Protection Act, California Business and Professions Code § 22575(a), quoted from a verbatim mirror at [codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/business-and-professions-code/bpc-sect-22575/): "An operator of a commercial Web site or online service that collects personally identifiable information through the Internet about individual consumers residing in California... shall conspicuously post its privacy policy." The "any person or company in the United States... whose website collects personally identifiable information from California consumers" framing found in this sweep is a secondary paraphrase of CalOPPA's reach, not the statute's own text, and is flagged as such rather than quoted as law. One real caveat: CalOPPA's "consumer" is defined (§ 22577) around personal, family, or household purposes, so a Practice Owner buying practice-management software for her business may sit outside that definition — but the Clients whose health-adjacent records the product stores, and any Client-portal user, are squarely inside it, so the posting duty holds regardless of whether the Owner herself counts. Doula Cloud sells to US Practices with no state exclusion, so a California-resident Owner, Staff member, or Client is expected, not hypothetical. CalOPPA's obligation is to **post conspicuously**, not to force a click-through; it does not ask for a checkbox, only for the policy to be reachable and visible.

**Putting sections 2 and 3 together, the same design satisfies both.** A privacy-policy link that sits, Meyer-style, directly beside the same sentence that links the terms of service — both visible on the signup screen, both coupled to the submit action — is simultaneously (a) the design a Second Circuit court would treat as giving conspicuous notice under contract law, and (b) a conspicuous posting under CalOPPA. No second, disconnected privacy-policy page is required to satisfy either regime; one sentence with two links does both jobs. Uber's own model sentence already reads this way: "By creating an Uber account, you agree to the TERMS OF SERVICE & PRIVACY POLICY."

**HIPAA does not apply today, and a privacy policy is not what would change that if it did.** [`docs/research`'s answer to #30](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/30) (recorded in that issue's resolution comment, not yet promoted to a standalone file) established that HIPAA binds only a *covered entity* — a health plan, clearinghouse, or a provider who transmits health information in a HIPAA standard electronic transaction such as an insurance or Medicaid claim (45 CFR § 160.103) — or a *business associate* acting on a covered entity's behalf. An independent doula who never bills insurance or Medicaid electronically is not, by that test, a covered entity, and neither is Doula Cloud a business associate of one, so no Business Associate Agreement is owed and HIPAA's own notice-of-privacy-practices requirement does not attach. [ADR-0012](../adr/0012-mailgun-is-vendor-no-baa-needed-for-v1.md) records the live consequence of the same test for a different vendor relationship: "once *any* tenant Practice becomes a covered entity" the trigger could fire platform-wide, because the shared infrastructure cannot silo compliance per tenant — New York has been expanding Medicaid doula reimbursement, which is exactly the electronic-billing trigger. **This is a fact to monitor, not a fact that changes what `/signup` must show today.** Whatever privacy policy Doula Cloud publishes should describe what it actually does with Client data honestly — the Meyer/CalOPPA analysis above already requires a real, accurate policy to exist and be linked — but no HIPAA-specific consent or Notice of Privacy Practices is owed at signup while the covered-entity trigger has not fired.

## 4. What must be recorded about the agreement

**No statute in this sweep names a specific record format for a clickwrap-style acceptance; *Meyer* itself shows what a defendant actually needed to prove, and needed records to prove it with.** Uber's motion to compel arbitration rested on a declaration from a named engineer, Vincent Mi, who represented that Uber kept records of when and how each user registered, and who used those records to identify the exact date and method — an Android phone, October 2014 — Meyer had registered by; attached to that declaration were screenshots of the two specific screens a user registering on that date and platform would have seen. The Second Circuit's holding that notice was conspicuous rests on those reconstructed screenshots, not on a general description of "the Uber sign-up flow." Read against *Nicosia*'s opposite outcome — where the same kind of question (was this checkout page's notice conspicuous) went to a jury instead of being resolved on the pleadings, because the design itself, not a records gap, left room for reasonable disagreement — the shared lesson is that both the *design* and the *record* have to be strong enough to let a court decide the question as a matter of law rather than send it to trial. The practical requirement is that whoever cannot reconstruct **which version of the terms a specific person saw, and when, and that they took the action bound to it** cannot make Meyer's argument if it is ever challenged.

**Stripe's own analogous field is the template already in this codebase's vocabulary.** [Update service agreement acceptances](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/updating-service-agreements) records exactly two facts against an account's own service-agreement acceptance: `tos_acceptance[date]` and `tos_acceptance[ip]`. The Sandbox walk on [#421](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/421) observed the v2 equivalent live: `identity.attestations.terms_of_service.account.date` and `.account.ip`. Stripe — a company whose own agreements are litigated in exactly the cases cited above — records timestamp and IP address as the minimum evidentiary pair, and nothing more elaborate.

**Doula Cloud's own cross-cutting audit-trail expectation asks for the same thing it always asks for.** `CLAUDE.md`'s audit-trail rule: "A user must be able to answer 'how did this thing come to be?'... Anything that changes state records who did it and when." Agreeing to the terms of service is exactly such a state change, so it needs no special-case reasoning — it needs the same treatment every other Doula Cloud state change already gets.

**What that means concretely for `/signup`:** at the moment the Practice is created, record (1) **who** — the Staff record (the Owner) whose account is agreeing, since she is the one clicking "Create Practice"; (2) **when** — a timestamp, server-side, not client-supplied; (3) **which version** — an identifier for the exact terms-of-service and privacy-policy text in force at that moment, so a later edit to the copy does not retroactively change what a past Practice is deemed to have agreed to; and (4), following Stripe's own minimum and the evidentiary lesson of *Meyer*/*Nicosia*, the **IP address** the request arrived from. All four together let Doula Cloud answer, months or years later, "she agreed, to this exact text, at this time, from this address" — the same question the case law shows actually gets litigated.

## Determination

**A declaration sentence, in reading order immediately above the button, plus a button whose own label states the action — not a checkbox — is the form Doula Cloud should use at `/signup`.** ADR-0021 requires checking `docs/design/govuk-alignment.md` before any form decision, and having checked it (its table has no consent-checkbox pattern recorded either way) and GOV.UK's own [Check answers pattern](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/check-answers/) directly, the recorded example is a declaration sentence — "By submitting this application you are confirming that, to the best of your knowledge, the details you are providing are correct" — followed by a button labeled to state what it does, such as "Accept and send," with the guidance "make sure the 'submit' button clearly shows the action it performs." GOV.UK's [Checkboxes component](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/checkboxes/) page, also fetched directly, says nothing for or against a checkbox for this purpose — it simply is not the pattern GOV.UK demonstrates for a declaration-before-submission moment. Following it: the text reads "By creating your Practice, you agree to the Terms of Service and the Privacy Policy," with both as visibly distinct hyperlinks, positioned directly above the submit button; the button itself is relabeled from "Create Practice" to something that states the action, such as "Accept and create Practice."

**This design is Meyer's own shape, corrected for the one gap Meyer's screen actually had.** Section 2 already established that Meyer's court enforced Uber's terms on exactly this structure — a declaration sentence coupled to a button, no checkbox — because "a reasonable user would know that by clicking the registration button, he was agreeing to the terms and conditions accessible via the hyperlink." What Uber's own screen did not do is put that meaning into the button's own accessible name: a screen-reader user tabbing directly to a button labeled only "Register" hears nothing about terms unless the reading order happens to carry the preceding sentence into the same context. Relabeling the button "Accept and create Practice" closes that gap by GOV.UK's own preferred mechanism — a descriptive button label — rather than by adding a checkbox GOV.UK's pattern does not use. It is at least as strong a fit for Meyer's "unambiguous manifestation of assent" prong as a checkbox would have been, because the assent is now named on the very control a screen reader announces, and it costs nothing that ADR-0021 would have to record as a departure.

**On Stripe (section 1):** nothing about the Stripe Connected Account Agreement belongs on `/signup`. Doula Cloud's own terms-of-service text, once drafted, must include Stripe's suggested sentence naming and linking the Connected Account Agreement, and must (there, or on the later Connect-specific screen the map assigns to [#442](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/442)) link Stripe's Privacy Policy. Stripe's hosted onboarding captures the Practice's actual acceptance of its own agreement later, automatically, with no code from Doula Cloud.

**On the privacy policy (section 3):** the same declaration sentence and the same two links satisfy CalOPPA's conspicuous-posting duty and the Second Circuit's notice-and-assent test at once. No separate HIPAA consent is owed today, because the covered-entity trigger has not fired for any tenant; that is a fact to watch, not a fact that changes `/signup`.

**On what is recorded (section 4):** the Staff record's id, a server-set timestamp, an identifier for the exact version of the terms and privacy policy in force at that moment, and the request's IP address, all written at the same time the Practice itself is created — the same audit-trail treatment every other state change in this repository already gets, following the evidentiary minimum Stripe's own `tos_acceptance` fields and the *Meyer*/*Nicosia* record fights both point to.

**What this determination assumes and does not do.** It assumes a real, accurate terms-of-service and privacy-policy text exists to link — drafting that text is the "product-legal work" [#375](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/375) put out of scope for its own map, and this ticket does not reopen that ruling or write that prose. It answers only the mechanism question the ticket asked: what form of agreement is sufficient, and what must be captured about it.

## What could not be confirmed

1. **Which sources were fetched and read directly, versus quoted from a search engine's summary of them.** Fetched and quoted directly: Stripe's `service-agreement-types` and `updating-service-agreements` pages, `docs/research/stripe-platform-production-requirements.md`, 15 U.S.C. § 7001(a) and (c) (via `uscode.house.gov`), New York State Technology Law § 304 (via `nysenate.gov`), *Specht v. Netscape* and *Meyer v. Uber* (both via FindLaw's mirror; Justia's copy of each returned HTTP 403 to this session), the GOV.UK Check answers pattern and Checkboxes component pages, `docs/design/govuk-alignment.md`, and `app/src/routes/(signed-out)/signup/+page.svelte`. Quoted from a search engine's return rather than a direct fetch of the source: *Nicosia v. Amazon.com* (CourtListener and Justia both refused or returned empty content to this session), *Berkson v. Gogo* (never attempted directly, given Nicosia's failures), the SHIELD Act's General Business Law § 899-bb (`nysenate.gov` returned "entry could not be found" for this section), and CalOPPA's own cross-border scope statement in section 3 (the statute's *text*, § 22575(a), was fetched directly from FindLaw's mirror; the "reaches any company in the US" framing was not). Every quotation above is attributed to whichever category it actually falls in, rather than presented as uniformly primary.
2. **Whether Stripe's platform profile (the second, live-mode-only approval behind the Dashboard) asks for anything about the Practice's own consent language.** [`docs/research/stripe-platform-production-requirements.md`](stripe-platform-production-requirements.md) already flagged this as unconfirmable without a live account, and nothing in this research changes that.
3. **Whether any other state's equivalent of CalOPPA reaches a stricter result than California's.** Only California and New York were checked, because California is the one first-party statute found that mandates *posting* a privacy policy at all, and New York is the entity's home state. A Practice's or Client's residence in a third state was not swept.
4. **The exact evidentiary weight New York state courts (as opposed to the Second Circuit sitting in diversity or on federal claims) give to a sign-in-wrap design.** *Berkson* is an Eastern District of New York opinion applying New York contract law, and *Meyer* is a Second Circuit opinion also applying New York law to the Uber agreement, so both are on point, but no New York Court of Appeals decision was located that resolves this exact question at the state's highest court.
5. **The exact contents of the terms-of-service and privacy-policy text.** Deliberately out of scope, per [#375](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/375) and this ticket's own instruction to determine the *form*, not draft the prose.
