# What tech E&O and cyber insurance cost, and what they cover, for a one-person SaaS company

Research for [issue #386](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/386), "What insurance to carry, and what event binds it", on the map [The business behind Doula Cloud](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/375). Question: **what do insurers themselves publish about starting prices and coverage for a one-person software company, and what do New York statute and HHS say about breach-notification and HIPAA insurance obligations?**

Facts held fixed, from the map and CLAUDE.md: the business is a **single-member New York LLC**, pre-revenue, under $50,000 in near-term revenue, holding **health-adjacent Client records** (pregnancy, birth, postpartum care), with a **Google Cloud BAA held in the founder's personal name** pending the LLC's formation.

**This is not insurance or legal advice.** It is a record of what insurers, New York statute, and HHS themselves publish, with each claim traced to its source. Researched **19 September 2026**. Insurer starting prices are marketing-page figures, not underwritten quotes, and are the kind of number that moves; treat them as a floor, not an estimate for this business specifically. Several first-party pages (Hiscox, Thimble, HHS.gov) returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch; where that happened, the figure is corroborated by a live search snippet of that same page rather than a secondary write-up, and this is flagged inline.

## 1. Are tech E&O and cyber sold as one combined policy for small tech firms

Not as a single flat-priced product from any insurer checked, but the two are routinely **bundled into one policy document with one combined limit**, sold and underwritten together. Insureon's own SaaS-company page: the average figure it quotes, "$126 per month, or $1,516 annually, for tech E&O insurance," is explicitly a bundle — the search snippet of that page states the policy "bundles errors and omissions insurance with cyber insurance at a lower cost than buying the policies separately," at $1M per-occurrence/aggregate limits and a $2,500 deductible ([insureon.com](https://www.insureon.com/technology-business-insurance/saas-companies/cost), retrieved 19 September 2026). Embroker names the product itself "Technology Errors and Omissions / Cyber (commonly called Tech E&O / Cyber) insurance" and states it offers "tailored Tech E&O / Cyber solutions designed specifically for tech companies," while declining to publish a flat price because "policies are priced based on individual companies" ([embroker.com](https://www.embroker.com/coverage/tech-errors-omissions/), retrieved 19 September 2026). Next Insurance sells cyber only as an **add-on rider** to an existing general-liability or professional-liability policy, at $4/month or $15/month respectively, rather than as a bundled single line ([nextinsurance.com](https://www.nextinsurance.com/cyber-liability-insurance/), retrieved 19 September 2026). Coalition and Vouch publish no bundled price at all; both are quote-based. For a one-person company under $50k revenue, the practical shape is: one insurer, one binder, two coverage sections (tech E&O and cyber) under one combined limit — not two separate policies, and not one line-item price either.

## 2. Does a standard cyber policy cover SHIELD Act notification and credit-monitoring costs

Yes, in kind — a standard small-business cyber policy's first-party "breach response" section is built to pay exactly the categories of cost the SHIELD Act can force a business to incur, though the statute itself does not mandate credit monitoring. Next Insurance's own cyber liability page lists "Customer notification" and "Credit monitoring or identity protection services" as costs the policy responds to ([nextinsurance.com](https://www.nextinsurance.com/cyber-liability-insurance/), retrieved 19 September 2026). What the SHIELD Act itself requires, read directly from the statute text at nysenate.gov (retrieved 19 September 2026): **GBL §899-aa** requires a business that owns or licenses computerized private information to notify affected New York residents "in the most expedient time possible" after discovering unauthorized access or acquisition, and the notice must include the business's contact information, the phone numbers and websites of the relevant state and federal agencies "that provide information regarding security breach response and identity theft prevention and protection information," and a description of the categories of information involved (GBL §899-aa, subd. 7, [nysenate.gov](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/899-AA)). The statute does **not** itself require the business to pay for or offer credit monitoring — two direct fetches of the statute's full text found no such mandate; a business only needs to point affected people at existing state/federal identity-theft resources. **GBL §899-bb** separately requires "reasonable" administrative, technical, and physical safeguards for private information, with a lighter standard for businesses under 50 employees, $3M revenue, or $5M in assets, and makes noncompliance a violation of GBL §349 enforceable by the Attorney General, with no private right of action ([nysenate.gov](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/899-BB)). So the exposure a cyber policy is priced against is real — notification, forensics, and (voluntarily offered) credit monitoring — even though only the notification duty, not the credit-monitoring cost, is a statutory mandate in New York.

## 3. Is a HIPAA business associate required to carry cyber insurance

No — HIPAA itself does not require it, and search-snippet review of HHS's own "Business Associate Contracts" sample-provisions page (direct fetch returned HTTP 403 from hhs.gov; content corroborated via a live search snippet of that exact HHS URL, retrieved 19 September 2026) confirms the required contract provisions are: permitted/required uses and disclosures, a bar on further use or disclosure, safeguards including HIPAA Security Rule compliance, breach reporting, subcontractor flow-down of the same restrictions, and termination/return-or-destroy-PHI on contract end. **Insurance appears nowhere in that list.** This matches the plain reading of 45 CFR 164.504(e), which HHS's page restates. In practice, though, **covered entities commonly do ask vendors for proof of cover.** A search-snippet review of an insurer-published BAA checklist and vendor-guidance material (Beazley's business-associate-agreement checklist and Bite-Size HIPAA's guidance on requiring cyber liability from business associates, both retrieved 19 September 2026) states that business associates "should provide a Certificate of Insurance naming the Covered Entity as certificate holder," with limits, endorsements, and a declarations page, and that "provisions pertaining to cyber insurance and breach expense reimbursement are increasingly commonplace in BAA contracts" even though "the cyber liability provision is not explicitly required by HIPAA itself." So: not a HIPAA requirement, but a contractual ask that shows up often enough in vendor-side BAA templates that a pilot Practice could reasonably raise it — consistent with issue #386's own note that "a 14-doula agency may well ask what cover exists before handing over its Clients."

## 4. Does a no-premises, no-visitors, no-physical-product business need general liability

Insurers' own pages hedge rather than exempt. Hiscox frames general liability as covering "claims by others for medical costs, bodily injury, property damage" and prices it "starting at $30 a month," but its own worked example for a business like this is a **home-office visitor injury** — "even if a vendor comes to your home office and trips and falls, your homeowner's insurance likely won't cover this... a general liability policy could cover the associated medical costs" ([hiscox.com](https://www.hiscox.com/small-business-insurance/general-liability-insurance), retrieved 19 September 2026) — a scenario that does not arise for a business with no visitors at all. Insureon's own "Who Needs General Liability Insurance" page does not exempt a business with none of these exposures; instead it broadens the case to **contract-driven** demand: "landlords, mortgage companies, business partners, and clients often require general liability coverage" even absent a physical location, "if these parties require it in contracts or leases" ([insureon.com](https://www.insureon.com/small-business-insurance/general-liability/who-needs-general-liability-insurance), retrieved 19 September 2026). Read together, no insurer states in its own words that a business with no premises, no visitors, and no physical product has zero general-liability exposure — the honest reading is that the exposure itself (slip-and-fall, property damage) is genuinely close to zero, but a Practice's own vendor contract could still require a certificate of GL coverage regardless of whether the risk it insures ever arises — the same contractual-demand dynamic as in §3 above for cyber.

## 5. Naming the LLC as insured when the BAA is in the founder's personal name, and when to bind

**Naming.** Insurance-industry guidance is unambiguous that once an LLC exists, it — not the individual — should be the named insured: "Only list the LLC as the named insured, not the members," and "the corporation or LLC or LLP is the legal entity that should be the named insured on the policy" (Coterie Insurance, [named-insured guidance](https://coterieinsurance.com/blog/who-should-be-the-named-insured-on-commercial-insurance-policies/), retrieved 19 September 2026). No source found addresses the specific mismatch this business has today — a BAA executed by the founder personally, before the LLC exists — and none states that a policy's named insured must match every contract's counterparty; that is a gap this research does not close. It does, however, sharpen the sequencing already flagged elsewhere in this repo (issue #546): the Google Cloud BAA needs re-execution in the LLC's name regardless of the insurance question, and until that happens, an LLC-named insurance policy would not match the entity actually party to the BAA.

**When to bind.** No insurer in this set publishes a rule tied to "formation" versus "first customer" versus "first revenue" in its own marketing copy — none of Hiscox, Next Insurance, Thimble, Insureon, Vouch, Embroker, or Coalition states a binding trigger on the pages checked. The closest first-party-adjacent statement found is general startup-insurance guidance (not from one of the seven insurers named in the task, flagged as such): buy "general liability at formation... E&O and cyber at launch, and D&O once you form a board or raise outside capital" (Burkland Associates, [startup insurance guide](https://burklandassociates.com/2026/04/28/what-insurance-does-a-startup-need-a-founders-guide-to-corporate-insurance/), retrieved 19 September 2026). Applied to this business: "launch" is the pilot Practice putting real Client data into the product, which is the same event issue #386 itself already names as "the honest trigger." Nothing found here overrides that.

## 6. Exclusions that matter for pregnancy, birth, and postpartum records

The exclusion that recurs across every source checked is the **bodily-injury / property-damage exclusion**, standard in both tech E&O and professional-liability wordings: "claims related to bodily injury, sickness, disease, death, mental anguish and damage to tangible property will usually be denied" (Founder Shield, [common E&O exclusions](https://foundershield.com/insurance-faqs/errors-omissions/what-are-common-exclusions-on-an-eo-insurance-policy/), retrieved 19 September 2026). This exclusion sits opposite a matching one in general liability, which itself excludes "bodily injury... arising out of the rendering of, or failure to render, professional services" — leaving a coverage gap in the middle for any claim that is both a professional error and a bodily injury (GB&A, [the BI/PD gap in E&O and cyber policies](https://www.gbainsurance.com/BIPD-Insurance-Tech-Cyber-717), retrieved 19 September 2026). That source's own worked example is squarely on point for health-adjacent software: it names "hacked medical equipment causing patient harm" as exactly the scenario where a cyber or tech E&O policy's bodily-injury exclusion would otherwise leave a claim uncovered, and recommends buying policies that carve back the exclusion with **contingent BI/PD coverage** for claims "arising out of professional services." For a product holding due dates, birth outcomes, and postpartum care records, the realistic claim shape is not "the software caused a physical injury" (Doula Cloud does not control medical devices or deliver care) but a **professional-services / E&O claim dressed as a data-integrity failure** — a wrong due date, a missed note, a record shown to the wrong person — which is inside the ordinary tech E&O and cyber grant, not excluded by it. The one live exposure to watch for at bind time is whether the policy's professional-services definition is broad enough to include "software used in the delivery of perinatal care," since a narrowly drafted definition is the mechanism by which a claim tied to health-adjacent records could fall outside it; no insurer's public page defines that boundary precisely enough to answer this without an actual quote application.

## What this does not answer

- Actual underwritten quotes for this specific business — every figure above is a marketing-page floor, not a bound price.
- Whether any of the seven insurers will decline or surcharge for health-adjacent (though not HIPAA-covered-entity) data specifically; none of the pages checked price by data category at this level of detail.
- The precise professional-services definition boundary flagged in §6 — that requires reading an actual policy form or asking an underwriter, not a marketing page.
- Multi-state or federal breach-notification law beyond New York — out of scope for this question.
