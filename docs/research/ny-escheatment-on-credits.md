# Does New York escheat unspent Doula Cloud Credits?

Question: **does New York's Abandoned Property Law reach unredeemed prepaid Credits sold by Doula Cloud to its business customers?**

Facts held fixed: Doula Cloud is a **New York single-member LLC** selling practice-management software. Its customers are **Practices — businesses, not consumers**. A Practice buys **Credits** at a fixed price; one Credit is consumed when the Practice starts a client engagement. Credits are **single-purpose** (they buy nothing but Doula Cloud's own service), **not transferable**, **not redeemable for cash**, and are **not a gift card sold to a consumer**. Doula Cloud is about to publish a policy saying **Credits never expire and unspent purchased Credits are refundable at any time**.

**This is not legal advice.** It is a record of what New York's own statute and its own escheat administrator publish, with each claim traced to the provision or the guidance page that owns it. Researched **28 August 2026**.

Sources are first-party throughout: the **New York State Legislature's** publication of the consolidated laws (nysenate.gov) for statutory text, and the **New York State Office of the State Comptroller** (osc.ny.gov), which is the office that administers escheat and receives the reports. There is no secondary source in this document — no law-firm article, no unclaimed-property-vendor blog, no formation service.

Each claim below is labelled by how it was verified:

- **[statute]** — quoted from the enacted text of the Abandoned Property Law or the General Business Law.
- **[OSC]** — quoted from the Comptroller's own published holder guidance.
- **[checked absence]** — a thing that is *not* there, with the search that established it.
- **[inference]** — a conclusion drawn from the quoted material, marked so it is not mistaken for a quote.

## The answer in five lines

1. **Yes, New York reaches it.** The operative provision is **Abandoned Property Law § 1315(1-b)** — "any unclaimed amount for services not rendered" — not the gift-certificate provision. The dormancy period is **three years**, the amount escheated is **the whole unspent balance** (there is no percentage), and the report is due **10 March** to the Office of the State Comptroller.
2. **Business-to-business changes nothing.** The Comptroller says so in one sentence: "The APL does not provide an exemption for business-to-business transactions."
3. **The no-expiry policy is irrelevant to escheat.** New York's clock runs on "unclaimed by the owner", never on expiry. The Comptroller states that the law "applies even in those instances in which the instrument indicates an expiration date" — expiry neither starts nor stops the clock.
4. **The refund policy does not exempt anything either**, and an unclaimed *refund cheque* becomes its own reportable item under § 1315(1-a) on its own three-year clock.
5. **What actually prevents escheat is recorded contact.** A verifiable login counts (2 NYCRR § 125.1), and the Comptroller's own business-to-business rule makes a credit balance not reportable where the holder can show the customer used it, disclaimed it, or was made aware of it. The published policy is compatible with the APL — it just has to be backed by that record. See [What Doula Cloud should do](#what-doula-cloud-should-do).

## 1. Does the Abandoned Property Law reach this at all?

**Yes, and the reaching provision is not the one about gift certificates.** APL Article 13, § 1315 is headed *Miscellaneous unclaimed property*. Subdivision **1-b** is the one that fits these facts, and it is very short: **[statute]**

> Any unclaimed amount for services not rendered or for goods not delivered, which amount was received after July first, nineteen hundred seventy-four, or in the case of a public utility company as that term is defined in subdivision twenty-three of section two of the public service law, on or after July first, nineteen hundred eighty which has remained unclaimed by the owner of such amount for three years, shall be deemed abandoned property.

— [Abandoned Property Law § 1315(1-b)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/ABP/1315).

A purchased Credit is money Doula Cloud has received for a service it has not yet rendered. That is the whole of the test. **[inference]**

Read what the subdivision does *not* say, because each omission is load-bearing:

- **No consumer limitation.** The words "consumer", "personal", "family" and "household" do not appear anywhere in § 1315. **[checked absence]** — all six subdivisions of § 1315 (1, 1-a, 1-b, 2, 3, 4) were retrieved verbatim and searched for each of those terms; none occurs. ("Person" occurs, in subdivision 2's "the person or persons entitled thereto", which carries no consumer sense.) The only place a consumer limitation appears in this area of New York law is General Business Law § 396-i, which is a different statute doing a different job (see [section 3](#3-business-to-business-versus-a-consumer-gift-card--the-crux)).
- **No holder-type limitation.** Subdivisions 1 and 1-a are addressed to "any corporation (other than a public corporation), joint stock company, individual, association of two or more individuals, committee or business trust in this state". **Subdivision 1-b carries no such list at all** — it attaches to the amount, not to the form of the entity holding it. **[statute]** So the question "is a single-member LLC a 'corporation' for § 1315(1)?" — which is a real question, since the Limited Liability Company Law is not the Business Corporation Law — never has to be answered. It does not arise under 1-b. **[inference]** The Comptroller's own reference sheet for this reporting cycle is in any case titled "Unclaimed Property Relating to **All Business Entities**", not "corporations".
- **No instrument requirement.** Nothing turns on a card, a certificate, a serial number, or a transferable token. An "amount" is enough. **[statute]**

The Comptroller describes the same provision in its own words, and describes it as reaching exactly this shape of liability: **[OSC]**

> Section 1315 requires corporations to report outstanding checks issued for goods or services and unclaimed amounts issued for services not rendered or goods not delivered. This covers unclaimed accounts payable and accounts receivable credits, unrefunded overcharges, as well as gift certificates/cards.

— [Unclaimed Property Relating to All Business Entities — Reference Sheet](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/general-corporations.pdf), "Unclaimed Property".

### Is stored value / are gift certificates covered, and under what definition?

They are covered, by a **separate** subdivision with a **different** period, and it is worth being precise about why Credits do not sit there.

APL § 1315(**1**): **[statute]**

> Any unclaimed amount representing unredeemed gift certificates sold after December thirty-first, nineteen hundred eighty-three, including gift certificates for merchandise only in which case the face value of such certificate shall be deemed the amount deemed abandoned, and owing in this state, or held by any corporation (other than a public corporation), joint stock company, individual, association of two or more individuals, committee or business trust in this state, and which has remained unclaimed by the owner of such amount for five years, shall be deemed abandoned property.

**The Abandoned Property Law defines "gift certificate" itself, in APL § 103(g)** — and the definition is *broader* than the consumer gift-card statute's, which is the opposite of what a reader expecting relief would hope. **[statute]** It opens "Gift certificate shall mean a written promise or electronic payment device that: (i) is usable at a single merchant or an affiliated group of merchants that share the same name, mark, or logo, or is usable at multiple, unaffiliated merchants or service providers; and (ii) is issued in a specified amount", and closes with a list of exclusions — deposit-linked payment devices, prepaid telephone calling cards, flexible spending arrangements and accounts, Archer MSAs, dependent care reimbursement accounts, health savings accounts, prepaid discount cards, and payroll cards.

Three things follow, and each is checked: **[checked absence]**

- **The APL definition has no consumer limitation.** It does not contain "personal, family or household" or any restriction to consumer purchases — verified against § 103(g) directly. This is where it diverges from General Business Law § 396-i, whose otherwise near-identical wording *does* carry that limb (see [section 3](#3-business-to-business-versus-a-consumer-gift-card--the-crux)). **The escheat statute deliberately drops the consumer qualifier the consumer statute keeps.**
- **A single-merchant instrument is expressly included**, in the definition's own words: "usable at a **single** merchant". Closed-loop is not an exit.
- **None of the nine exclusions reaches a prepaid balance for the seller's own services.** Checked each in turn against these facts; the nearest, "prepaid discount card or program", is a discount mechanism, not a prepayment for the seller's own service, and it carries its own disclosure proviso.

**[checked absence]** — APL Article 1 is short: § 101 (short title), § 102 (declaration of policy) and § 103 (definitions). § 103 defines eleven terms — abandoned property fund; abandoned property heretofore paid to the state; banking organizations; infant; infancy; life insurance corporation; utility services; **gift certificate**; virtual currency; virtual currency business activity; and energy services company. It defines neither **"holder"** nor **"owner"** nor **"stored value"**, so no definitional argument about who holds the balance or what a unit of stored value is has any statutory hook in New York.

The Comptroller adds one gloss on top, closing the "we sell services, not merchandise" reading: **[OSC]**

> The term gift certificate includes gift certificates designated for merchandise and/or services.

— *All Business Entities Reference Sheet*.

**[inference]** Whether Credits fall inside § 103(g) is genuinely arguable — they are denominated in units of service rather than "issued in a specified amount", and there is no instrument to be a "written promise or electronic payment device". That argument is worth having only to decide between a five-year and a three-year clock. **It is not an escape**, because § 1315(1-b) does not use the term at all.

That gloss says a certificate for **services** is still a gift certificate, so "we sell services, not merchandise" is not an exit. **[inference]** But note what the gloss does *not* supply: it does not say a prepaid balance on a customer's own account is a gift certificate, and the Comptroller's property-type table lists the two as **different codes with different dormancy** — MS12 *Unredeemed Gift Certificates* at 5 years, MS04 *Payments For Goods and Services* at 3 years. **[OSC]** ([Property Type Table](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/property-type-tables.pdf), All Business Entities.)

**This distinction does not change the answer, only the deadline.** Credits are reportable either way: as a gift certificate they escheat at five years under 1315(1); as an amount for services not rendered they escheat at three years under 1315(1-b). **The shorter period is the safe assumption**, and it is the one the facts fit best — Credits are bought by the account holder for its own use, are not transferable, and are not an instrument. **[inference]** Reporting on the three-year clock also satisfies the five-year one.

### Does the definition require a consumer purchaser or a general-purpose instrument?

**No, on both counts, and this is the point where a reader who knows the gift-card rules is most likely to go wrong.**

**"General-purpose" is not a requirement.** § 1315 draws no distinction between a single-merchant (closed-loop) instrument and an open-loop one. **[checked absence]** — searched the assembled verbatim text of all six subdivisions of § 1315 (1, 1-a, 1-b, 2, 3, 4) for "closed", "open", "single", "multiple", "network" and "merchant"; none appears. And where New York's escheat law *does* address the point — in the § 103(g) definition — it resolves it the unhelpful way, by including an instrument "usable at a **single** merchant" in terms.

**A consumer purchaser is not required.** § 1315(1-b) has no purchaser qualification at all; § 103(g) omits the consumer limb that General Business Law § 396-i carries; and the Comptroller has said in terms that business-to-business does not escape. See [section 3](#3-business-to-business-versus-a-consumer-gift-card--the-crux).

### The provisions that were checked and do not apply

- **APL § 1314, *Unclaimed consumer credit balances*.** **[checked absence]** — its title is the only consumer-sounding hook in Article 13, so it was read. It is a plumbing section: it says amounts already transferable to the State under **General Business Law § 715** — retail instalment credit balances — are transferred to the Comptroller in the first fifteen days of February. **[statute]** It does not reach a prepaid balance for a seller's own services, and its own calendar (cut-off 31 December, report due 15 February) is different from § 1315's. It is not the operative section here.
- **APL § 1319, *Unclaimed virtual currency*.** **[checked absence]** — Credits are not virtual currency; they are a service entitlement denominated in units, are not transferable, and are not held in a wallet. The property type table lists VC01 "Virtual Currency (wallet balances)" under § 1315 at 3 years, which is the same clock in any case.
- **APL § 1310, *Voluntary disposition of miscellaneous property not otherwise subject to this chapter*.** **[checked absence]** — the escape hatch for property the APL does *not* reach, under which a holder may ask the Comptroller to take it after two years. It is irrelevant here precisely because § 1315(1-b) *does* reach this property; § 1310 is not needed and is not available as an alternative characterisation. **[inference]**

## 2. Dormancy, amount, form, deadline, office

### Dormancy period: three years

**[statute]** § 1315(1-b): "which has remained unclaimed by the owner of such amount for **three years**".

**[OSC]** The Comptroller's property type table assigns Credits-shaped property a three-year period under three separate codes that could each fit, and every one of them is three years:

| NAUPA II code | Description | APL section | Dormancy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **MS04** | **Payments For Goods and Services** | 1315 | **3 years** |
| MS09 | Credit Balances – Accounts Receivable | 1315 | 3 years |
| MS11 | Refunds Due | 1315 | 3 years |
| MS12 | Unredeemed Gift Certificates | 1315 | 5 years |
| CK13 | Vendor Checks | 1315 | 3 years |

— [Property Type Table](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/property-type-tables.pdf) (12/2025), *All Business Entities*.

**MS04 is the code for an unspent Credit balance.** **[inference]** MS11 becomes relevant only once a refund has been determined and not paid.

**When the clock starts, and what restarts it.** The Comptroller is explicit for credit balances: **[OSC]**

> The three-year dormancy period on credit balances commences at the time the credit was issued. However, if there is written communication from the customer acknowledging the existence of the credit, or activity with respect to the customer account affecting the amount of the credit balance (partial use of the credit), the three-year dormancy begins from the time of the written communication or activity.

— [Handbook for Reporters of Unclaimed Funds](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/handbook.pdf), "Business-to-Business Transactions".

So the clock starts at **purchase**, and **spending any Credit from the balance restarts it**. A Practice that buys Credits and consumes even one of them resets the three years on the remainder. **[inference]**

### Percentage of face value: all of it

**There is no partial escheat in New York.** **[checked absence]** — searched the assembled verbatim text of all six subdivisions of § 1315 for "per cent", "percent", "sixty", "portion" and "fraction": none appears. Searched the Handbook and the All Business Entities reference sheet for a percentage or discount applied to reportable amounts: none. The only two amount fields on the report form are "Initial Amount — Amount due the owner before any allowable deductions are subtracted" and "Remitted Amount — Net amount due the owner after any allowable deductions are subtracted", and the Handbook lists "service charges" among the items that **do not** eliminate the need to report, rather than among deductions a holder may take.

The Comptroller states the amount rule directly for the nearest analogue: **[OSC]**

> Gift certificates are reportable at **face or remaining value** even in those instances in which an expiration date is indicated or it is redeemable for merchandise only.

— *All Business Entities Reference Sheet*, emphasis added.

**Recorded so it is not re-raised: New York escheats 100% of the unspent balance.** It is not one of the states that takes a fraction of face value. **[inference from a checked absence]**

### Form, deadline, office

The Comptroller's calendar gives one row for **"Article V & 1315 — All Business Entities"**, and every date below comes from it: **[OSC]**

| Date | What |
| --- | --- |
| **10 December** | Send a notice by **first-class mail** to every owner expected to appear on the report who has a valid address (APL § 1422) |
| **31 December** | **Cut-off date.** Determine which accounts are dormant as of this date |
| **10 January** | Send a notice by **certified mail, return receipt requested**, to owners owed **over $1,000** who did not answer the first notice |
| **10 March** | **Final report, remittance and the Verification and Checklist (AC 2709) must be received** by the Office of the State Comptroller |

— [Calendar of Events](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/calendar-of-events.pdf) and [Annual notification to Corporations](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/annual-corporation-letter.pdf).

Note the sequencing trap: the **first-class mailing is due before the cut-off date it relates to**. The due-diligence letter goes out on 10 December for balances that will be measured on the following 31 December. **[inference]**

**The forms.** **[OSC]**

> Reporters filing 25 accounts or fewer may use paper format AC 2686. Use these guidelines to submit the report details. When using this format, a Verification and Checklist form AC 2709 is required. Note, **we do not accept preliminary or negative report filings.** Include payment by Electronic Funds Transfer or check (payable to Comptroller, State of New York).

— [Report of Abandoned Property – Form AC 2686 Guidelines](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/2025/pdf/ac2686-guidelines.pdf), emphasis added.

- **AC 2686** — *Report of Abandoned Property*, the paper detail form, available for **25 accounts or fewer**. Above that, file electronically in **NAUPA** format, which the Comptroller has made its preferred format.
- **AC 2709** — *Verification and Checklist for Unclaimed Property*, required with every report.
- **Office:** the **New York State Office of the State Comptroller, Office of Unclaimed Funds** (Reports Processing Unit, `NYSRPU@osc.ny.gov`).

**There is no zero return here.** This is the opposite of the sales-tax posture recorded in `docs/research/ny-llc-tax-obligations.md` (on branch `research/ny-llc-tax-obligations`), where a quarter with no sales still needs a filing. The Comptroller will not accept a negative report. In a year where nothing has gone dormant, Doula Cloud files nothing. **[inference from the quoted guidance]**

**What being late costs.** **[OSC]**

> If you are late paying or delivering abandoned property, late filing interest is due and payable. Interest is **10% per annum (year)** from the date payment or delivery was due to the date you make the payment or delivery.

> If you **willfully** fail to file full and complete reports or required affidavits, you are subject to penalties of **$100 for each day** the report or affidavit shall be willfully delayed or withheld. Refer to §1412 of the APL.

— *Handbook for Reporters*.

**And what remitting buys.** APL § 1404 is the reason a holder should want to report rather than sit on the money: **[OSC]** "Section 1404 of the APL provides that New York State assumes liability for payment of all claims for abandoned property paid to the State". Once remitted, a Practice that later turns up asking for its Credits is the State's problem, and the Practice claims from the State. **[inference]**

**Reach back, if this is ever discovered late.** The voluntary-compliance reach-back for this class of property is **10 years plus the dormancy period**, and the Comptroller notes the shortening amendment in the same breath: **[OSC]**

> Accounts payable and receivable credits including merchandise credits and gift certificates – 10 years plus 5-year dormancy. Due to a statutory amendment, accounts payable and receivable credits, including merchandise credits **issued after 2011 have a dormancy period of 3 years**.

— *Handbook for Reporters*, "Reach Back". Voluntary compliance is available to first-time reporters "free of interest and penalties", but only until the Comptroller opens an audit: "You are ineligible for voluntary compliance once we contact you regarding an audit."

## 3. Business-to-business versus a consumer gift card — the crux

**New York does not treat a business-to-business prepaid balance differently.** The Comptroller says it in one sentence, in a section of the Handbook created for exactly this question: **[OSC]**

> **The APL does not provide an exemption for business-to-business transactions.** Therefore, under §1315 of the APL, credit balances, as well as checks representing the refund of credit balances, **whether payable to a business or an individual**, are deemed abandoned if unclaimed for three years.

— [Handbook for Reporters of Unclaimed Funds](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/handbook.pdf), "Business-to-Business Transactions", emphasis added.

That is as direct an answer to the crux as a state administrator gives. **A number of states do have a B2B exemption; New York's administrator has published that New York is not one of them.** **[OSC]**

### Why the consumer gift-card rules feel like they should help, and do not

The instinct that a B2B, single-purpose, non-transferable Credit "isn't a gift card" is **correct** — and it is correct under the **wrong statute**.

New York's gift-card rules live in **General Business Law § 396-i**, which is consumer-protection law administered nowhere near the Comptroller. Its definition of a gift certificate is broad — **[statute]** a "certificate, electronic card, or other pre-funded record that: (i) is usable at a single merchant or an affiliated group of merchants that share the same name, mark, or logo, or is usable at multiple, unaffiliated merchants or service providers; and (ii) is issued in a specified amount" — note that "usable at a single merchant" is expressly *included*, so closed-loop does not escape § 396-i either. What limits § 396-i is the **purpose of the purchase**: **[statute]** subdivision 1-a reaches "goods or services used or bought for use primarily for **personal, family or household purposes**".

A Practice buying Credits to run its business is not buying for personal, family or household purposes. **So § 396-i's consumer protections most likely do not attach to Credits.** **[inference]**

**And that is worth nothing for escheat — because the consumer limb is in the wrong place to help.** Look at where each piece sits. § 396-i's **definition** of a gift certificate (subdivision 1) has no consumer limb; the consumer limb sits in subdivision **1-a**, which scopes *what the section regulates*, not *what a gift certificate is*. **[statute]** The Abandoned Property Law then writes out the same definitional wording for itself at § 103(g) — and takes only the definition. **It never imports subdivision 1-a.** So the one sentence that makes § 396-i a consumer statute is precisely the sentence the escheat statute leaves behind. **[inference]**

**[checked absence]** — searched § 1315 (all six subdivisions, verbatim) and APL § 103 for any cross-reference to the General Business Law: § 1315 references only the Public Service Law and the Tax Law, § 103(g) states its own self-contained definition, and the one APL section that *does* point at the GBL is § 1314, which points at **§ 715**, not § 396-i. **No provision of the APL incorporates § 396-i or its consumer scope.**

The practical effect is worth stating plainly: **being too commercial for the consumer gift-card statute does not make Credits too commercial for the escheat statute.** The two are not opposite ends of one spectrum. § 396-i governs what a seller may do to a card — expiry dates, dormancy fees, disclosure. § 1315 governs where the money goes when nobody claims it. The Comptroller mentions § 396-i in its reference sheet only to warn holders not to confuse the two clocks: **[OSC]**

> **Gift Card Expiration Dates — General Business Law Section 396-i.** Gift cards and certificates, other than promotional gift certificates, may not expire earlier than nine years after the date of issue or the date on which funds were last loaded. **Be mindful that the dormancy period under the Abandoned Property Law is five years.** Regardless of expiration dates, these items are reportable as unclaimed funds once they meet the five-year dormancy period.

— *All Business Entities Reference Sheet*, emphasis added.

That paragraph is the whole relationship between the two statutes: they run on different clocks and one does not switch the other off. **The consumer statute may not apply to Credits; the escheat statute does.** **[inference]**

### The one place the B2B character does help

Being business-to-business does not create an exemption, but it does put Doula Cloud inside the Comptroller's **B2B credit-balance procedure**, which is a genuine, published route to a balance not being reportable — and it turns on contact, not on category: **[OSC]**

> However, such property is not reportable to this Office if the holder is able to demonstrate that the customer has either: (i) used the credit balance, (ii) disclaimed entitlement to the credit balance or, (iii) been made aware of the credit balance.
>
> Accordingly, prior to the time that a credit balance would be outstanding for three years, the holder must contact the customer in writing advising the customer of the credit. The customer may: (i) request that the credit be applied to an open invoice or request payment of the credit, (ii) disclaim entitlement to the credit in writing, or (iii) acknowledge existence of the credit, but let the credit remain outstanding.

— *Handbook for Reporters*, "Business-to-Business Transactions".

Note the third limb of the first list: **"been made aware of the credit balance"**. That is satisfiable unilaterally by the holder — a written notice of the balance, sent and recorded, before three years elapse. **[inference]** This is the mechanism that makes Doula Cloud's published policy workable, and [What Doula Cloud should do](#what-doula-cloud-should-do) builds on it.

The Handbook also closes the tempting shortcut: **[OSC]** "a holder cannot write off open customer credit balances in the absence of written documentation evidencing that the credit was issued in error or improperly applied, or a specific written disclaimer from the customer." **Expiring or zeroing a stale Credit balance in the database is not a way out.**

## 4. Does "Credits never expire" make escheat more likely, less likely, or is it irrelevant?

**Irrelevant to whether escheat happens — but it does remove the only lever that could have shortened the balance's life.**

New York's trigger is the word **"unclaimed"**, not the word "expired". § 1315(1-b) deems the amount abandoned when it "has remained **unclaimed by the owner** of such amount for three years". **[statute]** Expiry appears nowhere in the section. **[checked absence]** — searched the assembled verbatim text of all six subdivisions of § 1315 for "expire", "expiration", "expired" and "expiry": none occurs in any subdivision. The nearest thing to a time-limit concept anywhere in the section is subdivision 4's state tax-refund debit card "which shall not have been activated for one year from the date of issuance", which is a *non-activation* trigger for a State-issued card and has nothing to do with a private seller's expiry terms. The word "expiration" does appear once in the definition at § 103(g), and only inside the exclusion for prepaid discount cards; it is not a trigger anywhere in § 1315.

The Comptroller states the same thing twice, affirmatively: **[OSC]**

> The law applies even in those instances in which the instrument indicates an expiration date.

> Gift certificates are reportable at face or remaining value **even in those instances in which an expiration date is indicated** [...] Regardless of expiration dates, these items are reportable as unclaimed funds once they meet the [...] dormancy period.

— *All Business Entities Reference Sheet*, emphasis added.

So: **[inference]**

- **Some states escheat precisely because a balance never expires** — the premise in the question is a real pattern elsewhere. New York does not work that way. New York would escheat a Credit that expired last year and a Credit that never expires on the identical three-year clock, measured from purchase or last activity.
- **An expiry date would not have helped.** Had Doula Cloud instead published "Credits expire after 24 months", the balance would still be reportable at three years from the last activity — the Comptroller says so in terms — and the Practice would additionally have lost the money without the State ever holding it for them. Expiry is worse for the customer and identical for escheat.
- **What "never expires" does change is the population.** A balance that never expires is a balance that stays on the books indefinitely, so over time more balances accumulate that can reach three years of no activity. That is an operational consequence, not a legal one: it raises the number of rows that might one day be reportable, not the probability that any given dormant row is reportable. **The published commitment does not collide with the APL.**

## 5. Does "refundable at any time" change the analysis?

**No — and the refund itself creates a second, later escheat exposure that has to be handled.**

**Refundability does not create an exemption.** A refundable prepaid balance is precisely the thing the Comptroller's B2B section calls a "credit balance", and the sentence quoted above covers "credit balances, **as well as checks representing the refund of credit balances**". **[OSC]** Both the balance and the refund of the balance are named as reportable property. Refundability is, if anything, the feature that makes the balance most clearly an "amount ... unclaimed by the owner" — it is money the customer could demand back and has not.

**A refund that goes uncashed becomes its own reportable item.** If Doula Cloud refunds a Practice by cheque and the cheque is never cashed, § 1315(1-a) picks it up: **[statute]**

> Any amount representing outstanding checks issued on and after July first, nineteen hundred seventy-four in payment for goods or for services, and owing in this state, or held by any corporation [...] and which has remained unclaimed by the owner of such amount for three years, shall be deemed abandoned property.

And the Comptroller sets out how the clock moves when a credit balance turns into a cheque: **[OSC]**

> With respect to business-to-business credit balances that are subsequently paid to the customer in the form of a check, the three-year dormancy commences from the original date the credit was issued (or the date the customer last acknowledged or used the credit balance) **unless the holder was instructed in writing by the customer to issue a check for the credit balance**. If a check for the credit balance was issued upon the written request of the customer, the issue date of the check would commence the dormancy period.

— *Handbook for Reporters*.

**[inference]** Read that carefully, because it decides how a refund should be triggered:

- A refund Doula Cloud issues **on its own initiative** — sweeping stale balances back to customers, say — does **not** restart the clock. It inherits the original balance's dormancy date. A self-initiated refund cheque issued at month 34 can be reportable at month 36.
- A refund issued **because the Practice asked for it in writing** restarts the clock from the refund date, and the written request is itself the "used / acknowledged" evidence that took the underlying balance out of scope.

So the refund policy is fine, but **the request must be the customer's and it must be recorded**. A refund flow where the Practice clicks a button in the app and Doula Cloud keeps the record of that request is a written instruction; a back-office sweep is not. And the safest refund rail is one that lands in the Practice's account automatically — a reversal to the original payment method rather than a cheque that can sit uncashed. **[inference]**

**One thing the refund policy does buy.** By making the balance refundable, Doula Cloud makes a Practice's *request* the natural way an account is closed out, which is the cleanest possible entry in the "used the credit balance" limb of the Comptroller's test. The problem class narrows to **Practices that churn silently and never ask** — which is exactly the population section 6 addresses.

## 6. What Doula Cloud should do

**The published policy does not have to change.** "Credits never expire and unspent purchased Credits are refundable at any time" is compatible with the APL, and is a better position than an expiry date would have been. What has to exist is the record that shows contact. **[inference throughout this section, built on the OSC quotations above.]**

1. **Record every login as contact, and keep it.** The Comptroller accepts it in terms: **[OSC]** "Certain types of electronic contact can be used to satisfy the written communication requirements in the APL to prevent the property from being deemed abandoned. This includes email communication from the entitled owner of the property that matches the registered email address on record with the holder or **a verifiable login by the owner using a website or mobile application made available by the holder**" (2 NYCRR § 125.1, quoted in the *Handbook*). A Practice that logs in is not dormant. For a SaaS product this collapses the exposure almost to nothing — dormancy realistically accrues only where a Practice has stopped using the product entirely and still holds unspent Credits.
   - **Consequence for the product:** login timestamps must be retained per Practice for at least the dormancy period plus the retention period. The APL retention rule is **[OSC]** "five years following December 31 of the year for which an Abandoned Property Report was filed" (APL § 1412-a) — so a login record needs to survive well beyond a typical log-rotation window. Do not let authentication logs age out at 90 days.
2. **Send an annual balance statement to every Practice holding unspent Credits.** This is the "been made aware of the credit balance" limb, and it is the one limb Doula Cloud can satisfy without the customer doing anything. Send it in writing, keep the send record, and send it before the balance is three years old.
3. **Make refund the customer's recorded request.** Put a refund control in the app, keep the request record, and refund to the original payment method rather than by cheque. That converts a would-be § 1315(1-b) balance into a transaction, and avoids creating a § 1315(1-a) uncashed cheque.
4. **Never zero a stale balance in the database.** **[OSC]** "a holder cannot write off open customer credit balances in the absence of written documentation evidencing that the credit was issued in error or improperly applied, or a specific written disclaimer from the customer." Writing off is the one action that turns a reportable balance into an audit finding.
5. **Store the two dates escheat reporting will ask for**, per Credit purchase: the **purchase date** and the **date of last activity or contact** (spend, login, written acknowledgement). The report form has a field for the second one, **Date of Last Activity**, described as **[OSC]** "Date of last owner contact or transaction – enter the date on which the last deposit or withdrawal occurred, or the date on which the property became payable, redeemable, dormant, or returnable (e.g., issue date of a check)", and the Comptroller requires holders to **[OSC]** "Provide the Dormancy Begin Date (Starting Transaction Date in NAUPA) for all property that appears on the report." Deriving these after the fact from event logs is much harder than storing them.
6. **Put 10 December and 10 March on the compliance calendar**, alongside the IT-204-LL and ST-100 dates already recorded in `docs/research/ny-llc-tax-obligations.md` (on branch `research/ny-llc-tax-obligations`). No report is due in a year with nothing dormant — the Comptroller does not accept negative filings — but the December due-diligence mailing is what makes a March report unnecessary in the first place.
7. **The earliest date this can possibly bite** is three years after the first Credit is sold. With a January 2027 launch, a Credit sold in January 2027 and never touched reaches three years in January 2030, falls inside the 31 December 2030 cut-off, and is reportable by **10 March 2031**. There is time; the thing that has to be built now is the *record*, because a login timestamp that was never written cannot be reconstructed in 2030.

## 7. What this does not answer

- **Other states.** Every state has its own abandoned-property act, its own dormancy periods, and — unlike New York — many do have a **business-to-business exemption**. Under *Texas v. New Jersey* (1965), quoted in the *Handbook*, property goes to "the state of the owner's last known address", so a Practice in Ohio takes Ohio's law, not New York's. New York catches only the balance where no address is known, by the secondary rule ("the holder's state of incorporation"). **This is a real, unresearched exposure and it grows with every out-of-state Practice.**
- **Whether Credits are a "gift certificate" for GBL § 396-i.** The reasoning above concludes they most likely are not, because a Practice does not buy for personal, family or household purposes — but that conclusion was reached to show § 396-i is the wrong statute for escheat, not to settle Doula Cloud's consumer-law position. If a solo doula ever buys Credits in a way that looks personal, that question is live and is separate from this one.
- **Sales tax on the sale of a Credit** — specifically whether tax attaches when the Credit is bought or when it is spent. New York taxes the underlying product (`docs/research/ny-llc-tax-obligations.md` (on branch `research/ny-llc-tax-obligations`) § 5), and a prepaid-credit model puts the payment and the service in different tax periods. Not researched here.
- **Revenue recognition for the unspent balance.** An accounting question, not an escheat one.
- **What a Credit is worth on escheat when the price of a Credit has changed** since purchase. § 1315 speaks of an "amount" and the gift-certificate rule says "face or remaining value"; a Credit denominated in units rather than dollars needs a documented dollar value per unspent Credit. Worth resolving when Credits are priced, not later.
