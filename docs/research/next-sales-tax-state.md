# After New York, which state does Doula Cloud register in next?

Research question: given New York is the only state Doula Cloud is registered in today, which state is realistically the next one it has to register for sales tax in? The business is a New York single-member LLC selling practice-management software to doula practices as prepaid "Credits" — remotely accessed prewritten software, taxed at purchase where it is taxable at all. Situs is **where the practice's staff work**, a headcount apportionment: a purchase is attributed to the states its doulas work in, split proportionally, not to the client's location. Physical presence is New York only. Pre-launch; target launch and first revenue January 2027.

**This is not tax advice.** It is a record of what each state's own department of revenue, its own statutes, and the Streamlined Sales Tax Governing Board publish, with every claim traced to the page, statute section, or regulation that owns it, and labelled by how it was verified. Researched **28 August 2026**; thresholds, rates and effective dates are those in force on that date. No blog posts, no sales-tax-software vendor marketing — Avalara, TaxJar, Sovos and similar were used only as a map to find the underlying primary source, never cited as the source itself.

This document is downstream of, and matches the house style of, [`docs/research/ny-llc-tax-obligations.md`](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/blob/research/ny-llc-tax-obligations/docs/research/ny-llc-tax-obligations.md), which found that New York taxes this product and requires a Certificate of Authority before the first sale.

## The findings that matter

1. **Two of the ten thresholds are materially above $100,000, exactly as suspected.** Texas is $500,000 and California is $500,000 — both confirmed against the states' own sources, not assumed. Both push a huge population base far down the realistic-candidate list.
2. **Connecticut's threshold is an AND, not an OR.** Connecticut's Department of Revenue Services and Connecticut's own General Statutes both confirm a seller must cross **both** $100,000 in gross receipts **and** 200 separate retail sales — measured over the twelve months ending the preceding September 30 — before nexus attaches. Several vendor summaries (and the Streamlined Sales Tax Governing Board's own remote-seller guidance page) get this wrong and list it as OR. It is AND.
3. **Ohio's OR-with-a-transaction-count test is the headline finding of this document, not a footnote.** Ohio's $100,000-**or**-200-transactions test explicitly covers services, not just goods. Under a low-dollar, high-frequency Credits model, 200 purchase events can be reached at a small fraction of $100,000 in revenue — meaning Ohio nexus could trip years before a revenue-only state like Pennsylvania or Massachusetts, independent of Ohio's market size. See §6.
4. **California is about to flip from exempt to taxable, on the exact month Doula Cloud launches.** SB 122, signed into law 29 June 2026, makes prewritten software and SaaS taxable in California effective **1 January 2027** — the same month Doula Cloud's first revenue is planned. The Stripe Tax sandbox's `product_exempt` read for California is correct **today** and wrong from that date forward. This is flagged loudly in §3; it needs to be re-verified against the sandbox after the flip, not assumed away.
5. **Four of the five `product_exempt` states check out, and three of them never require registration even at scale.** New Jersey, Florida, Illinois and Wyoming all confirm, from their own sources, that this product is not taxable there. New Jersey's own FAQ, and the statutory structure of Florida's and Wyoming's economic-nexus tests, mean a seller of nothing but this product can cross the dollar threshold and still owe **no registration at all** — not even a zero return. Illinois carries a real caveat: if Doula Cloud ever ships a downloadable app or agent, that could flip its Illinois treatment.
6. **The most likely next state is Ohio, on the transaction-count path, with Pennsylvania a close second on the revenue path.** Both tax the product outright, both sit at the $100,000 tier, and both have larger doula-relevant population bases than Massachusetts or Connecticut. Ohio's OR-test gives it a second, much shorter route to nexus that the others lack. See §5 and §6 for the reasoning and the arithmetic, and §6 for a product recommendation that follows directly from the transaction-count exposure.

## 1. Economic nexus thresholds, state by state

Ten states are in scope: the five the Stripe Tax sandbox measured as taxing this product (Connecticut, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Texas, Ohio) and the five it measured as `product_exempt` (Wyoming, New Jersey, California, Florida, Illinois). Every threshold below is confirmed against the state's own department of revenue, its own statutes, or — for the initial pointer to the right primary source — the Streamlined Sales Tax Governing Board's remote-seller guidance page, then verified against the state's own text.

| State | Threshold | Transaction test | AND / OR | Measurement period | Verified against |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Connecticut | $100,000 | 200 or more retail sales | **AND** — both required | 12 months ended the preceding September 30 | Conn. Gen. Stat. § 12-407(a)(12)(G) (cga.ct.gov), corroborated by CT DRS's own 2019 legislative summary |
| Pennsylvania | $100,000 | none | — (dollar-only) | Calendar year | pa.gov (PA Dept. of Revenue), Online Retailers page |
| Massachusetts | $100,000 | none | — (dollar-only) | Prior or current calendar year | 830 CMR 64H.1.9(3)(a) (via Cornell LII's mirror of the Massachusetts regulation) |
| Texas | **$500,000** | none | — (dollar-only) | Preceding 12 calendar months | Texas Comptroller, Remote Sellers page |
| Ohio | $100,000 | 200 or more separate transactions | **OR** — either trips it | Current or preceding calendar year | Ohio Rev. Code § 5741.01(I)(2)(g)–(h) (codes.ohio.gov), effective 3 April 2025 |

Two corrections to the assumptions in the brief, both confirmed:

- **Texas is $500,000, confirmed.** The Texas Comptroller's own remote-sellers page states the threshold directly: a remote seller has "total Texas revenue of less than $500,000 in the preceding twelve calendar months" to stay under the safe harbor — cross it and registration is required starting no later than the first day of the fourth month after the month the threshold was exceeded.
- **California is $500,000, confirmed.** CDTFA's own Wayfair guidance: nexus attaches once "the total combined sales of tangible personal property for delivery in California by the retailer and all persons related to the retailer exceed $500,000" in the preceding or current calendar year (established by AB 147, 2019).

One correction to a common secondary-source error: **Connecticut is AND, not OR.** The Streamlined Sales Tax Governing Board's own remote-seller state-guidance page lists Connecticut as "$100,000 OR 200+ transactions" — this is wrong for the current law. Connecticut's Department of Revenue Services' own 2019 legislative summary states plainly: "The economic nexus threshold for requiring remote sellers to collect and remit sales tax will be reduced on July 1, 2019, to $100,000 of sales **and** 200 or more transactions" (both conditions). The statute itself confirms it and adds the precise measurement window: a remote retailer is one "making retail sales of tangible personal property or services from outside this state to a destination within this state, provided such person has gross receipts of at least one hundred thousand dollars **and** made two hundred or more retail sales... during the twelve-month period ended on the September thirtieth immediately preceding the monthly or quarterly period with respect to which such person's liability for tax under this chapter is determined" (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 12-407(a)(12)(G)). Practically: Connecticut is the hardest of the ten to trip, because it requires both a revenue floor and a transaction-count floor to be crossed in the same trailing window, not either one alone.

## 2. Confirming taxability in the five states the sandbox says tax this product

The Stripe Tax sandbox measured Connecticut, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Texas and Ohio as taxing this product. All five check out against the states' own sources — including the two special regimes the brief flagged.

**Connecticut — taxable at a reduced 1%, not the general 6.35% rate, because this is a B2B sale.** Connecticut splits canned/prewritten software into two buckets. Effective 1 October 2019, electronically accessed or transferred canned software sold **for personal/non-business use** is taxed as tangible personal property at the general 6.35% rate. But software "sold to a business for use by the business remains taxable at the 1% rate for computer and data processing services" (CT DRS, Special Notice SN 2019(8), "Sales of Canned or Prewritten Software for Business Use," read directly from the PDF at portal.ct.gov). Statutory authority: Conn. Gen. Stat. § 12-407(a)(37)(A), as amended by 2019 Conn. Pub. Acts 117 § 322. Doula Cloud sells only to practices — businesses — so the 1% rate applies, not 6.35%. The buyer has to identify itself as a business to claim it; the retailer keeps records substantiating that.

**Pennsylvania — taxable at 6%.** Act 84 of 2016 amended the Tax Reform Code (72 P.S. § 7201(m)(2)) to bring canned software into the definition of taxable tangible personal property "whether electronically or digitally delivered, streamed or accessed" — the state's own page states plainly that "canned computer software, by definition and long-standing case law, is tangible personal property," and that the delivery mechanism, including SaaS, does not change that (pa.gov, Canned Computer Software, Digital Goods page). The state rate is 6% (pa.gov, REV-221 rate schedule); Philadelphia adds 2% and Allegheny County adds 1% on top, neither of which applies to a New York-run seller with practices elsewhere.

**Massachusetts — taxable at 6.25%.** The statute itself, not just a regulation, reaches this: M.G.L. c. 64H § 1 (malegislature.gov) defines "tangible personal property" and adds that "a transfer of standardized computer software, including but not limited to electronic, telephonic or similar transfer, shall also be considered a transfer of tangible personal property" — remote access included. The rate, from the same chapter's § 2, is "6.25 per cent of the gross receipts." The regulation sharpens the remote-access point specifically: 830 CMR 64H.1.3(3)(a) states "prewritten computer software, regardless of the method of delivery" is taxable, and its own example language covers "transfers of rights to use software installed on a remote server" — i.e., SaaS with nothing downloaded, taxed the same as a download (Massachusetts DOR regulation, read via Cornell LII's mirror of 830 CMR 64H.1.3).

**Texas — taxable as a data processing service, with 20% of the charge exempt, confirmed.** The brief's suspicion is correct. Texas's own guidance states "data processing services providers include sellers of software as a service and application service providers" — SaaS is explicitly inside the taxable category — and "twenty percent of the charge for data processing services is exempt from tax" (Texas Comptroller, Publication 96-259, *Taxable Services*). So 80% of a Doula Cloud Credit purchase is taxable in Texas. The state rate is 6.25% (comptroller.texas.gov), applied to the 80% taxable portion, i.e. an effective rate of 5% on the full charge before any local add-on (local jurisdictions can add up to 2% more, for a combined maximum of 8.25%, also applied to the 80% base).

**Ohio — taxable, because this is sold for business use, confirmed.** Ohio's statute draws the same personal-vs-business line Connecticut does, but with a binary result instead of a rate difference: "automatic data processing, computer services, or electronic information services" are a taxable "sale" specifically "when...provided for use in business" and the true object of the transaction is receipt of those services (Ohio Rev. Code § 5739.01(B)(3)(e), codes.ohio.gov). Personal/non-business use of the same services is excluded. Doula Cloud sells only to practices, so it is squarely on the taxable side. The state rate is 5.75% (Ohio Rev. Code § 5739.02).

## 3. Do the `product_exempt` states check out?

New Jersey, California, Florida and Illinois were checked against their own sources, and Wyoming — the fifth `product_exempt` state — was checked too since a mis-set Stripe product tax code is exactly the kind of error worth catching. Four of the five hold up cleanly today. One does not hold up going forward, and it matters a great deal.

**New Jersey — genuinely not taxable, confirmed.** NJ Technical Bulletin TB-72 (nj.gov, read directly from the PDF) states SaaS is not taxable because there is "no transfer of title to tangible personal property" — the customer never downloads or takes possession, so it falls outside the sales-tax base entirely. The bulletin does carve out "information services" (its own examples: Westlaw, LexisNexis, CCH, RIA) as potentially taxable, but that exception is for products whose value *is* the delivered information or database content. Doula Cloud is practice-management functionality, not an information/database service, so the general SaaS rule applies, not the carve-out.

**California — not taxable today, taxable from 1 January 2027. This is the loud flag.** Under the CDTFA's long-standing Regulation 1502, electronically delivered software with no tangible medium is not taxable, and that is what the sandbox measured. But **SB 122, chaptered 29 June 2026 as Chapter 23, rewrites this.** Read directly from the bill text on leginfo.legislature.ca.gov: it defines "digital product" to include "prewritten computer software transferred on tangible storage media, transferred electronically, or accessed remotely" (new § 6016.1), taxes "any permanent or temporary transfer of the right...to open, view, access, download, copy, update, possess, store, manipulate, or otherwise use a digital product transferred electronically or accessed remotely" (new § 6006(i)), explicitly confirms cloud-based and SaaS offerings are covered, and sets the effective date at **1 January 2027 — the same month Doula Cloud plans its first revenue.** A transaction is taxable if it involves both a right to use and consideration given on or after that date, so there is no grandfathering angle available to a company that launches that month. Custom software stays exempt (new § 6010.9), which does not help a multi-tenant product. **The Stripe Tax sandbox's `product_exempt` read for California is not wrong — it is correct as of today and will become wrong on the day Doula Cloud goes live.** A tax code that is right today and silently stops being right is functionally indistinguishable from one that was wrong all along if nobody re-checks it. **Recommendation: re-run the Stripe Tax sandbox measurement for California after 1 January 2027 (or confirm directly with Stripe that its product tax code mapping has been updated for SB 122) before treating any California sale as exempt.** Do not carry this forward as a settled fact past that date.

**Florida — genuinely not taxable, confirmed from the statute itself.** Florida's sales tax statute (Fla. Stat. § 212.05, leg.state.fl.us) taxes tangible personal property and a specific enumerated list of services — telecommunications, protection services, nonresidential cleaning, pest control, and similar named categories. Computer software, SaaS, and "information services" delivered electronically are not on that list, and nothing elsewhere in Chapter 212 puts them there. Florida's Technical Assistance Advisements on cloud computing (e.g., TAA 16A-014) reach the same conclusion from the same premise: no transfer of tangible personal property, no tax. This is a clean, structural exemption, not a close call.

**Illinois — not taxable if genuinely browser-only, confirmed, with a real caveat.** Illinois taxes canned/prewritten software "regardless of the method of delivery, including electronic means" (86 Ill. Adm. Code 130.1935(a)) — but a Department of Revenue letter ruling, read directly from the PDF (Illinois Priv. Ltr. Rul. No. ST 24-0002-PLR, cited and quoted in General Information Letter ST 25-0045-GIL, 19 August 2025, tax.illinois.gov), draws the line at whether anything is downloaded: "computer software provided through a cloud-based delivery system...in which computer software is never downloaded onto a client's computer and is accessed only remotely, such as Software-as-a-Service ('SaaS'), is not subject to tax." **The caveat:** the same letter warns that if a SaaS provider gives the customer an API, applet, desktop agent, or "remote access agent" to install, that transfer is treated as a transfer of tangible personal property and becomes taxable — unless it separately qualifies as a nontaxable license under the five-part test in 86 Ill. Adm. Code 130.1935(a)(1). Doula Cloud today is a pure web application with nothing installed client-side, so it is on the non-taxable side. **Flag for the roadmap: if Doula Cloud ever ships a downloadable desktop or mobile app or agent, that single product decision could flip Illinois from exempt to taxable, independent of anything else in this document.**

**Wyoming — not taxable, confirmed, and worth recording how close this came to a false alarm.** An automated fetch of Wyoming's own "Computer Sales and Services" bulletin (Wyoming Dept. of Revenue, Excise Tax Division, issued July 2014, revised February 2024 — found on the Excise Tax Division's own Educational Materials page) initially returned a summary claiming SaaS *is* taxable in Wyoming. That summary was wrong — reading the source PDF directly (not trusting the automated summary) shows the opposite: "Providing a platform where customers can access hosted software via an internet connection such as the most common cloud computing service models of Software-as-a-Service (SaaS)...are not subject to Wyoming sales tax provided the customer does not receive any tangible personal property or enumerated service embedded within the service," and separately, "Fees charged to access web hosted sites are not subject to sales tax" because "the consumer never takes possession or control of tangible personal property." The statutory basis is W.S. § 39-15-101(a)(vii): a "sale" in Wyoming requires "any transfer of possession," and browser-based access to a hosted product transfers nothing. This is recorded here as a caution about automated document summarization generally, not as a finding about Wyoming — the state's actual position, read first-hand, matches the sandbox.

## 4. Does an exempt state still require registration once the threshold is crossed?

This changes what "next state to register in" even means: a state can tax nothing of Doula Cloud's and still be a state to plan a filing calendar around, if crossing its dollar threshold obligates registration regardless of taxability. Checked per state, for the four permanently exempt states plus California pre-2027.

**New Jersey — no, confirmed directly, not inferred.** NJ's own remote-seller FAQ states the rule in as many words: **"Remote sellers that only make nontaxable retail sales are not required to register with New Jersey."** This is an explicit quote from NJ's own FAQ page (nj.gov/treasury/taxation/remotesellersfaq.shtml), not a conclusion drawn from a statute's structure. A seller of 100% nontaxable SaaS never has to register in New Jersey, at any volume.

**Florida — no, but this one is inferred from the statute's own definition, not stated as an FAQ.** Florida's threshold, in Fla. Stat. § 212.0596, is defined as "any number of taxable remote sales in the previous calendar year in which the sum of the sales prices...exceeded $100,000" — the statute counts only **taxable** remote sales toward the $100,000. A seller whose Florida sales are 100% nontaxable SaaS has taxable remote sales of $0, which can never exceed $100,000, so the threshold is structurally never crossed and registration is never triggered. This is Florida's own statutory text doing the work, but it is this document's inference from that text that "$0 in taxable sales never crosses $100,000," not a sentence Florida's Department of Revenue has published saying so directly. Treat it as strong, not certain.

**Illinois — no, inferred the same way, and slightly less certain because it turns on a distinction (ROT vs. SOT) this research did not fully chase down.** Illinois's own bulletin defines its post-2026 threshold as "$100,000 or more in cumulative gross receipts of sales of TPP" (tax.illinois.gov, Bulletin FY 2026-12) — tangible personal property, specifically. Illinois's own letter ruling (§3 above) establishes that pure browser-based SaaS is not TPP in Illinois. Putting those two together, SaaS revenue should not count toward the Illinois ROT threshold at all, meaning no registration regardless of volume — but this document did not independently verify whether a separate Service Occupation Tax nexus test exists with different counting rules. Treat this one as the weakest of the four "no registration" findings.

**Wyoming — no, inferred from the statute's own scope-limiting language.** W.S. § 39-15-501(a) opens by defining who it even applies to: "any seller of tangible personal property, admissions or services **which are subject to taxation** under chapter 15 or 16 of this title." A seller of nothing but nontaxable SaaS is not selling anything "subject to taxation" in Wyoming, so the section's $100,000 threshold — which is itself phrased as "gross revenue from the sale of tangible personal property, admissions or services" — is read here as never engaged in the first place. As with Illinois, this is this document's reading of the statute's structure, not a sentence from the Wyoming Department of Revenue saying "you don't have to register." It is a reasonably confident reading given how the section is scoped, not a certainty.

**California, before 1 January 2027 — no, by the same TPP-only logic as Illinois; after that date, registration mechanics apply once the (unverified) threshold math says so.** Pre-SB 122, SaaS is not tangible personal property in California, and CDTFA's own threshold language is "total combined sales of **tangible personal property**" — so SaaS revenue should not count toward the $500,000 test today, and no registration is owed on that basis. **What is not verified:** whether SB 122 also amends how the $500,000 threshold is calculated, so that digital-product revenue counts toward it from 2027 onward. The bill text confirms digital products become taxable; it was not confirmed here whether the nexus-threshold statute itself was amended to fold that revenue into the calculation, or whether that is left to forthcoming CDTFA guidance (CDTFA has stated it is still workshopping SB 122 implementation as of this writing). Treat "California's $500,000 threshold will count SaaS revenue from 2027" as this document's inference, not a confirmed fact — worth a follow-up check closer to the effective date.

## 5. Ranking the realistic candidates

Only states that actually tax the product can be a "next state to register in" for sales tax purposes — a huge, exempt population base (Florida, New Jersey, Illinois) is not a candidate at all, by §4. That narrows the field to five taxing states today (Connecticut, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Texas, Ohio) plus California from 1 January 2027.

**The doula-headcount proxy used here is state population, and it is a weak one — said plainly.** The brief allows population, CDC/NCHS births, or a certifying body's directory count. Births would be the more direct proxy (doula demand tracks births more tightly than raw population), but every attempt to reach CDC's own vital-statistics tables and press-room state map (cdc.gov/nchs) returned an HTTP 403 during this research — the source could not be reached in a form that could be verified first-hand, so it was not used rather than reproduced from a secondary source's numbers. Population is a genuine proxy for "how many doulas could plausibly work here," not a good one — it does not account for birth rate, urbanization, insurance/Medicaid doula-reimbursement policy, or existing doula-density hot spots, all of which move independently of headcount. Figures below are the Census Bureau's own Vintage 2025 population estimates (as of 1 July 2025), from the underlying dataset at census.gov, read directly rather than taken from a secondary summary:

| State | Population (Census, Jul 2025) | Taxes the product? | Threshold |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Texas | 31,709,821 | Yes | $500,000 |
| California | 39,355,309 | From 1 Jan 2027 | $500,000 |
| Pennsylvania | 13,059,432 | Yes | $100,000 |
| Ohio | 11,900,510 | Yes | $100,000 or 200 txns |
| Massachusetts | 7,154,084 | Yes | $100,000 |
| Connecticut | 3,688,496 | Yes | $100,000 and 200 txns |

**Ranked, combining taxability, threshold ease, and population:**

1. **Ohio.** Taxes the product (§2), sits at the $100,000 tier, and — uniquely among the five — has a second, independent route to nexus via 200 transactions, which §6 shows can be reached at a small fraction of $100,000 in revenue under a low-dollar Credits model. Population 11.9M, second-largest of the states that tax the product this year.
2. **Pennsylvania.** Taxes the product, $100,000 with no transaction shortcut, largest population (13.1M) of the states that tax the product this year. The most "boring" and predictable candidate — nothing about it depends on assumptions about how Doula Cloud bills.
3. **Massachusetts.** Same threshold shape as Pennsylvania ($100,000, dollar-only), smaller population (7.2M).
4. **Texas.** By far the largest doula-relevant population among the taxing states (31.7M) — and by far the highest bar (5x the others). The high threshold is what keeps it out of the top spot despite the size.
5. **Connecticut.** Smallest population of the states that tax the product (3.7M) and the hardest test to trip (AND, not OR) — last on both axes.
6. **California.** Largest population of any state in this document by a wide margin (39.4M), but not taxable until 1 January 2027, and then at the same $500,000 bar as Texas. Not a near-term candidate; a state to watch from 2027 onward once its threshold mechanics are confirmed (§4).

**Most likely next state: Ohio, moderate-to-good confidence.** The confidence is "moderate-to-good" rather than "high" because the ranking's top spot genuinely depends on a fact this research could not verify — how Doula Cloud will actually bill Credits (see §6) — and because "which state a practice happens to be in" is a function of future sales, not something knowable in advance. Pennsylvania is a close, more conservative second: if Doula Cloud bundles Credits into infrequent, larger purchases (suppressing the transaction count, see the recommendation in §6), Ohio's advantage disappears and Pennsylvania becomes the more likely first trigger by virtue of its larger population alone.

## 6. How far away is this — a 2028 question or a 2031 question?

At $5 per Credit and one Credit per client engagement, the arithmetic is straightforward once a threshold is fixed. There are **two different paths to nexus, and they have very different timelines.**

**The revenue path — Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, Connecticut, and (once taxable) Texas and California.** $100,000 in revenue in one state within the measurement period ÷ $5/Credit = **20,000 Credits (client engagements) delivered to practices in that one state within twelve months**, before nexus attaches. Texas and California, at $500,000, need **100,000 Credits** in a single state within twelve months. Connecticut needs the same 20,000 Credits *and* 200 separate purchase transactions in the same trailing window — but 200 transactions is a low bar once there is any steady base of paying customers making periodic purchases (even a few dozen practices topping up monthly clears 200 transactions well inside a year), so revenue, not transaction count, is realistically the binding constraint for Connecticut too. Twenty thousand client engagements in a single state within a year is a large multiple of the pilot's current scale — the pilot is sized around one ~14-doula agency plus several smaller practices (per the company's own planning), not tens of thousands of engagements in one state alone. Reaching that scale in any single revenue-path state is not a near-term event.

**The transaction-count path — Ohio only, among the states that tax the product.** Ohio's OR-test can be tripped by 200 separate purchase transactions **regardless of revenue**, if Credits are billed as many small, frequent purchases rather than infrequent bulk top-ups. Illustrating the sensitivity rather than asserting a real billing pattern (this is a hypothetical to show the exposure, not a stated fact about how Doula Cloud bills): if a purchase transaction were, say, a single Credit at a time, 200 transactions is only **$1,000 of Ohio revenue** — two orders of magnitude below the $100,000 a revenue-only state requires. Even at a more realistic granularity — a handful of Ohio practices each making a modest number of top-up purchases a year — 200 cumulative transactions across all Ohio customers combined is a plausible outcome well before $100,000 in cumulative Ohio revenue, because transaction count scales with the number of active customers and how often each one buys, not with total dollars.

**This is the recommendation that follows directly from that exposure: batch Credit purchases into fewer, larger transactions (e.g. billing top-ups monthly or per-engagement-bundle rather than per Credit) rather than many small ones, specifically to avoid tripping Ohio's transaction-count test years ahead of the revenue that would otherwise justify registering there.** This is a product/billing decision, not a tax filing, but it is the one lever available before the fact — after 200 transactions have already occurred, there is nothing left to decide.

**Net answer:** on the revenue path, this reads as a **2029–2031 question at the earliest** — twenty thousand engagements in one state is a scale-up of one to two orders of magnitude past the pilot, and nothing in the company's current plans reaches that inside two or three years of launch. On Ohio's transaction-count path specifically, the timeline **could compress to 2027 or 2028** if Ohio practices are billed in small, frequent increments and the recommendation above is not adopted — that gap between "years away" and "next year" is real, is entirely within the company's control through a billing decision, and is the single most actionable finding in this document.

## What this does not answer

- **Whether SB 122 amends California's $500,000 threshold calculation to include digital-product revenue.** Flagged in §4 as this document's inference, not a confirmed fact. Worth a direct check of CDTFA's SB 122 implementation guidance closer to 1 January 2027.
- **Whether Illinois has a separate Service Occupation Tax nexus test with different counting rules** that could pull SaaS revenue into an Illinois threshold calculation even though it is not "TPP" for Retailers' Occupation Tax purposes. Flagged in §4.
- **A real doula-headcount-by-state figure.** Population was used because CDC's birth tables could not be reached (§5); a certifying body's directory listing count (e.g. DONA International) was not attempted in this pass and would likely be a sharper proxy — worth a follow-up if the ranking in §5 needs tightening before a registration decision is actually made.
- **How Doula Cloud will actually bill Credits** (per-unit, bundled, subscription top-up). This is the single fact that would resolve §5's Ohio-vs-Pennsylvania ranking and §6's timeline range. It is a product decision, not a research gap.
- **What switches on if Doula Cloud ever ships a downloadable app.** Flagged in §3 as a specific trigger that could flip Illinois from exempt to taxable, independent of everything else in this document.
