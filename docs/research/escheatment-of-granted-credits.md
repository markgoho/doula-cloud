# Does New York escheat a Credit nobody paid for?

Question: **does New York's Abandoned Property Law reach a Credit Doula Cloud gave away — `founding_grant` or `signup_bonus` — for which the holder never received any payment?**

Facts held fixed: this ticket does not re-litigate purchased Credits — [`docs/research/ny-escheatment-on-credits.md`](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/blob/research/ny-escheatment-on-credits/docs/research/ny-escheatment-on-credits.md) (from [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)) already established that a **purchased** unspent Credit escheats to New York under APL § 1315(1-b), three years' dormancy, whole balance, no business-to-business exemption, and that recorded contact — not expiry — stops the clock. [#439](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/439) then created two kinds of Credit the holder was never paid for: **`founding_grant`** (three Credits per Staff member, given free to a pilot Practice — roughly 150 Credits across the pilot, ~$3,000 at the $20.00 list price, of which the fourteen-doula agency alone holds $840) and the pre-existing **`signup_bonus`** (three Credits given free at signup). Both are published as non-refundable: `docs/copy/support-page.md` states *"Credits given free of charge are not refundable."* [#439](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/439) decided `founding_grant` Credits carry no date expiry.

**This is not legal advice.** It is a record of what New York's own statute and its own escheat administrator publish, with each claim traced to the provision or the guidance page that owns it, plus one contrast against a model act New York has not adopted. Researched **29 August 2026**.

Sources are first-party throughout for the New York question: the **New York State Legislature's** publication of the consolidated laws (nysenate.gov) for statutory text, and the **New York State Office of the State Comptroller** (osc.ny.gov), the office that administers escheat and receives the reports. The **Revised Uniform Unclaimed Property Act (RUUPA)** is quoted only as contrast, from the Uniform Law Commission's own drafted text — New York has not enacted it, and its answer is never substituted for a missing New York one.

Each claim below is labelled by how it was verified, matching the convention `docs/research/ny-escheatment-on-credits.md` established:

- **[statute]** — quoted from the enacted text of the Abandoned Property Law.
- **[OSC]** — quoted from the Comptroller's own published holder guidance.
- **[checked absence]** — a thing that is *not* there, with the search that established it.
- **[RUUPA]** — quoted from the Uniform Law Commission's drafted text, contrast only, not New York law.
- **[inference]** — a conclusion drawn from the quoted material, marked so it is not mistaken for a quote.

## The answer in five lines

1. **Not escheatable, on the statute's own words and its own codified regulation — high confidence.** Both operative subdivisions of § 1315 are written around a payment the holder received: § 1315(1) reaches gift certificates *"sold"*; § 1315(1-b) reaches an unclaimed amount *"which amount was received."* **2 NYCRR § 115.1**, the Comptroller's own regulation titled *"Property to be reported,"* restates both verbs exactly rather than broadening them, and treats "credit balance" only as an example of a *received* amount, never as a free-standing category. A `founding_grant` or `signup_bonus` Credit was never sold and no amount was ever received for it. Neither the statute's nor the regulation's trigger word is satisfied.
2. **Beyond that regulation, the Comptroller has never published guidance squarely on this fact pattern.** Its Handbook and Reference Sheet were searched for "consideration," "free of charge," "no cost," "complimentary," "gratuitous," "promotional," and "loyalty" — none of the first five appears anywhere in either document, and "promotional" appears exactly once, in a passage about *expiration dates* under a different statute (General Business Law § 396-i), not about reportability under § 1315. This is a narrower gap than it looked before § 115.1 was found, but it is still a gap in the administrative narrative — the regulation confirms the trigger words, it does not add a sentence about free grants specifically.
3. **The reasoning does not split `founding_grant` from `signup_bonus`.** Neither was sold; neither was an amount received; the statute's text does not ask who the recipient is or what relationship produced the grant. The only difference between the two is practical, not legal — see [section 3](#3-does-founding_grant-differ-from-signup_bonus).
4. **If a granted Credit were nonetheless found escheatable, it would escheat at the $20.00 list price, not zero.** Both § 1315(1)'s own text and the Comptroller's guidance value a reportable gift certificate at *"face value,"* never at the holder's cost to create it — see [section 4](#4-if-a-granted-credit-is-nonetheless-escheatable-what-value-escheats).
5. **This does not reverse [#439](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/439)'s no-expiry decision, on either branch of the answer.** If granted Credits are not escheatable, there is nothing for an expiry date to solve. If they were escheatable, [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390) already established that New York's clock runs on *unclaimed*, not *expired* — an expiry date would not stop the clock, only take the Credit from the Practice earlier. Expiring granted Credits buys no escheat protection under either finding.

## 1. The operative text, read for the word that matters this time: consideration

[#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research read § 1315(1-b) closely for a consumer limitation, a holder-type limitation, and an instrument requirement, and found none of the three. It did not need to read the subdivision for a *consideration* requirement, because the fact pattern in front of it — a purchased Credit — obviously satisfied one if there were one. This ticket's fact pattern does not, so the same sentence has to be reread for a word that did not matter before.

**§ 1315(1-b), retrieved again 29 August 2026, is unchanged from the quotation in [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research:** **[statute]**

> Any unclaimed amount for services not rendered or for goods not delivered, **which amount was received** after July first, nineteen hundred seventy-four, or in the case of a public utility company as that term is defined in subdivision twenty-three of section two of the public service law, on or after July first, nineteen hundred eighty which has remained unclaimed by the owner of such amount for three years, shall be deemed abandoned property.

— [Abandoned Property Law § 1315(1-b)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/ABP/1315), emphasis added.

Parse the grammar. The thing the subdivision deems abandoned is *"any unclaimed amount ... which amount was received."* The relative clause is restrictive: it is not any unclaimed amount for services not rendered, it is an unclaimed amount **that was received** by the holder. A `founding_grant` or `signup_bonus` Credit is created unilaterally by Doula Cloud — nothing changes hands, nothing is received, in the transaction that produces the row. **There is no "amount" for the sentence to be talking about.** **[inference]**

**§ 1315(1), the gift-certificate subdivision, uses a different but equally consideration-shaped verb:** **[statute]**

> Any unclaimed amount representing unredeemed gift certificates **sold** after December thirty-first, nineteen hundred eighty-three, including gift certificates for merchandise only in which case the face value of such certificate shall be deemed the amount deemed abandoned, and owing in this state, or held by any corporation ... and which has remained unclaimed by the owner of such amount for five years, shall be deemed abandoned property.

— [Abandoned Property Law § 1315(1)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/ABP/1315), emphasis added.

A gift certificate that was given away, not sold, is not a *"gift certificate sold."* **[inference]** § 1315(1) has its own definition of "gift certificate" at § 103(g), and that definition — quoted at length in [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research — describes the *shape* of the instrument ("a written promise or electronic payment device that ... is issued in a specified amount") without saying how it came to exist. But § 103(g) only defines the noun; it is subdivision (1) that supplies the verb that makes something reportable, and that verb is "sold," not "issued." **[inference]**

Both routes into § 1315 that could plausibly reach a Credit — (1-b)'s "amount ... received" and (1)'s "gift certificates sold" — are written around the holder having been paid. **Neither is written around the holder having issued something.** That distinction did not matter to [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390) because a purchased Credit satisfies both readings; it matters here because a granted Credit satisfies neither.

**[checked absence]** — the brief pointed at APL §§ 1300 and 1301 as possible definitions sections; both were retrieved verbatim 29 August 2026 and are neither. § 1300 (*Unclaimed surplus from sale of pledged property*) and § 1301 govern surplus proceeds after a pledgee sells collateral posted against a loan — a pawnbroker-shaped provision with its own one-year clock, nothing to do with a service credit and no bearing on consideration. Neither mentions gift certificates, credit balances, or free/promotional property. They contribute nothing to this question and are not cited further.

**One place the statute's own vocabulary points the other way, and it is worth naming rather than hiding.** Nothing in § 1315(1-b) or § 103(g) *excludes* a no-consideration balance either — there is no carve-out, the way RUUPA has one (see [section 6](#6-ruupa--102--contrast-only-not-new-york-law)). The argument here is that the affirmative trigger is never met, not that an exclusion is met. That is a real distinction: an affirmative-trigger-not-met reading is generally the stronger one for a holder (the Comptroller has nothing to point to that includes the property), but it has not been tested against New York's administrator, which is the subject of the next section. **[inference]**

## 2. What the Comptroller's own guidance says — and does not say — about no-consideration balances

**Two documents were pulled directly from osc.ny.gov and searched in full, verbatim, not summarized:** the [Handbook for Reporters of Unclaimed Funds](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/handbook.pdf) (642 lines of extracted text) and the [Unclaimed Property Relating to All Business Entities — Reference Sheet](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/general-corporations.pdf) (492 lines), both retrieved and text-extracted 29 August 2026.

**[checked absence]** — full-text search of both documents for the following terms returned zero matches in either: **"consideration"** (the two hits in the Handbook are the unrelated heading "Important Considerations" and a cross-reference to "considerations" as in "factors"), **"free of charge,"** **"no cost,"** **"at no charge,"** **"complimentary,"** **"gratuitous,"** **"loyalty,"** and **"rewards."** The Comptroller has not published a sentence addressing whether a balance issued for free is reportable. This is the gap the brief anticipated — "this is likely where the operational answer actually lives" — and it turns out not to hold the answer either.

**"Promotional" appears exactly once, across both documents, and not in a reportability discussion:** **[OSC]**

> **Gift Card Expiration Dates — General Business Law Section 396-i.** Gift cards and certificates, other than promotional gift certificates, may not expire earlier than nine years after the date of issue or the date on which funds were last loaded. Be mindful that the dormancy period under the Abandoned Property Law is five years. Regardless of expiration dates, these items are reportable as unclaimed funds once they meet the five-year dormancy period.

— [All Business Entities Reference Sheet](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/general-corporations.pdf), "Gift Card Expiration Dates."

This is the closest either document comes to the fact pattern, and it is genuinely ambiguous rather than dispositive. **[inference]** The passage exists to explain a different statute's expiration-date rule (GBL § 396-i lets a *promotional* gift certificate expire sooner than nine years, unlike a sold one), then adds that expiration dates do not matter for APL reportability. Whether *"these items"* in the closing sentence includes the just-excluded "promotional gift certificates," or refers back only to the "gift cards and certificates" that are subject to the nine-year rule, is not resolved by the sentence's own grammar — the antecedent noun phrase is *"gift cards and certificates, other than promotional gift certificates,"* and "these items" most plausibly points at that whole phrase, promotional exclusion included, but the paragraph was never written to settle this. **It is evidence, not a holding**, and it sits under a heading about a different statute entirely, not under the Comptroller's own "Section 1315" or "Business-to-Business Transactions" headings quoted in [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research.

**One source narrows the gap: New York has actually codified a regulation on exactly this text, and it does not broaden it.** [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research checked APL § 103 and Article 13 for definitions and stopped there; a further search this ticket ran turned up **2 NYCRR § 115.1**, *"Property to be reported,"* adopted by the same Comptroller's office and published in the New York State Register (effective 4/17/2019) — a codified regulation, not merely a Handbook gloss, and squarely titled to be the operational answer. Retrieved and confirmed verbatim against Cornell's Legal Information Institute mirror 29 August 2026: **[statute]**

> Property deemed abandoned pursuant to subdivisions (1), (1-a) or (1-b) of section 1315 of the Abandoned Property Law shall include, but not be limited to: (a) **Any unclaimed amount representing gift certificates sold** after December 31, 1983, including gift certificates for merchandise only, remaining unclaimed for five years. (b) Any amount representing outstanding checks issued on and after July 1, 1974 in payment for goods or for services, and **any unclaimed amount received** after July 1, 1974 for services not rendered or for goods not delivered that has remained unclaimed by the owner thereof for three years. (c) **Property referred to in subdivision (b) of this section** shall include, but not be limited to: deposits or payments for repairs not made or purchases of goods or services not delivered; unrefunded overcharges; accounts payable balances, **credit balances**, accounts receivable credit balances, rebates, and outstanding checks issued to vendors and suppliers of goods or services.

— 2 NYCRR § 115.1, emphasis added.

**This regulation is a restatement, not an expansion.** Its (a) reproduces § 1315(1)'s own "sold"; its (b) reproduces § 1315(1-b)'s own "received," word for word. And its (c) — the clause that names "credit balances" as an example — is expressly scoped as *"property referred to in subdivision (b)"*, so a credit balance only counts under this regulation if it independently satisfies (b)'s "amount received" trigger. **The regulation gives "credit balance" no free-standing, origin-neutral definition of its own** — it is an *example* of the kind of thing that can be a received-and-unclaimed amount, not a category that escapes the received requirement. **[inference]** This is the strongest single piece of evidence in this research: New York's own escheat administrator, writing a regulation whose entire purpose is to specify what counts as reportable property, chose to copy the statute's payment-shaped verbs rather than write a broader "held" or "issued" standard — which it plainly knew how to do, since the Comptroller's own Handbook uses "issued" one paragraph over (quoted below). **That a drafter had "issued" available and used "received" instead, in the one document written specifically to answer "what must be reported," raises confidence in the [section 1](#1-the-operative-text-read-for-the-word-that-matters-this-time-consideration) reading rather than leaving it purely inferential.**

**Where the Handbook does use origin-neutral language, and why that does not settle it either.** The Business-to-Business Transactions section — the same section [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390) relied on for the no-B2B-exemption rule — glosses a credit balance's start date this way: **[OSC]**

> The three-year dormancy period on credit balances commences at the time the credit was issued.

— *Handbook for Reporters of Unclaimed Funds*, "Business-to-Business Transactions."

*"Issued"* is broader than *"sold"* or *"received,"* and read on its own this sentence would sweep in a granted Credit. **[inference]** But context limits it: this passage is explaining *when the clock starts running* for a credit balance the Comptroller has already assumed is reportable — it is not independently establishing reportability, and the "credit balance" concept it glosses is introduced one paragraph earlier by quoting § 1315 itself, the very subdivision whose own text requires an amount to have been *received*. A plain-English timing gloss cannot expand the statute it is glossing. **[inference]** Read together with the checked absence above, the honest description is: **the Comptroller's guidance neither confirms nor forecloses this fact pattern.** It was written for the ordinary case — a business that overpaid a vendor, or a customer who returned merchandise — where consideration is never in doubt, and no drafter appears to have contemplated a balance issued for free.

## 3. Does `founding_grant` differ from `signup_bonus`?

**Not in what the statute asks.** § 1315(1-b)'s trigger is whether an amount *"was received,"* and § 1315(1)'s trigger is whether a certificate *"was sold."* Neither turns on who the recipient is, what relationship produced the grant, or how large it is. A `founding_grant` issued to a fourteen-doula agency that Doula Cloud has an ongoing pilot relationship with, and a `signup_bonus` issued to an anonymous self-serve signup, are identical on the one fact the statute tests: **no amount was received for either.** **[inference]**

**Two things do differ, and both are practical rather than legal — and both bear on the "not reportable" side of [section 7](#7-what-the-ledger-must-record), not on whether the grant is escheatable in the first place.**

- **Ease of proving recorded contact.** [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research established that even reportable business-to-business balances drop out of scope if the holder can show the customer *"used the credit balance, disclaimed entitlement to the credit balance, or [was] been made aware of the credit balance."* **[OSC]** A pilot Practice receiving a `founding_grant` is a named counterparty Doula Cloud is in active written communication with about the pilot terms — the awareness limb is close to automatic. A `signup_bonus` recipient is a stranger who signed up and may never be heard from again; awareness has to come from login telemetry or an affirmative notice, which does not yet exist anywhere in the codebase (confirmed below, [section 7](#7-what-the-ledger-must-record)). **This makes `signup_bonus` the harder-to-clear category operationally, even though the two are identical legally.** **[inference]**
- **Volume and per-dollar exposure.** `founding_grant` is roughly 150 Credits (~$3,000 at list price) across a known, small pilot population; `signup_bonus` scales with every future self-serve signup and is unbounded. If the answer in [section 1](#1-the-operative-text-read-for-the-word-that-matters-this-time-consideration) were ever wrong — administratively overturned, or litigated the other way — `signup_bonus` is the larger long-run liability of the two, simply because it has no ceiling. **[inference]**

Neither difference changes the escheat analysis. Both are reasons to build the record in [section 7](#7-what-the-ledger-must-record) regardless of which origin the row carries.

## 4. If a granted Credit is nonetheless escheatable, what value escheats

**Face value — $20.00 per Credit at today's list price — not zero, and not what it cost Doula Cloud to create.** This is answered conditionally, because [section 1](#1-the-operative-text-read-for-the-word-that-matters-this-time-consideration) concludes the better reading is that granted Credits do not reach § 1315 at all; but the brief asks the value question regardless of that conclusion, and the sources answer it cleanly if the trigger were ever found to be met.

**The statute values a gift certificate by what it is worth to the owner, never by what the holder paid to produce it:** **[statute]**

> ... including gift certificates for merchandise only in which case **the face value of such certificate shall be deemed the amount deemed abandoned** ...

— [Abandoned Property Law § 1315(1)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/ABP/1315), emphasis added.

The Comptroller repeats the same rule for the general case: **[OSC]**

> Gift certificates are **reportable at face or remaining value** even in those instances in which an expiration date is indicated or it is redeemable for merchandise only.

— [All Business Entities Reference Sheet](https://www.osc.ny.gov/files/unclaimed-funds/reporters/pdf/general-corporations.pdf), emphasis added.

**"Face value" is a per-unit price, not a cost basis.** Neither the statute nor the Comptroller ever asks what the holder spent to bring a gift certificate or credit balance into existence — a retailer's cost of goods is irrelevant to what a gift certificate for those goods escheats at, and that logic transfers exactly to a Credit. **[inference]** A Credit's face value is unambiguous in this codebase: [#439](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/439) set the list price at **$20.00**, the same figure a Practice would pay to buy an equivalent Credit today, and that is also the number [#449](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/449) already uses to size the grant in dollars for FIFO-lot-pricing purposes (a `founding_grant` lot priced at "$0.00 of consideration paid," which is a different fact from "$0.00 of face value").

**Zero is not the right answer**, and it is worth saying why the intuition is wrong rather than just asserting the number. "Zero" would follow from valuing the property at the holder's cost or at the price paid — which is the natural instinct for a refund (refunds run "at the price paid," per [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s decided policy, and the price paid for a grant is genuinely zero). **Escheat is not a refund.** It runs on face value because the point of the statute is to make the *owner* whole for what they hold, not to reverse the holder's transaction. **[inference]** A gift certificate given away as a store promotion still says "$20 off" on its face; New York's rule treats that $20 as what escheats, regardless of what the merchant charged the original recipient — which, for a promotional certificate, was also nothing.

## 5. Does this reverse #439's no-expiry decision?

**No, and it does not reverse it under either branch of the answer.** [#439](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/439) decided `founding_grant` Credits carry no date expiry. This finding leaves that standing, for two independent reasons that each hold on their own — stated separately so neither is doing all the work:

1. **If granted Credits are not escheatable** (the conclusion of [section 1](#1-the-operative-text-read-for-the-word-that-matters-this-time-consideration)), there is no state-forfeiture liability for an expiry date to protect against. The premise that would have made expiry attractive — "being generous creates a debt to Albany" — does not hold, so it supplies no reason to add machinery that removes value from a pilot Practice's grant.
2. **If granted Credits were escheatable anyway**, expiring them still would not help, because [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research already settled this exact mechanism for purchased Credits and nothing about that mechanism is specific to purchased Credits: **[statute]** New York's trigger word is *"unclaimed,"* not *"expired,"* and the Comptroller states plainly that the law **[OSC]** *"applies even in those instances in which the instrument indicates an expiration date."* An expiry date does not stop the three-year dormancy clock; it just takes the Credit away from the Practice before New York would have. Adding an expiry date to a `founding_grant` would convert a hypothetical state liability into a certain loss to the pilot Practice, without averting the state liability at all.

**What actually would matter, on either branch, is the same thing [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390) already recommended for purchased Credits: recorded contact.** If the escheat question were ever resolved the other way, the fix is the login-timestamp and annual-notice machinery [section 7](#7-what-the-ledger-must-record) describes, not an expiry date. This finding adds no new argument for expiry and removes the one argument ("we might owe New York for these") that could have supplied one.

## 6. RUUPA § 102 — contrast only, not New York law

**New York has not adopted RUUPA.** This section exists only to show that the model act's drafters treated "issued without consideration" as a real, decidable question worth writing a rule for — which is evidence that the gap in New York's own guidance ([section 2](#2-what-the-comptrollers-own-guidance-says--and-does-not-say-about-no-consideration-balances)) is a real gap and not a sign that the question is nonsensical. **RUUPA's answer is never treated below as New York's answer.**

RUUPA § 102(14) defines a "loyalty card" by its lack of consideration, in terms: **[RUUPA]**

> "Loyalty card" means a record given without direct monetary consideration under an award, reward, benefit, loyalty, incentive, rebate, or promotional program which may be used or redeemed only to obtain goods or services or a discount on goods or services. The term does not include a record that may be redeemed for money or otherwise monetized by the issuer.

— Revised Uniform Unclaimed Property Act § 102(14), retrieved from the Uniform Law Commission's drafted text 29 August 2026.

And § 102(24), defining "Property" — the term that triggers the whole Act — expressly carves a loyalty card back out after first listing "a credit balance" as an included example: **[RUUPA]**

> (24) "Property" means tangible property described in Section 205 or a fixed and certain interest in intangible property held, issued, or owed in the course of a holder's business ... The term: (A) includes all income from or increments to the property; (B) includes property referred to as or evidenced by: (i) money, virtual currency, interest, or a dividend, check, draft, deposit, or payroll card; (ii) **a credit balance**, customer's overpayment, stored-value card, security deposit, refund, credit memorandum, unpaid wage, unused ticket for which the issuer has an obligation to provide a refund, mineral proceeds, or unidentified remittance ... (C) does not include: ... (ii) game-related digital content; [or] (iii) **a loyalty card**[;] ...

— Revised Uniform Unclaimed Property Act § 102(24)(B)(ii), (C)(iii).

**A `founding_grant` or `signup_bonus` Credit matches RUUPA's "loyalty card" definition almost exactly** — a record given without direct monetary consideration, under a promotional/incentive program, redeemable only for the issuer's own services, not monetizable by the recipient. **[inference]** Under a RUUPA-adopting state, this would be a clean, drafted, on-point exclusion; the question this ticket asks would not need researching there.

**New York has no equivalent provision.** [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research already established, by checked absence, that APL § 103 defines only eleven terms and none of them is "holder," "owner," or "stored value"; this research adds that **none of the eleven is "loyalty card" or anything resembling RUUPA's no-consideration carve-out either.** New York's escheat statute was drafted decades before RUUPA (§ 1315(1-b)'s effective date is 1974) and has not been revised to add one. **The gap in [section 1](#1-the-operative-text-read-for-the-word-that-matters-this-time-consideration) and [section 2](#2-what-the-comptrollers-own-guidance-says--and-does-not-say-about-no-consideration-balances) is not evidence that no-consideration property escapes escheat everywhere — RUUPA states are decided the other way — it is evidence that New York decided the question implicitly, through the trigger words "sold" and "received," rather than explicitly, through a named exclusion.** That is a real difference for confidence: an explicit RUUPA-style carve-out would resolve this with certainty; New York's implicit, word-choice-driven answer does not.

## 7. What the ledger must record

**Checked directly against the schema, 29 August 2026.** `credit_ledger` (`api/db/migrations/00015_credit_ledger.sql`, extended by `00021_credit_ledger_consumption_origin.sql`) has `id`, `practice_id`, `origin` (`credit_ledger_origin` enum: `signup_bonus`, `purchase`, `consumption`), `quantity`, `consumed_engagement_id`, `consumed_at`, `created_at`. There is no per-Practice or per-Staff login, last-seen, or last-contact timestamp anywhere in the schema — a repo-wide, case-insensitive search for `last_login`, `last_seen`, and `last_active` (excluding `.git` and `node_modules`) returns no match anywhere in the repository, this document included at the time the search ran.

**The one field that already exists and already does real work: `origin`.** The brief asks whether "never paid for" itself needs to be a recorded field — it does not need a new column, because it is already fully recoverable from `origin`: `purchase` rows were paid for, `signup_bonus` and `founding_grant` (once [#449](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/449) lands) were not. **This single enum is what makes a granted-Credit row provably *not* reportable under [section 1](#1-the-operative-text-read-for-the-word-that-matters-this-time-consideration)'s reading**, and provably reportable under [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s reading for `purchase` rows — a query that groups outstanding balance by `origin` answers the escheat-scope question directly, with no inference required. **This is exactly why [#449](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/449)'s separate `founding_grant` origin earns its keep, independent of how the legal question above ultimately resolves.** **[inference]**

**What is missing, on top of `origin`, for either branch of the answer to be defensible three years from now:**

1. **A grant-date column already exists (`created_at`) and is sufficient as the dormancy start for a granted Credit**, on the same "issued" logic the Comptroller uses for ordinary credit balances — no new column needed for this piece.
2. **`founding_grant` still needs "who issued it and when" per [#449](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/449)'s own acceptance criteria** — not yet built. That answers "why does this row exist" for audit purposes; it does not by itself answer "is the Practice still around," which is the escheat-relevant question.
3. **Recorded contact does not exist anywhere in the product yet, for any Credit — granted or purchased.** [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research already flagged this against purchased Credits (recommendation 1: *"login timestamps must be retained per Practice for at least the dormancy period plus the retention period"*) and it remains unbuilt. It is not specific to granted Credits, but a `founding_grant`/`signup_bonus` Practice that never logs in again is exactly the population that would need this record if the answer in [section 1](#1-the-operative-text-read-for-the-word-that-matters-this-time-consideration) is ever wrong or overturned. **Building it once covers both purchased and granted Credits** — there is no reason to build two separate contact-tracking mechanisms.
4. **If the answer in [section 1](#1-the-operative-text-read-for-the-word-that-matters-this-time-consideration) is ever going to be relied on defensively** — i.e., "we did not report this because it was never paid for" — the record that proves it is `origin` itself, plus this research document as the reasoning trail. No additional ledger column makes the argument stronger than `origin` already does; what would make it *weaker* is losing the distinction between `signup_bonus`/`founding_grant` and `purchase`, which is precisely what a single undifferentiated "grant" origin (rejected by [#449](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/449) in favor of two) would have done.

**Net: the schema #449 is already building is the right shape.** The one gap this research surfaces that is not already someone's open ticket is login/contact tracking — real, but not new; it is [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s unbuilt recommendation, now confirmed to matter for granted Credits too, not only purchased ones.

## 8. What this does not answer, and confidence

**Confidence: high on the textual and regulatory reading, moderate on whether the Comptroller's informal Handbook-level guidance would agree if asked directly.** The distinction matters and is not a hedge — it is the honest shape of what the sources support. The statutory argument in [section 1](#1-the-operative-text-read-for-the-word-that-matters-this-time-consideration) is clean: two independent trigger words, "sold" and "received," both unmet by an unpaid grant, with no consumer or B2B qualifier doing any work either way. § 2's discovery of **2 NYCRR § 115.1** moves this from a purely textual argument to a *codified-regulation* argument — the Comptroller's own regulation, written specifically to define what is reportable, chose the same payment-shaped verbs the statute uses and did not write a broader "issued" or "held" standard, even though the Comptroller's own Handbook shows "issued" was available vocabulary. What keeps confidence short of certain is that the *Handbook* — the informal guidance an auditor would actually cite day-to-day — has never addressed this exact fact pattern in a sentence of its own, and the one passage that comes close (the "promotional gift certificates" / "these items" sentence) is ambiguous and sits under an unrelated statute's heading. **This is stated as high-but-not-certain, with the residual uncertainty at the informal-guidance layer named explicitly, not manufactured away.**

**What would actually settle it, if it needs to be settled with certainty before January 2027:**

- **A direct question to the Office of Unclaimed Funds** (`NYSRPU@osc.ny.gov`, the same office [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research names for reporting) asking specifically whether a promotional or complimentary credit balance, for which no payment was ever received, is reportable under § 1315. No FAQ or advisory opinion addressing this was found in the search conducted here.
- **A published New York Advisory Opinion or Tax Appeals Tribunal-adjacent ruling** on point — none was located. Searches for "promotional gift certificate," "no consideration," and "issued without consideration" against New York unclaimed-property sources returned only the two OSC documents already quoted and out-of-state material (Washington's statute, discussed below).
- **What another state actually did with the identical question is informative, not dispositive, and is recorded here only as color, never as New York's rule**: a general web search surfaced that Washington State's abandoned-property law lets a promotional gift certificate expire within three years specifically *"if it is part of a promotional program without money being given in exchange"* — a legislature there chose to write a no-consideration exception directly into statute, the same choice RUUPA's drafters made and New York's Legislature has not made. This is the same pattern as the RUUPA contrast in [section 6](#6-ruupa--102--contrast-only-not-new-york-law): other drafters treated the question as real and answered it explicitly; New York's Legislature and Comptroller have not.

**What this research does not reach:**

- **Other states.** [#390](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/390)'s research already flagged that a Practice's escheat address governs under *Texas v. New Jersey*, and every non-New-York Practice carries its own state's rule, which was not researched there and is not researched here either — including whether that state's rule has a no-consideration carve-out New York's lacks.
- **Whether a court, as opposed to the Comptroller administratively, would read § 1315(1-b)'s "received" the way [section 1](#1-the-operative-text-read-for-the-word-that-matters-this-time-consideration) does.** No New York case construing § 1315(1-b) against a no-consideration fact pattern was located in this research; none of the sources here are judicial.
- **Whether Doula Cloud should report anyway, as a matter of risk tolerance rather than legal requirement**, once a `founding_grant` Practice goes dormant. That is a business decision built on top of this finding, not a finding this document makes.
