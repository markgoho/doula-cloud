# Changed terms of service and privacy policy: what notice and assent binds a customer

Research question: when an online service changes its Terms of Service or Privacy Policy after a customer agreed to an earlier version at signup, what notice and what assent does US law need for the changed terms to bind that customer. Scope is federal law, New York law, and the Second and Ninth Circuits, since those two circuits carry the largest body of online-contract-formation case law and are the most cited nationally. Researched September 2026. This file reports what the law says; it makes a determination at the end about the minimum and the stronger mechanism, without recommending a lawyer be consulted, per the terms of this research task.

## Summary

No US statute sets a single national notice-and-assent standard for changed online terms; the rule is built case by case out of ordinary contract law (offer, acceptance, and the covenant against illusory promises), plus the FTC's unfairness and deception authority for privacy-specific changes. Three propositions are settled across the Second and Ninth Circuits and the district courts inside them. First, posting a changed term on a website with no notice to the customer does not bind the customer, no matter how long she keeps using the service — the leading case is *Douglas v. U.S. District Court* (9th Cir. 2007). Second, email notice can bind a customer, but only if the sender can show it was actually sent to that customer's address, was reasonably conspicuous about being a legal notice rather than routine correspondence, and the customer had a real chance to act on it before being bound; email notice has failed in more reported cases than it has succeeded, because proving the first two elements is where companies keep losing. Third, a "we may change these terms at any time" clause, standing alone, has been held to make the whole contract illusory and unenforceable — not just the changed part — unless it is paired with a savings clause that exempts already-arisen disputes and a real advance-notice period. A re-acceptance screen (a button next to a sentence linking the new terms, that must be clicked before the customer can continue) has not been tested by a reported changed-terms case in either circuit, but it satisfies the same "reasonably conspicuous notice and unambiguous manifestation of assent" test that both circuits already use for initial account signup, and is the mechanism every commercial pattern surveyed below converges on for a business's own material changes. A privacy policy carries an extra federal rule on top of contract law: the FTC treats a material, retroactive change to how a company uses previously collected personal data, made without affirmative express consent, as an unfair or deceptive practice in its own right, independent of whether the underlying contract law would enforce the changed term.

## 1. Posting a change on the website with no notice — not enforceable

*Douglas v. U.S. District Court for the Central District of California* (ex rel. Talk America), 495 F.3d 1062 (9th Cir. 2007), is the case every later opinion in this area starts from. Talk America added four new provisions to its service contract, including an arbitration clause, and posted the revised contract on its website without telling Douglas it had changed. The Ninth Circuit refused to enforce the new terms. Fetched directly at [caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-9th-circuit/1307125.html](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-9th-circuit/1307125.html): "Parties to a contract have no obligation to check the terms on a periodic basis to learn whether they have been changed by the other side." The court went further and said even visiting the site would not have helped: "Even if Douglas had visited the website, he would have had no reason to look at the contract posted there." Critically, the court held that continued use of the service does not cure the absence of notice — notice has to come first: "Even if Douglas's continued use of Talk America's service could be considered assent, such assent can only be inferred after he received proper notice of the proposed changes." The court distinguished two prior cases where the modified contract had been enforced, and the distinguishing fact in both was that the customer got notice outside the website itself: "The customers in these cases received notice of the modified contract by mail. The service provider in Bischoff mailed the contract to the customer... and the service provider in Crawford gave notice to the customer that she could see the contract terms online or call the service provider to learn of the terms."

*Rodman v. Safeway, Inc.*, 2014 WL 6984703 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 10, 2014), applies Douglas directly to a website's own posted terms. Safeway argued that a customer's continued use of safeway.com after it revised its "Special Terms" (governing a delivery-fee program) showed assent to the revision. The court rejected that, in language that tracks Douglas almost word for word: "customers' assent to the revised Terms cannot be inferred from their continued use of safeway.com when they were never given notice that the Special Terms had been altered." This quote is confirmed by two independent secondary sources reproducing the same opinion language (a law-firm case summary on CaseMine and the Technology & Marketing Law Blog), but this research could not fetch the district court's own opinion text directly — see "What could not be confirmed."

Taken together, the rule from these two cases is unqualified on one point: a business cannot make "check our website" or "we posted it, so you're on notice" carry the notice burden. Something has to reach the customer through a channel she did not have to go looking for.

## 2. Email notice plus continued use — contested both ways

This is the most fought-over fact pattern, and the "for" side is weaker than it first looks — most of the reported wins for a company are cases the customer did not actually contest on the notice question, while most of the contested Second and Ninth Circuit cases found the notice inadequate.

**Against — Schnabel v. Trilegiant Corp.**, 697 F.3d 110 (2d Cir. 2012), applying Connecticut law. Trilegiant enrolled customers in a paid membership after an online purchase and then emailed them a set of terms, including an arbitration clause, after enrollment. The Second Circuit held the email did not give reasonably conspicuous notice, reasoning (quoted via the Technology & Marketing Law Blog's reproduction of the opinion at [blog.ericgoldman.org](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2012/10/second_circuit_5.htm); this research could not fetch the court's own PDF — Justia and FindLaw both returned errors, see "What could not be confirmed"): "A reasonable person may understand that terms physically attached to a product may effect a change in the legal relationship between him or her and the offeror when the product is used[,] [b]ut a reasonable person would not be expected to connect an email that the recipient may not actually see until long after enrolling in a service (if ever) with the contractual relationship he or she may have with the service provider, especially where the enrollment required as little effort as it did for the plaintiffs here." The court's holding turned on notice never being conspicuous, not merely on delivery being unproven.

**Against — Starke v. SquareTrade, Inc.**, 913 F.3d 279 (2d Cir. 2019). SquareTrade emailed a post-sale confirmation containing a hyperlink to terms with an arbitration clause. Fetched directly at [caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/1975340.html](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/1975340.html): "The 'Terms & Conditions' hyperlink is some of the smallest text in the email and comes after several prompts unrelated to the enclosure of the contract." The court also found no instruction telling the customer the link mattered: "The hyperlink appears without any 'language advising [Starke] to click on [it],' or a 'clear prompt directing [Starke] to read the Terms and Conditions.'" And it faulted the surrounding design: "The interface here is cluttered with diverse text, displayed in multiple colors, sizes and fonts, and features various buttons and promotional advertisements that distract the reader from the relevant hyperlink." The court concluded Starke "was not on sufficient notice of the terms of the Post-Sale T&C, including its arbitration clause." Email was proved sent here — the loss turned entirely on the notice not being conspicuous.

**Against — Daniel v. eBay, Inc.**, No. 15-cv-01294 (D.D.C.), isolates the fact question sub-question 2 asks about most directly: was the email proved sent. This research read the September 29, 2016 Report and Recommendation directly (PDF, docket entry 15, at [law.justia.com/.../173291/23/](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/district-of-columbia/dcdce/1:2015cv01294/173291/23/) — the magistrate judge's document itself, saved and read as a PDF during this research). That R&R actually recommended *granting* eBay's motion to compel arbitration, because eBay's 2012 change-in-terms notice went out through "My eBay Message Center" and gave users a 30-day opt-out window, and the plaintiff did not dispute receiving it or use the opt-out: "It is undisputed that Plaintiff failed to opt out of the arbitration clause when given the opportunity to do so in 2012. ... In so doing, he further manifested his assent to the terms of eBay's User Agreement and the arbitration clause therein." That recommendation, however, was not the final word. Multiple secondary sources (Kelley Drye's Ad Law Access blog and the New Media and Technology Law Blog) report that the district judge later rejected the magistrate's recommendation and denied arbitration in a 2018 order, on the ground that eBay had not proved it actually sent the notice to this particular plaintiff's email address — "eBay failed to establish mutual assent to arbitrate because it failed to meet its burden of demonstrating that Mr. Daniel was personally notified." This research located but could not read the 2018 order's own text (see "What could not be confirmed"), so the final holding is reported from secondary sources, while the R&R's opt-out mechanism and reasoning above are a direct read of the primary document.

**For (uncontested) — DeVries v. Experian Information Solutions, Inc.**, No. 16-cv-02953-WHO (N.D. Cal.). Experian's 2016 terms carried a clause letting it change terms by posting them, with continued use as acceptance, and Experian's own record showed it sent DeVries email notices of the change on specific dates. Per a secondary case summary (CaseMine), "[n]either party argued that DeVries did not assent to this agreement," and the court's February 24, 2017 order compelled arbitration on that basis. This research directly fetched a later order in the same case (the June 1, 2017 order denying leave to reconsider, at [cases.justia.com/.../299326/63/0.pdf](https://cases.justia.com/federal/district-courts/california/candce/3:2016cv02953/299326/63/0.pdf)), which confirms arbitration had already been compelled but does not itself brief the notice question, since it was unopposed. The honest reading of DeVries is not "email plus continued use was tested and upheld" — it is "email plus continued use went unchallenged," which is a materially weaker precedent than Schnabel or Starke's contested losses.

**Net finding for sub-question 2**: every contested case found in the Second or Ninth Circuit went against the company on the notice question. The one case where email-plus-continued-use bound the customer is a case the customer did not fight on that point. The email notice mechanism that came closest to working on the merits — eBay's message-center notice plus an affirmative 30-day opt-out window — worked at the magistrate level but reportedly still failed at the district court because the company could not prove the message reached that specific customer. The recurring fact that decides these cases is not the notice's channel (email is not disfavored as a channel) but whether the company can prove, with a record, that a specific, reasonably conspicuous notice reached that specific customer before she was held to have accepted.

## 3. A re-acceptance screen — treated as stronger evidence of assent

No reported Second or Ninth Circuit case was found that squarely tests a re-acceptance click-through screen presented specifically for a *changed* term after signup (a targeted search for such a case came back empty; this is flagged again in "What could not be confirmed"). What both circuits have tested repeatedly, and settled, is the same mechanism at initial signup — the clickwrap-versus-browsewrap line — and the standard they use there governs a changed-terms re-acceptance screen by direct extension, since the legal question (was there reasonably conspicuous notice, and an unambiguous act manifesting assent) does not change depending on whether the terms being agreed to are a customer's first or fifth version.

*Meyer v. Uber Technologies, Inc.*, 868 F.3d 66 (2d Cir. 2017), states the standard both circuits apply: "reasonably conspicuous notice of the existence of contract terms and unambiguous manifestation of assent to those terms." Fetched directly at [caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/1871227.html](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/1871227.html). Uber's registration screen put a hyperlinked "Terms of Service & Privacy Policy" directly below the registration button, on a screen with nothing else competing for attention: "The Payment Screen is uncluttered, with only fields for the user to enter his or her credit card details," the hyperlinked text sat "directly below the buttons for registration," and "the entire screen is visible at once, and the user does not need to scroll." On that design, the court held "Meyer unambiguously manifested his assent to Uber's Terms of Service as a matter of California law" by tapping Register — a single affirmative act, next to a conspicuous, linked reference to the terms, was enough.

*Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble, Inc.*, 763 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2014), is the mirror image and shows why a re-acceptance screen (clickwrap) is treated as categorically stronger than a mere posted link (browsewrap). Fetched directly at [caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-9th-circuit/1675706.html](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-9th-circuit/1675706.html). The court defined clickwrap as agreements "in which website users are required to click on an 'I agree' box after being presented with a list of terms and conditions of use," against browsewrap, where "a website's terms and conditions of use are generally posted on the website via a hyperlink at the bottom of the screen." Barnes & Noble had only the hyperlink, with no required click, and lost: "where a website makes its terms of use available via a conspicuous hyperlink on every page of the website but otherwise provides no notice to users nor prompts them to take any affirmative action to demonstrate assent, even close proximity of the hyperlink to relevant buttons users must click on — without more — is insufficient to give rise to constructive notice."

Read together, Meyer and Nguyen supply the exact standard a changed-terms re-acceptance screen needs to meet, even without a case naming that fact pattern directly: the screen must (a) be uncluttered enough that the terms link is the thing the customer's eye lands on, (b) sit next to the button the customer must press to continue, not merely somewhere on the same page, and (c) require an affirmative click that cannot be completed without passing the link — the three things Meyer had and Nguyen lacked. A blocking re-acceptance screen built this way is materially stronger than either posting alone (Douglas) or an emailed link (Schnabel, Starke), because it supplies both elements Douglas requires in sequence — notice, then an act that manifests assent to that specific, dated version — inside a single interaction the company fully controls and can log.

## 4. The "we may change these terms at any time" clause — illusory unless limited

*Harris v. Blockbuster, Inc.*, 622 F. Supp. 2d 396 (N.D. Tex. 2009), is the clearest statement of the problem. Blockbuster's terms let it modify "any part of the contract" — including the arbitration clause itself — "at its sole discretion" and "at any time," effective on posting. Fetched directly at [leagle.com/decision/infdco20090416981](https://www.leagle.com/decision/infdco20090416981). The court held the arbitration promise illusory because Blockbuster had, in substance, promised nothing: "there is nothing in the Terms and Conditions that prevents Blockbuster from unilaterally changing any part of the contract other than providing that such changes will not take effect until posted on the website." A company that can rewrite its own promise to arbitrate the moment it becomes inconvenient has not really promised to arbitrate at all.

The opinion also names precisely what would have saved the clause, by pointing to what was missing. It uses the term "Halliburton type savings clause" (referring to the Texas Supreme Court's reasoning in *In re Halliburton Co.*, 80 S.W.3d 566 (Tex. 2002), a case that upheld a similar modification power because it was paired with real limits): "There are likewise no 'Halliburton type savings clauses,' as there is 'nothing to suggest that once published the amendment would be inapplicable to disputes arising, or arising out of events occurring, before such publication.'" In other words, Blockbuster's clause failed on two independent grounds that a properly limited clause would not fail on: (1) it let a change reach backward onto disputes that already existed when the change was posted, and (2) it gave no advance notice period before the change took effect — "effective immediately upon being posted" is the opposite of an advance-notice or right-to-leave structure. *In re Zappos.com, Inc., Customer Data Security Breach Litigation*, 893 F. Supp. 2d 1058 (D. Nev. 2012), reached the identical conclusion on the identical reasoning — Zappos's terms let it "change the Terms of Use, including the Arbitration Clause, at any time without notice to the consumer," and the court held the arbitration promise illusory for that reason (reported via Wikipedia's summary of the opinion and a Quimbee case brief; this research did not fetch the court's own opinion text directly — see "What could not be confirmed").

The rule that emerges, and that is consistent with ordinary contract doctrine on illusory promises and the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing that limits a unilateral-modification power even where one exists: a "we may change these terms at any time" clause is safe only if it (1) does not let a change reach disputes, claims, or data uses that predate the change (no retroactive effect), (2) builds in a real advance-notice period before the new version takes effect rather than "effective immediately," and (3) in practice gives the customer a way to leave rather than be bound — either an express termination right tied to the notice, or, functionally, a subscription the customer can simply cancel before the new terms take effect. A clause with all three is not illusory, because the company has surrendered something real: the ability to change the deal on a customer already mid-dispute or mid-relationship without warning.

## 5. Does a business customer get a different answer than a consumer

Partly, and *Register.com, Inc. v. Verio, Inc.*, 356 F.3d 393 (2d Cir. 2004), is the primary authority for the difference. Fetched directly at [caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/1420249.html](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/1420249.html). Verio, a web-development business, queried Register.com's WHOIS database every day and used the data commercially, without ever clicking an "I agree" button on any Register.com terms. The Second Circuit still held Verio bound, because repeated, knowing commercial use substituted for a formal click: "Verio visited Register's computers daily to access WHOIS data and each day saw the terms of Register's offer; Verio admitted that, in entering Register's computers to get the data, it was fully aware of the terms on which Register offered the access." The court stated the general contract principle this rested on: "When a benefit is offered subject to stated conditions, and the offeree makes a decision to take the benefit with knowledge of the terms of the offer, the taking constitutes an acceptance of the terms, which accordingly become binding." A sophisticated business's repeat, knowing engagement with a service can supply the "notice and assent" Douglas requires even without any clickwrap step at all — something no consumer browsewrap case in either circuit has been willing to do (see Nguyen, above, refusing exactly this substitution for an individual consumer).

That said, this is a difference in what counts as *proof* of notice and assent (a sophisticated repeat commercial user's conduct is stronger circumstantial evidence than a one-time consumer's), not a difference in the underlying rule. Douglas's core holding — that posting alone, with nothing reaching the counterparty, is not notice — was not limited to consumers and has not been read as consumer-only by any case in this research; nothing in Douglas, Rodman, Harris, or Zappos turns on the customer being an individual rather than a business. The general commercial-law backdrop reinforces this: courts routinely hold sophisticated commercial parties to the terms they had a chance to read and did not, on the theory that a business "making the agreement" bears responsibility for reviewing what it was given notice of — but that is a rule about *actual notice* being available and unread, not a rule that a business can be bound with *no* notice at all.

**Where B2B versus consumer actually matters for this product.** Doula Cloud's paying customer is a Practice — a business — but the Privacy Policy in practice also governs data about individual Clients, who are not the paying party to any contract with Doula Cloud at all. The FTC's unfairness/deception framework in section 7 below is consumer-protection law; it runs to the individuals whose personal data is used, not to the Practice as a commercial counterparty. That means a Terms-of-Service change directed at the Practice can, in principle, rely on the more permissive commercial-notice reasoning in Register.com, while a Privacy Policy change touching Client data still has to clear the FTC's stricter, consent-based bar regardless of the fact that the Practice — a business — is the one who signed up.

## 6. What careful small-SaaS and payment platforms actually do

**Stripe.** Fetched directly at [stripe.com/legal/ssa](https://stripe.com/legal/ssa) (Section 11.8 of the Stripe Services Agreement). Stripe notifies users of a change "by posting a revised version of the modified portion(s) on the Stripe Legal Page or by notifying User," gives "at least 30 days notice (or longer period if Law requires)" before the change takes effect, and treats continued use as acceptance: "By continuing to use Services after the effective date of any modification to this Agreement, User agrees to be bound by the modified Agreement." Stripe's own text also contains the exact sentence Douglas rejects — "User is responsible for checking the Stripe Legal Page regularly for modifications to this Agreement" — meaning Stripe's own drafting is not, by itself, immune to a Douglas-style challenge; what saves it in practice is that Stripe pairs it with an actual 30-day notice period and often direct notification, rather than relying on the "check the page yourself" language alone.

**GitHub.** Fetched directly at [docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service) ("Changes to These Terms"). GitHub distinguishes material from non-material changes. For material changes ("such as price increases"), it commits to "notify our Users... at least 30 days prior to the change taking effect by posting a notice on our Website or sending email to the primary email address specified in your GitHub account," and treats it as accepted only after that window: "Customer's continued use of the Service after those 30 days constitutes agreement to those revisions of the Agreement." For non-material changes, the bar drops to ordinary continued use with no stated notice period.

**Basecamp / 37signals.** Fetched directly at [37signals.com/policies/terms](https://37signals.com/policies/terms) (which is where basecamp.com/about/policies/terms now redirects). The mechanism is much lighter than Stripe's or GitHub's: "If we make significant changes, we will refresh the date at the top of this page and notify users who have signed up to our policy updates mailing list," and the document frames all use as acceptance of "these latest Terms of Service" — no defined notice period, and notice is opt-in (a mailing list a user has to have joined) rather than sent to every account by default.

**Pattern across all three.** Every one of them uses posting plus continued use as the *baseline* mechanism, exactly the mechanism Douglas says is not enough on its own — but every one of them pairs it with something Douglas's facts lacked: Stripe and GitHub commit to a real advance-notice period (30 days) and a direct-notification channel as well as posting; GitHub goes further and withholds "accepted" status from a material change until the 30 days have run. None of the three commits to a blocking re-acceptance click-through for an ordinary change — that mechanism (section 3, above) shows up in practice mainly at initial signup, not as these companies' habitual response to routine changes; a re-acceptance screen is treated in the industry as reserved for a genuinely major change (a new fee structure, a new arbitration clause, a data-use change), where a company wants the strongest available proof of assent rather than the baseline notice-plus-continued-use mechanism.

## 7. The FTC and a privacy policy's retroactive material change

Yes, and the FTC has said so directly, twice, twenty years apart, using the same theory both times.

**In re Gateway Learning Corp.** (2004) is the FTC's first case on this. Gateway's privacy policy promised: "We do not sell, rent or loan any personally identifiable information regarding our consumers with any third party unless we receive customer's explicit consent." Gateway then began renting that data to marketers and changed its privacy policy afterward to permit it, without notice or consent from the consumers whose data it had already collected. Fetched directly at [ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2004/07/gateway-learning-settles-ftc-privacy-charges](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2004/07/gateway-learning-settles-ftc-privacy-charges): the FTC alleged "that Gateway Learning's retroactive application of a materially changed privacy policy to information it had previously collected from consumers was an unfair practice," and the resulting consent order "prohibits it from applying future material changes to its privacy policy retroactively without consumers' consent." This is the origin of the industry-standard "opt-in for material retroactive changes" rule.

**The FTC's 2024 restatement** shows the rule has not softened, and extends it explicitly to AI-driven data reuse. Fetched directly at [ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2024/02/ai-other-companies-quietly-changing-your-terms-service-could-be-unfair-or-deceptive](https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2024/02/ai-other-companies-quietly-changing-your-terms-service-could-be-unfair-or-deceptive): "It may be unfair or deceptive for a company to adopt more permissive data practices — for example, to start sharing consumers' data with third parties or using that data for AI training — and to only inform consumers of this change through a surreptitious, retroactive amendment to its terms of service or privacy policy." And, stated as a categorical rule rather than tied to any one company's facts: "A business that collects user data based on one set of privacy commitments cannot then unilaterally renege on those commitments after collecting users' data."

**What this adds on top of ordinary contract law.** Sections 1 through 4 above are about whether a *contract* term binds a customer. The FTC's rule is independent of contract law and does not care whether the changed terms would otherwise be enforceable as a contract matter — even a perfectly-drafted, Meyer-standard re-acceptance screen collecting valid contractual assent to a new privacy policy would not, by itself, satisfy the FTC's rule if the new use is materially more permissive and applied to data already on file: the FTC's remedy in Gateway Learning was expressly "consumers' consent," i.e., an opt-in, not merely notice-and-an-opportunity-to-object. A prospective change (a new user's data going forward, under a policy she saw before she handed over any data) is treated differently and more leniently than a retroactive one (reusing data already collected under an earlier, more restrictive promise); notice-and-opt-out is workable for the former, but the FTC's own language — "consumers' consent," "affirmative express consent" in the broader guidance this file's earlier search turned up but did not itself fetch — points to opt-in being the standard the agency actually enforces for the latter.

## Determination

**The minimum mechanism that is safely enforceable** for an ordinary Terms of Service change, under both circuits' case law and matching the pattern Stripe and GitHub actually use: direct notice sent to the account's own primary contact address (email, not only a website posting), stating in the notice itself — not merely by a buried link — that the terms are changing and naming what changed; a real advance-notice period before the new version takes effect (30 days is the figure both Stripe and GitHub converge on, and is long enough that a customer who objects can leave before being bound); no application of the new terms to a dispute, claim, or right that already existed before the notice went out; and a persisted, queryable send record — recipient address, timestamp, the specific terms-version identifier sent, and the delivery/open status if available — because Schnabel, Starke, and (on the secondary reporting available) Daniel v. eBay were all lost or nearly lost not on the theory but on the company's inability to prove that specific notice reached that specific customer. Continued use after that notice period, without more, is what closes the loop; Douglas is satisfied because notice preceded the inferred assent, on a channel the customer did not have to seek out.

**The stronger mechanism**, needed whenever the change is the kind the FTC or a court would call material — a new arbitration clause, a fee change, or any data-use change — is a blocking re-acceptance screen built to the Meyer/Nguyen standard: an uncluttered screen, a hyperlink to the new version's exact text positioned immediately next to the button the customer must press, a sentence stating that pressing the button means agreeing to the new version, and no way to reach the rest of the product without pressing it. The audit-trail record this requires, matching this repository's cross-cutting expectation: which staff member or account (`staff_id` or equivalent) saw the screen, the exact terms-version identifier presented, the timestamp of the click, and — since this is evidence a court will ask for exactly as it did in Meyer and Nguyen — a durable copy of what the screen actually looked like at that version and date, not just a boolean "agreed" flag.

**For a privacy policy specifically, the stronger mechanism is not optional the moment a change is retroactive and material.** Gateway Learning and the FTC's 2024 statement both treat notice-and-continued-use, however conspicuous, as insufficient once the change reaches data already collected under a more restrictive promise; the FTC's own remedy language is "consumers' consent," which means the re-acceptance screen (or an equivalent opt-in step, such as a checked, unticked-by-default box) is required, not merely advisable, for that category of change. A prospective, forward-only privacy change — one that only ever governs data collected after the customer saw the new policy — can use the minimum mechanism above; a retroactive one cannot.
