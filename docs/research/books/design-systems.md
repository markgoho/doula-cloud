## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, chapter by chapter, with its own exceptions, what has changed since 2017, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that a design system is a set of "interconnected patterns" and "shared practices" organized to serve a product's purpose (ch. 1, p. 18), and that what makes one effective is not its tooling but the shared design knowledge in the team about what good design means for that product (ch. 6, p. 165). Patterns are the repeating parts of an interface, split into two kinds the whole book turns on: functional patterns, the concrete modules such as a button or a form element, "a bit like nouns or verbs", and perceptual patterns, the descriptive styles such as color, type, spacing and animation, "similar to adjectives" (ch. 1, p. 27). Practices are how a team chooses to create, capture, share and use those patterns (ch. 1, p. 18). A pattern library is a tool to collect and share patterns and is not the system itself (ch. 1, pp. 31, 35). The author's evidence is three years as a designer at FutureLearn and eighteen months of interviews with five other companies, Airbnb, Atlassian, Eurostar, Sipgate and TED (Introduction, pp. xiii–xv).

The author says what the claim is not. The book is aimed at "small and medium-sized" product teams (Introduction, p. viii). It does not touch information architecture, content strategy or design research, and it is "not a technical book": there are no code samples and no analysis of development tools (Introduction, p. ix). It "isn't about what to design" and is not a guide to designing digital products, for which the author sends the reader to Cooper, Gothelf and Goodwin (Introduction, p. ix, and its footnote). Its focus is the functional and perceptual patterns as core building blocks; user flows, domain patterns and persuasive patterns are named as other kinds a system contains and set aside (ch. 1, p. 27). And the book is explicit that the right system for one team is not another's and that every approach it describes has downsides (ch. 6, p. 165).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Foreword (Ethan Marcotte, unnumbered front matter, PDF pages 7–8) and Introduction (pp. viii–xv)

Marcotte frames the book through the artist Emily Garfield, who "grows" a watercolor map by repeating and varying one pattern, and argues that reusable interface modules "aren't enough" without a larger process that keeps them unified (Foreword, PDF pages 7–8). The Introduction states the research question, what makes a design system effective and enduring, and the audience of small and medium product teams (Introduction, pp. viii–ix). It fixes the book's terminology: pattern, functional pattern or module, perceptual pattern or style, pattern language, design system, and pattern library versus style guide, where a style guide traditionally covers styles only and a pattern library a broader set (Introduction, pp. xi–xii). It then introduces the six companies: FutureLearn, where the author worked for three years; Airbnb's Design Language System, whose "distinguishing aspect" is strictness; Atlassian's open-contribution model with a curating team; Eurostar, building its first pattern library and given a dedicated team after a year; Sipgate, whose 2015 pattern library grew "too many patterns" for lack of communication between product teams; and TED, a small group with deep shared knowledge and no comprehensive pattern library (Introduction, pp. xiii–xv).

### Chapter 1, Design Systems (pp. 18–45)

The chapter's definition is patterns plus practices, "coherently organized" toward the product's purpose (ch. 1, p. 18). Its opening evidence is a pair of screens, Thomson Reuters Eikon and FutureLearn, where dense panels serve multitasking and a single spacious column serves "thoughtful reading", so the product's purpose shapes its patterns (ch. 1, pp. 18–19). Functional patterns come from the domain; perceptual patterns from the ethos or brand, and without them same-domain products would feel much the same, with HipChat and Slack as the example (ch. 1, pp. 20–21). Patterns are traced to Christopher Alexander's *A Pattern Language* and its 253 architectural patterns (ch. 1, p. 22), then to interface patterns such as tabs and dropdowns, with Bootstrap and ui-patterns.com as sources (ch. 1, pp. 23–24). The chapter's claim about distinctiveness is that most patterns are familiar and new ones rare, so distinctness comes from how patterns are "executed and applied", not from their novelty (ch. 1, p. 24). A design language emerges as the product is built, and articulating it makes it "actionable and reproducible"; Tom Osborne's visual loudness guide, a suite of buttons and links ordered by "volume", is the worked example (ch. 1, pp. 25–26). The payoff claimed is that time saved on redesigning a dropdown can go to finding out whether a dropdown is needed at all (ch. 1, p. 26).

Shared language: a button can mean an outlined clickable area, a link, or a form submit, so a team needs "a shared use of language", not only shared vocabulary; the "Sequence" module is the example, and Don Norman's gulf between system image and user model is the reason a user's interpretation matters (ch. 1, pp. 28–30). Pattern libraries have a long lineage, Palladio's *Four Books of Architecture* of 1570, NASA's 1975 Graphics Standards Manual, Yahoo's early web pattern library, and MailChimp's living one (ch. 1, pp. 31–35). Their limitations: even a comprehensive living library "is not the system itself", no library "will fix bad design", and a library "reflects the design system behind it", rigid or free (ch. 1, pp. 35–36); with automated generators a library "can be done much quicker", but until then it is only "a collection of modules" (ch. 1, p. 37). Effectiveness is defined as cost-effectiveness in the design process plus efficiency and satisfaction of the user experience against the product's purpose (ch. 1, p. 37), and, via Donella Meadows, as subsystems aligned toward a shared purpose, the Whitney's dynamic W logo being the example of a system within a system (ch. 1, pp. 38–39). A fragmented system shows as conflicting messages, many shades of one color and several versions of one button (ch. 1, p. 39). The chapter closes with a fictional ten-minute recipe site walked through purpose and values, design principles, behaviors and functional patterns, aesthetics and perceptual patterns, and shared language (ch. 1, pp. 40–45).

### Chapter 2, Design Principles (pp. 46–62)

Principles differ by company, brand-focused at Pinterest, team-focused at GDS, temporary in Dan Mall's per-project manifesto, century-old at Jack Daniel's; larger companies keep separate sets for UX, brand and system, which can fragment the system (ch. 2, pp. 46–47). Atlassian is moving toward one set, "Bold", "Optimistic" and "Practical, with a wink", applied at different intensities along the journey: nobody wants "a bold support page" (ch. 2, p. 48). The chapter's definition is "shared guidelines" capturing what good design means for the team (ch. 2, p. 49). Four qualities of effective principles follow. Authentic: "Simple. Useful. Enjoyable" are givens, like accessibility and performance, and TED's "Be timeless, not cutting edge" is the counter-example of a principle specific enough to refuse a parallax effect (ch. 2, pp. 49–50). Practical: FutureLearn's "Make it simple" against "No needless parts", the latter answerable by walking the interface, with Pinterest's "Only one number 1 priority" and GDS's "Start with needs" as further rewrites, and the advice that nothing helps more than pairing a principle with a real example (ch. 2, pp. 51–53). Point of view: Salesforce Lightning's "Clarity. Efficiency. Consistency. Beauty" applied in that priority order, Medium's "Direction over Choice" traded formatting options for guidance, and Dan Mall's line that without a perspective without one a team may as well use Material Design (ch. 2, pp. 53–55). Relatable and memorable: the test is whether anyone in the company can recite them; working memory is cited through Nelson Cowan for "between three and five"; TED, Atlassian and Airbnb recalled theirs instantly, Airbnb's being "Unified", "Universal", "Iconic", "Conversational", and Spotify's acronym TUNE is in daily use (ch. 2, pp. 55–57). The chapter's four tips for defining them: start with the purpose, TED's being spreading the talks as widely as possible; ask team members to write five sentences each and compare; write for colleagues, not a brochure; and test and evolve them in critiques (ch. 2, pp. 57–60). From principles to patterns: Medium chose the simplest editor, TED's hero banner accommodates long titles because the message outranks the aesthetic, Atlassian's "optimistic interface" moves a JIRA card before the checks finish (ch. 2, pp. 60–61). Principles and patterns refine each other continuously (ch. 2, p. 62).

### Chapter 3, Functional Patterns (pp. 63–81)

Functional patterns "enable or encourage certain user behaviors" and are shaped by the domain; a recipe card is title, image, ingredients and a button, each with its own goal (ch. 3, pp. 63–64). The chapter's central evidence is FutureLearn over three years: the course progress module, discussion threads and the course list all changed in style, function and interaction while "their purpose fundamentally stayed the same" (ch. 3, pp. 64–67). Undefined patterns were duplicated: several progress modules and comment variants, and the general case of "30 different product displays" (ch. 3, pp. 67–68). Patterns need not be visual; they can be "read out by a voice" (ch. 3, p. 68). Five techniques for defining them early follow. A pattern map ties core modules to stages of a user journey, for FutureLearn discovering, learning and achieving, so the designer thinks in "families of patterns" rather than pages (ch. 3, pp. 69–71). An interface inventory, after Brad Frost, is done with printouts or Keynote, is comprehensive the first time and focused afterwards, and is repeated "every few months" at "a couple of hours" each (ch. 3, pp. 71–72). Viewing patterns as actions, "Promote a course" rather than "Image header", produced the name "Billboard" (ch. 3, pp. 73–74). Drawing a pattern's content structure, the core content slots and their hierarchy, let four social-feed modules be unified into one "Feed item", and lets designers know how far a pattern can be pushed before it "becomes something different" (ch. 3, pp. 74–77). Placing patterns on a scale, the visual loudness idea again, stops modules competing and stops a module being recreated at a volume that already exists; imagining the interface read aloud, with volume and intonation, is offered as a way to think about it that also helps screen readers (ch. 3, pp. 77–78). The book's advice is to treat content as a hypothesis, start with the purpose and build for content that fits it; when content keeps failing the pattern one of three things is wrong, the purpose, the design, or the content (ch. 3, pp. 78–80). The fragile module example is a FutureLearn page whose copy pushed tabs below the fold, nearly answered with a custom smaller heading (ch. 3, pp. 80–81). purpose "determines everything else" (ch. 3, p. 81).

### Chapter 4, Perceptual Patterns (pp. 82–106)

The house-with-the-same-furniture analogy: perceptual patterns are what make two houses feel different (ch. 4, p. 82). They "are always present" whether designed or not, and, the chapter argues, are effective only when they live "at the core of the brand" rather than as a skin (ch. 4, p. 83). Evidence that they express brand: the author's word processor, Ulysses, "crisp and calm"; Spotify's "ambience of intimacy" from imagery, the green-to-black ratio, subtle interactions and type, with "over 100 million monthly users"; Smashing Magazine's slight angles; and a recognition test where type, shapes, colors and icons alone identify Slack and TED (ch. 4, pp. 83–86). Relationships matter as much as building blocks: TED's Michael McWatters on red, too much red, or red in the wrong place, and it "doesn't feel like TED anymore" (ch. 4, pp. 86–87). Perceptual patterns connect a modular system, Vox and the Guardian being the contrast, with a footnote that Vox went denser weeks after the screenshot (ch. 4, p. 87); they carry a brand across platforms that follow native conventions strictly, and Twitter's heart animation is the small detail that propagates (ch. 4, pp. 88–89). Exploring them: Aarron Walter's design persona and five to seven traits, MailChimp's "Fun, but not childish", or the author's alternative of a place and its ambience (ch. 4, pp. 89–90); then mood boards, Samantha Warren's style tiles, and Dan Mall's element collages, distinguished by fidelity (ch. 4, pp. 91–94). Iteration: Wolff Olins' initial FutureLearn brand became "more grounded" and more practical once applied to real screens, and the icons gained a gap meaning learning is never complete (ch. 4, pp. 94–97). Balancing brand with consistency: a "fine line" separates consistency from uniformity; FutureLearn's courses page became cleaner and better for SEO and metrics but some early "visual distinctiveness" was lost, so a good system balances consistency with "creative expression" (ch. 4, pp. 98–99). Signature moments, from Dan Saffer, are small interactions with a story behind them, such as TED's play-button ripple echoing its video intro (ch. 4, pp. 99–100). Small-scale experiments: a celebratory circular progress button did not feel part of the system until the team started echoing its circle, bounce and tick elsewhere; triangles introduced on the home page worked only "sparingly" in discovery and marketing areas, never on learning pages (ch. 4, pp. 100–102). Business requirements: a yellow "Just started" banner was fine with few courses and "garish and sales-oriented" with many, and slow to fix because it was "seen as part of styling" (ch. 4, pp. 103–104). The chapter ends with a team exercise listing a product's signature perceptual patterns, FutureLearn's list running from tone of voice through "1px light-gray strokes" to "triangles in promotional areas" (ch. 4, pp. 104–106).

### Chapter 5, Shared Language (pp. 107–130)

Teams add sloppy patterns and duplicate modules unless they share an understanding of the system, and Alexander's pattern language, groups building as if with "a single mind", is the model, with the caveat that Alexander does not say how much work it takes (ch. 5, pp. 107–108). Naming: after James Britton, naming an object brings it into existence, and a button named "pink" can only be pink (ch. 5, p. 109). Sipgate's presentational names, "Prominent tile", "Circle with a dot", fragmented the system, in Tobias Ritterbach's words because patterns cannot be found as the library grows (ch. 5, p. 110). Atlassian names from the user's perspective, "Lozenges", "Inline Edit", an engineering overhead that is "driving user empathy" (ch. 5, pp. 110–111). FutureLearn's good name is "focused, memorable and embodies the purpose", and the effective ones were metaphorical, "Brackets" and "Spotlight"; had personality, "Minions" and "Boss", with `.minion` and `.boss` in the CSS, "Whisperbox" and "Boombox"; and communicated purpose, many minions on a page but "only one boss" (ch. 5, pp. 111–116). Names nobody remembered, "Progress toggle button", "Binary radio button", were recreated rather than reused (ch. 5, pp. 113–114). A hard naming problem is "a red flag": Eurostar's SEO-only module became "le blurb" (ch. 5, pp. 116–117). Naming collaboratively means diverse perspectives, not headcount, done in a dedicated Slack channel, tested with users on paper cards, and closed by the pair who built the module (ch. 5, pp. 118–121). Immersion: a pattern wall of A3 printouts in journey order, MOO's style-guide postcards, a Slack bot that reminds the team of names, referring to objects by their agreed names even when it sounds bizarre, induction courses at FutureLearn and Atlassian, half-hour design-system catch-ups for 16–20 people weekly then fortnightly, cross-discipline pairing, and a glossary, Intercom's being one language "from code to customer" (ch. 5, pp. 121–130). Three years earlier FutureLearn had a PDF brand document and a developer style guide and "didn't collaborate nearly as much" (ch. 5, p. 130).

### Summary of Part I (pp. 131–133)

Purpose, principles, patterns and shared language restated as one chain, with the observation that any team shipping interfaces already has a system, unless the process is "entirely random", and the question is what kind: flexible or fixed, cohesive or fragmented, free or hierarchical; problems recur "systematically" when the mechanism that produced them remains (Summary of Part I, pp. 131–133).

### Chapter 6, Parameters Of Your System (pp. 134–165)

Sipgate's cautionary opening: enthusiastic teams documented every pattern, and a year later there were so many modules that adding a new one became easier than finding one, leaving product sites full of duplicates, "thoroughly documented" ones (ch. 6, p. 134). The chapter's frame is three continua, strictness of rules, modularity of parts, and centralization of the organization, none binary (ch. 6, p. 135). Strict: Airbnb, over 2,000 employees and about 60 product designers, a DLS team of six designers plus engineers, an eight-pixel spacing grid, design and code "fully synchronized" with matching names and cross-platform modules, a three-step JIRA process for new components aimed at reusing "around 90%" of existing modules, and guidelines generated from the master Sketch file (ch. 6, pp. 136–140). Loose: TED, five or six people, design what is right over what is consistent, whiteboard sketches shared in Dropbox or InVision, a small set of "swatches" rather than a library, because there has simply been no need to document everything (ch. 6, pp. 140–143). What makes TED's system work is "shared design knowledge" rooted in the culture (ch. 6, p. 144). Strictness is not a function of size, the author having worked in a small team with an authoritarian creative director; strict systems give predictability and can become rigid enough to force "UX compromises" for consistency's sake, so they need room outside the rules and "clear, comprehensive and compelling documentation"; loose systems suit sensitivity to context and can turn "messy and fragmented" elsewhere (ch. 6, pp. 144–145).

Modular versus integrated: modularity means interchangeable parts, and its advantages are listed as agile, cost-effective, maintainable, adaptable and generative; integrated design is specific to content and art direction, more coherent, quicker as a one-off, easier to coordinate (ch. 6, pp. 146–148). more modular is "not always better" (ch. 6, p. 151); the architectural examples are Puma City's cargo containers, the Greendo apartments, and Paris's basket apartments that only look modular (ch. 6, pp. 148–151). Modular suits products that scale, adapt, repeat parts and have parallel teams, "e-commerce, news, e-learning, finance, government"; integrated suits one-purpose, one-off, art-directed work such as conference sites and Spotify's campaign pages (ch. 6, pp. 151–154). Modularity's costs: a reusable module takes longer and pays back only on reuse; modules go generic and composition and story are lost; teams force reuse, and FutureLearn occasionally sacrificed a page's potential impact; and modules combined badly give consistency without coherence, so the connections, relative loudness and hierarchy matter as much as the modules (ch. 6, pp. 154–155). The degree can move either way, and FutureLearn moved toward custom sections for marketing campaigns (ch. 6, p. 156).

Centralized versus distributed: a central group owns patterns, holds veto and curates, giving ownership and focused direction, as at Apple or Airbnb; a distributed model makes every user a maintainer, giving autonomy and resilience, as at TED and FutureLearn, where a dedicated team was "impractical" and everyone contributing a little is the only way its library stayed maintained for three years (ch. 6, pp. 157–158). Eurostar, four designers, four product managers, ten front-end developers, tried distributed and saw "a couple of people" doing most of it, then progressed after centralizing; the BBC's GEL went the other way, because each product team had strong views, and provides "platonic ideal" patterns that departments implement (ch. 6, pp. 158–162). Centralized groups become bottlenecks, Airbnb's new-module process taking "up to two weeks"; distributed ones dilute direction (ch. 6, p. 160). Atlassian is large, over 2,000 employees, with a curating team and an open-source contribution model (ch. 6, p. 161). The three scales placed: Airbnb strict, modular, central; TED loose, distributed; FutureLearn between, having moved from central and integrated toward distributed and modular while its rules grew stricter (ch. 6, pp. 162–163). Conway's law is invoked, and Sipgate's centralization is read as a cultural shift that first had to show the company what full autonomy looked like (ch. 6, pp. 164–165). The chapter ends: the right system is the one whose "downsides" a team can manage (ch. 6, p. 165).

### Chapter 7, Planning And Practicalities (pp. 166–183)

Systems start when a team notices its workflow failing, and a side project is not enough; senior support is needed (ch. 7, p. 166). The business case is cost: a comparison graphic of inconsistent buttons is not enough for a CEO, and "a modular interface" is a more familiar pitch than "a comprehensive design system" (ch. 7, p. 167). Three quantified arguments: at FutureLearn a simple custom component takes about three hours and a modular one twice that, but its second use is "almost free"; Nathan Curtis's button story, 25 teams each building buttons costs an enterprise "$1,000,000 to have good buttons" (ch. 7, pp. 167–168); Etsy's diff for restyling buttons, from Marco Suarez's "Designed for Growth", shows how much code a small change touches (ch. 7, p. 169); and faster launches, a page from the library taking days against weeks for a new design, with Sipgate's Tobias Ritterbach claiming pages built "10–20 times faster" on the connected library (ch. 7, pp. 170–171). Other benefits: brand unity, visual consistency as "small promises throughout the interface", and collaboration, with Karri Saarinen on "huge leaps in productivity" after a week or two of a master Sketch file (ch. 7, pp. 171–172). Laura Elizabeth's advice is a small test project; even one sprint with two people is a start (ch. 7, p. 173). Under "Where to start", the chapter has a team agree goals in two groups, systematize the interface and establish shared processes, then keep a simple road map, at FutureLearn folded into the product road map, and expectations of "gradual and steady" rather than dramatic improvement (ch. 7, pp. 174–176). Under its heading on transparency, Eurostar published its style guide early, it made the team feel obliged to keep up (ch. 7, p. 176). A knowledge-sharing culture, in the chapter's telling, comes from the chapter 5 practices, "problem–solution" presentations, and Vitaly Friedman's component-a-day printouts by the kitchen sink (ch. 7, pp. 177–178). Morale: Atlassian's two-phase spike, "80% completion" quickly then refinement; FutureLearn's two-person sprint; and the lesson that adding every pattern as a screenshot first, then replacing each with living code, gave value a year sooner than refactoring one by one (ch. 7, pp. 179–181). The chapter closes with FutureLearn's first failed modular experiment, slices with no purpose, no names and no thought of reuse that "never made it into production", and announces the three-step exercise of the next two chapters: identify behaviors or qualities, audit existing elements, define patterns (ch. 7, pp. 181–183).

### Chapter 8, Systemizing Functional Patterns (pp. 184–211)

A bookstore whose sofas and handwritten notes reflect an ethos of discovery over sales is the opening image; Slack and Tinder are products that encourage different behaviors around similar goals, and behaviors are "platform-neutral", which is what makes patterns defined through them future-proof (ch. 8, pp. 184–185). A footnote names dark patterns as the same mechanism used to hijack attention (ch. 8, p. 185). The purpose-directed inventory groups elements by the behavior they support, not by appearance, so one group may hold things that look different; the outcome is a list of elements to standardize and sketches, not an accounting of every inconsistency (ch. 8, pp. 185–187). Preparation, as the book lays it out: after the foundational UX work and before a redesign; four to eight people across disciplines including a back-end developer, content and product; two printed sets of about 10–12 key screens, one on the wall in journey order and one for cutting (ch. 8, pp. 187–189). Step one identifies behaviors per journey segment, for the public-library example Discovery, Catalog and Wish list, and notes screens with conflicting behaviors; "the words we choose matter", with FutureLearn's "retention" metric as the cautionary case, and "Discovering" preferred to "Promotion" because it has value for the reader too; behaviors are broken into actions (ch. 8, pp. 189–193). Step two cuts out and groups elements by purpose at one level of granularity (ch. 8, p. 194). Step three defines patterns with two tools. The specificity scale: the more specific, "the less reusable", and too many generic modules give "generic designs"; events and exhibitions are split if visitors are meant to perceive them differently and unified as "things to do in the library" if not (ch. 8, pp. 195–196). Content structure: elements that merge share a structure; elements that need to look different despite one structure become variants, a default with core styles plus additional ones, and the test is whether changing one should change the others the same way (ch. 8, pp. 197–201). Matching content names to style names, "book title" to "large title", and standardizing character counts and image sizes increases reuse (ch. 8, p. 201). Naming reflects the specificity position; "Course tabs" became "Page tabs" when it was allowed elsewhere, and naming "is also a UX decision" (ch. 8, pp. 202–203). The process repeats at smaller scale for buttons, headings, lists, tabs, form controls, feedback, navigation, images and icons (ch. 8, p. 203).

Buttons and links: the traditional split, links navigate and buttons act, is hard to design from; developers and designers define "button" differently; IBM Carbon reserves buttons for data changes while Shopify Polaris lets buttons navigate (ch. 8, pp. 204–205). The author's test is "a consistent expression of purpose", and the distinction found simplest is Heydon Pickering's in *Inclusive Design Patterns*: links versus calls to action, a CTA presented as a button and marked up as link or button depending on the interaction, it is "first and foremost" a CTA (ch. 8, pp. 206–207). Visual hierarchy: Marvel's flat buttons for mandatory actions and ghost buttons for optional ones; Atlassian's and Polaris's primary button as "the most important actions" appearing once per screen, with a basic button as default, and the interface read aloud as the test of loudness (ch. 8, pp. 208–209). Special cases such as a "Reserve" button with its own states or FutureLearn's "Progress toggle", which could have carried a function-specific name such as "Mark complete", are opportunities for signature moments and are kept occasional (ch. 8, pp. 209–210). The exercise is "a bit like gardening": the longer it is left, the harder it is to reshape (ch. 8, p. 211).

### Chapter 9, Systemizing Perceptual Patterns (pp. 212–242)

Two accordions with identical function, one feeling "flimsy and fragile" through a too-subtle hover, slow transitions and low heading contrast, open the argument that a functional tool also feels "simple, safe and robust" (ch. 9, pp. 212–213). Beyond style properties: Pivotal's color variables are typical, but "twenty shades of blue" are no problem while blue keeps one meaning, and FutureLearn's unified type scale did not stop headings being used inconsistently until usage guidelines existed (ch. 9, pp. 213–215). The starting point is aesthetic qualities and the signature patterns exercise from chapter 4, including where the design is off-brand ("small subtle animations", not bouncy ones) and proportions rather than lists ("primarily white" with pink and blue accents) (ch. 9, pp. 216–218). A list of styles to work through, color, interactive states, animation, typography, spacing, iconography, shapes, illustration, photography, voice and tone, sound, each by the same four steps: purpose, collect and group, define patterns and building blocks, agree principles; each needs its own inventory and possibly a sprint (ch. 9, pp. 219–220).

Color: purpose stated specifically, text hierarchy, links and actions, messages, separation and emphasis, data, against the Canadian Web Experience Toolkit's vague statement; an audit in a Google doc by type, example, value and feel; TED's black headers and FutureLearn's full gradient reserved for milestones (ch. 9, pp. 220–224). Defining patterns of usage before hex values: a red non-interactive heading on a site where red means interactive is changed or made interactive, and such changes can shift the whole aesthetic, as almost happened when FutureLearn nearly swapped its square progress module for circles (ch. 9, pp. 225–226). Building blocks: Marcin Treder found 62 shades of gray at UXPin; start only with what the roles need, only "three variations of blue" are needed; FutureLearn avoided tints and shades to keep the palette crisp, while UXPin's light and dark modes need several shades; a base value with meaningful increments applies to color, type, spacing and animation alike (ch. 9, pp. 227–229). Contrast is tested against WCAG 2.0 with Lea Verou's Contrast Ratio, Color Safe and Tanaguru, and a footnote notes that a palette built with accessibility from the start would not need such rebalancing (ch. 9, pp. 229–231). Principles: Sky's no color coding of sites and Oxford's explanation of where its blue is and is not used (ch. 9, pp. 231–232).

Animation: roles are softening state transitions, adding emphasis, and hiding and revealing; feel follows Val Head's brand adjectives; an audit is recorded with screen capture; FutureLearn found emphasis animations playful and state changes subtle (ch. 9, pp. 232–235). Building blocks are timing and easing, and Sarah Drasner's idea is a base duration with increments, with a base of 0.5 seconds a small icon takes less and a full-screen transition one or two steps more, because two elements at one speed feel different at different sizes (ch. 9, p. 236). Principles for a team not yet confident: reserving animation for "the most important moments" and keeping animation out of a task's way; Salesforce's advise short timing and subtle motion; Material's physical metaphor is offered as a mental model (ch. 9, p. 237). Voice and tone: Léonie Watson, a screen reader user, reports that her experience of products often comes through the "style of writing", so writing is a perceptual pattern and design, brand and marketing coordinate on it (ch. 9, p. 238). Ellen de Vries harvested Clearleft's language patterns from meetings and pitches; MailChimp's Voice & Tone shifts with the user's emotional state; Salesforce gives copy patterns per use case; Intuit's Harmony explains how to apply each principle (ch. 9, pp. 238–241). The summary treats each style as "a system in its own right" connected to the others (ch. 9, p. 242).

### Chapter 10, Pattern Libraries (pp. 243–284)

A library needs a system foundation; the chapter restates the chapter 7 strategies and adds that in every team interviewed multidisciplinary libraries are "more resilient and enduring", while one built for a single discipline "is more fragile": Sipgate's technically complex first library kept designers out, so they drew pages from scratch in Photoshop and developers fitted them "until they fit", producing "numerous if-statements, exceptions and duplicate patterns" (ch. 10, pp. 243–245). Content before tooling: FutureLearn spent "far too much time" on tools; a Google Docs folder is "an MVP pattern library", as WeWork's Plasma began (ch. 10, pp. 245–247). Organization: a table of nine systems shows "components" as the near-universal word for functional patterns and no consensus for perceptual ones, Foundation, Style, Design tokens, Visuals (ch. 10, pp. 248–249). Findability is among "the greatest barriers" to adoption (ch. 10, p. 249). Alphabetical lists avoid categorization debates; hierarchical schemes follow atomic design, but FutureLearn found no use for templates and pages, debated molecule versus organism for far too long, merged those two and kept two levels, and the author's observation is that more than two tiers "can get confusing"; a second table shows other systems' two- to four-level names; and stricter systems document larger patterns, looser ones smaller parts (ch. 10, pp. 250–253). After two years FutureLearn settled on classification by purpose, promotional, progress, communication, social; Polaris grouped by an open card sort and keeps studying it, Selene Hinkley noting designers think in structure and developers in function (ch. 10, pp. 253–256). "Work is never done" is the phrase heard most (ch. 10, p. 256).

Documentation starts lightweight: name, purpose, example, variants. A name is prominent and conveys purpose; a purpose is one or two sentences, and Sipgate's "Showcase" description was "factually correct" but useless where Fact Grid as "a shortlist of facts" giving "an immediate impression" works, plus content rules such as "Maximum of 12 facts" (ch. 10, pp. 256–259). Examples are self-documenting at Marvel and "less than inspiring" for FutureLearn's Billboard; living examples are preferred, static images where a state cannot be reproduced, as Carbon does (ch. 10, pp. 259–261). Variants are shown as a suite with their differences explained, Carbon's date pickers against Office Fabric's unexplained list, and Atlassian's button copy "could benefit from being more precise" (ch. 10, pp. 261–263). Optional extras: versioning, team members as at Sky, related patterns as in Polaris, and Vitaly Friedman's two checklists (ch. 10, pp. 264–265). Perceptual patterns are documented by usage, not only values: GOV.UK's color by role, Polaris's dos and don'ts, the US web standards' type pairings; styles cross-referenced at module level and together, Carbon's tabs and FutureLearn's grid of interactive states; relationships shown, Open Table's color hierarchy, and FutureLearn's spacious, regular and compact section types where larger, higher-contrast type "requires more white space" and consistent spacing values do not guarantee consistent density (ch. 10, pp. 265–271). The author calls separate style pages a limitation and hopes for libraries that show styles working together (ch. 10, p. 271).

Workflow: Nordnet's three steps, submit to a Dropbox UI kit, discuss, document, with a fortnightly Trello review; Shyp on GitHub; a submission template of name, description, author, date, and FutureLearn's three questions, what is it, what is it for, how does it achieve its purpose (ch. 10, pp. 272–273). Criteria for adding: either every element is added under a strict review, or elements are added only on second or third use with a log of undocumented ones; FutureLearn uses specificity of purpose, adding generic elements and treating a seasonal promo as a one-off that is shared and explained, and redefined as generic when someone else needs it (ch. 10, pp. 274–275). Responsibilities: adding a component can be part of the creating story; a systems team is a curator, Atlassian's model, or a producer, Airbnb's, and is "partners, rather than police", Amy Thibodeau's phrase (ch. 10, pp. 275–277). Aligning facets: code, design and the library are one system, and what matters is the same approach to naming, structure and purpose across them rather than full synchronization, Carbon's consistent folder names and Nordnet's BEM-named Sketch files being the examples (ch. 10, pp. 277–278). Tools: KSS-style CSS parsers, Pattern Lab for large atomic-design sites, Fractal as lightweight and unopinionated; full library-to-code synchronization is "extremely difficult", and Jürgen Spangl's Atlassian is "always slightly off-sync", and too perfect would not work (ch. 10, pp. 279–280). Master design files: Sketch is the designers' tool, Abstract versions it, InVision's Craft syncs a kit, UXPin, Brand.ai and Lingo manage a library without code; at FutureLearn the library became the source of truth and the Sketch master holds only stable elements, with paper sketches replacing specs (ch. 10, pp. 281–283). The future: tools that fit the whole team's workflow, since FutureLearn's library let no designer edit descriptions and left developers carrying documentation which at times "felt like a burden"; systems free time for "bigger and more meaningful problems" (ch. 10, pp. 283–284).

### Conclusion and Further Reading (pp. 285–288)

Alexander's 1996 OOPSLA keynote is invoked for the "moral imperative": will these patterns make human life better? The web builds for short-term commercial benefit, patterns that hook users or carry bias, and rarely considers what happens to accounts when someone dies or how inclusive a system is (Conclusion, pp. 285–286). Further reading names Alexander, Meadows and Stewart Brand as the three sources the author kept returning to, then Covert, Debenham, Frost, Marcotte and Pickering (Further Reading, p. 287).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits her own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** The book is for small and medium product teams and offers a perspective from the author's own design experience; information architecture, content strategy, design research and everything technical are outside it (Introduction, pp. viii–ix). It is not about what to design (Introduction, p. ix).
- **Pattern libraries.** A library guarantees no cohesion, a tool alone has "very little impact", and no library "will fix bad design"; TED's coherent product without a comprehensive library is the standing counter-example (ch. 1, pp. 35–36; ch. 6, pp. 142–143). Libraries are also "sometimes blamed for stifling creativity", which the author answers by saying the library only reveals the system behind it (ch. 1, p. 36).
- **Principles need not be written.** principles need not be official "or even written down"; what matters is agreement and awareness (ch. 1, p. 42). Principles cannot be "measured and quantified" and take iterations (ch. 2, p. 46). even the best-worded principle can be read differently (ch. 2, p. 53). Multiple sets work for some companies and fragment others (ch. 2, p. 47).
- **Novelty.** Entirely new patterns "are relatively rare", but the swipe gesture footnote allows that a product can be built on one, and products "live and die" on the timing (ch. 1, p. 24).
- **Early definition has limits.** an early product can change too fast to pin down, and FutureLearn's duplication is offered with "Perhaps this was inevitable" before the counter-question (ch. 3, pp. 64, 67). The inventory "doesn't have to encompass everything" after the first one (ch. 3, p. 72).
- **Content as hypothesis cuts both ways.** When content will not fit a pattern, the fault may be the purpose, the design, or the content itself (ch. 3, pp. 79–80).
- **Consistency is not the goal.** too much consistency "can also stifle" a brand; perfect consistency does not guarantee being "on brand"; FutureLearn accepted lost distinctiveness in some areas and experimented in others (ch. 4, pp. 98–99). A strict system can force "UX compromises" for consistency's sake (ch. 6, p. 145). much consistency with "little coherence" is named as a failure of modular design (ch. 6, p. 155).
- **Twenty shades of blue.** slight color diversions "aren't problematic" if the meaning is consistent; the concern is meaning, not the count (ch. 9, p. 214). More colors are legitimate for multiple themes and data visualization (ch. 9, p. 229).
- **Signature moments and one-offs.** Exceptions weaken a brand, and new requirements will demand "one-off tweaks" however the brand is guarded (ch. 4, p. 104). Special cases "should be occasional" but exist (ch. 8, p. 210). A seasonal promo is a legitimate one-off if shared and explained (ch. 10, p. 275).
- **Naming.** a good name differs by team (ch. 5, p. 110). Some people preferred the precise "Binary radio button"; the author's objection is only that nobody remembered it (ch. 5, p. 114). Too much input can produce "diluted or confusing names", so the final call stays with the pair who built the module (ch. 5, p. 121). start with "a more specific name" when in doubt (ch. 8, p. 202).
- **Shared language needs a culture.** not all teams are "equally collaborative", establishing a language requires "a certain kind" of culture, and it is "always a gradual, piecemeal process" (ch. 5, p. 130).
- **Every parameter has a downside.** Strict, loose, modular, integrated, centralized and distributed each get a stated cost, and one team's answer may not fit another; aspiring to "a system just like Airbnb" ignores that every approach has downsides (ch. 6, pp. 144–145, 154–156, 160, 165). Distributed "is not for everyone"; Eurostar's attempt failed and the BBC's centralization failed (ch. 6, pp. 158–161). Modular depends on the team and product and more modular is "not always better" (ch. 6, pp. 148, 151).
- **Reuse can be forced.** Teams sometimes "force the reuse" of modules and FutureLearn sacrificed page impact for reusability; the book's rule is that technical efficiency is balanced against the user's benefit (ch. 6, p. 155).
- **Timing of the inventory.** The exercise is counterproductive on a design with "fundamental flaws and usability issues" or one about to be redesigned (ch. 8, p. 187). The book places any redesign before the systemizing exercise (ch. 9, p. 226).
- **Buttons and links.** that criterion alone is hard to design from; both Carbon's and Polaris's opposite rules are presented as workable, because the test is consistency within one product (ch. 8, pp. 204–206). Marvel's simple purpose split is admired and then limited: a complex interface with many buttons cannot keep functions "so specific" (ch. 8, p. 208).
- **Atomic design may not fit "right out of the box"**; FutureLearn dropped templates and pages and merged molecules with organisms (ch. 10, p. 252). The order of systemizing functional before perceptual "is not critical" (ch. 7, p. 183).
- **Documentation.** covering everything at once "is not feasible" for small teams (ch. 10, p. 256). A living example is "usually preferred" but a static image or GIF is sometimes more helpful (ch. 10, p. 261). Full synchronization of library and code "is extremely difficult", Atlassian embraces being off-sync, and looser systems tolerate it (ch. 10, p. 280).
- **Adding patterns.** Both adding everything and adding on second use are presented with their own risks: duplication in the first, invisibility in the second (ch. 10, p. 274).
- **A pattern wall needs little space and not every screen** (ch. 5, p. 122). Catch-ups work with about "16–20 people" and larger groups take turns (ch. 5, p. 127).
- **The business case.** Some teams wait a while for a return on modularity, and the value "increases gradually over time" (ch. 6, p. 154; ch. 7, p. 176).
- **Patterns need not be visual**, and can be a bookstore interior or a voice (ch. 3, p. 68; ch. 8, p. 185).
- **The author's own credibility limit.** Her research is "insights from real-world products", mostly her own employer plus five interviewed companies, and the book is "just the beginning" of a conversation (Introduction, p. xiii; Other Resources, p. 288).

24. **Sketch as the designers' tool, a master UI kit, and the pattern library gaining "source of truth" status so the master file holds only stable elements** (ch. 10, pp. 281–283). Record: [ADR-0019](../../adr/0019-pen-dev-is-the-working-surface-and-code-is-the-truth.md), decided on [#412](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/412): pen.dev chosen over Stitch and Claude Design, one committed `docs/design/doula-cloud.pen`, a canvas node is "a sketch of something", never its definition, and `docs/design/doula-cloud.export.md` as a generated read-back.
25. **Documentation per pattern starts with name, purpose, example and variants, with variants presented "as a suite" and every state shown** (ch. 10, pp. 256–263). Record: [#104](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/104): each style-guide page every documented variant and state, "in light and dark"; [ADR-0018](../../adr/0018-templates-are-a-design-system-layer-with-two-named-exits.md): each Template gets a style-guide page with realistic content because an unfilled Template is "an empty frame"
26. **Typography and spacing are interlinked, larger, higher-contrast type "requires more white space", and consistent spacing values do not guarantee consistent density** (ch. 10, pp. 269–271). Record: the brief's "Rhythm": one spacing scale, one card padding, one row height, one gutter, "applied without exception"; `tokens.css`'s ramp, where every type size and spacing step is "a fluid step" with growth factors of 1.2 for type and 1.5 for spacing ([#531](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/531), [#540](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/540)).
27. **Perceptual patterns carry a brand across platforms and contexts, including campaign sites built outside the modular system that reuse "a few of the brand styles"** (ch. 4, pp. 88–89; ch. 6, pp. 153–154). Record: the brief's "Adopting this in `hugo/`": the palette, type scale and spacing are stated "as values", not app components, the marketing site may use the display step "more freely", and may not introduce "a second accent hue", a second family or shadows; [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93)'s out-of-scope note on `hugo/`.
28. **Patterns can "hijack user attention or manipulate people", and the pattern language carries "the moral imperative" that patterns improve human lives** (ch. 8, p. 185; Conclusion, pp. 285–286). Record: the brief's Motion rule, "no attention-seeking pulses", carousels or auto-advancing, and its Cognitive Bias row, "a default is never neutral"; [ADR-0028](../../adr/0028-the-shell-has-no-notification-bell.md); [ADR-0030](../../adr/0030-no-third-party-open-or-click-tracking-in-notification-email.md).

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **A sample of six, five of them interviews, and one of them the author's employer.** The book's evidence is FutureLearn from the inside and Airbnb, Atlassian, Eurostar, Sipgate and TED from interviews over eighteen months (Introduction, pp. xiii–xv). No company that abandoned a design system appears, and no team's outcome is measured against a team without one; the chapter 6 continua are illustrated by one company per end.
- **Correlation presented as cause.** Teams that made their work public "tend to progress faster" is offered as the author's observation with Eurostar as the sole case (ch. 7, p. 176). Sipgate's "10–20 times faster" pages (ch. 7, p. 171) and FutureLearn's three hours against six (ch. 7, p. 168) are interviewee estimates with no method behind them. Atlassian's "huge leaps in productivity" after a week or two of a Sketch file is a single quotation (ch. 7, p. 172).
- **The FutureLearn stories are told once from each side.** The circular progress button "received positive feedback" (ch. 4, p. 101) and the triangle experiments "worked with the brand" (ch. 4, p. 102); no user data is given for either, and the courses page that lost distinctiveness was "a positive change" for SEO and metrics (ch. 4, p. 99), so the book's own numbers point the other way from its aesthetic judgment.
- **A figure without a source.** The "between three and five" principles rests on a footnote to Nelson Cowan's working-memory paper (ch. 2, pp. 55–56), which concerns items held in mind at once, not principles a team can recall; the three teams that recalled theirs instantly are the three interviewed.
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** The chapter on parameters concludes that "the right system for you" is the one whose downsides you can manage (ch. 6, p. 165), which no case in the chapter tests, since none of the six teams is shown choosing between two directions on that basis. The gardening line, that an untended system gets harder to reshape (ch. 8, p. 211), is asserted rather than shown.
- **Examples that no longer exist or have moved.** Section 7 finds the Eurostar and TED URLs gone, HipChat discontinued, InVision redirecting to Miro, Abstract's domain unresolved, Pattern Lab and Fractal archived, and Polaris rebuilt on web components. The tooling chapter, chapter 10's last twelve pages, is the part of the book most tied to 2017.
- **Sketch-centric by assumption.** systematic designers "currently tend to use Sketch" (ch. 10, p. 281) is stated without a survey, and the whole master-file discussion follows from it; the tool that displaced Sketch is absent.
- **Vagueness the book itself warns against.** The signature patterns list for FutureLearn includes "Positive and encouraging tone of voice" and "Generous white space" (ch. 4, p. 105), which are the kind of unactionable adjectives chapter 2 criticizes in "Simple. Useful. Enjoyable" (ch. 2, p. 49). Chapter 9's animation principles, reserve it for important moments and keep it out of the way (ch. 9, p. 237), are offered as generic exactly where the chapter says helpful principles are specific.
- **Internal tension on naming.** Chapter 5 celebrates "Whisperbox" and "Minions" as names with personality (ch. 5, pp. 114–116) and chapter 8 regrets that "Progress toggle" was not "Mark complete", a plain description of function (ch. 8, p. 210); the two criteria, memorable metaphor and functional specificity, are never reconciled, and the "Binary radio button" nobody remembered was the precise one (ch. 5, p. 114).
- **Accessibility is present but thin.** Screen readers appear as a side benefit of the loudness exercise (ch. 3, p. 78), contrast gets two pages (ch. 9, pp. 229–231), and Léonie Watson gets a paragraph (ch. 9, p. 238); the CTA distinction is adopted from Pickering partly for keeping code "simple and accessible" (ch. 8, p. 208). Nothing in the book tests a pattern with assistive technology, and the book says accessibility is a given "done by design" (ch. 2, p. 49).
- **Nothing about a team of one.** The practices in chapters 5 and 7, a pattern wall, a Slack channel, catch-ups for 16–20 people, induction courses, a designer-developer pair with the final say on a name, assume a co-located human team of some size; the book says nothing about a system maintained by a single person, and tooling that generates a system is gestured at only in the closing pages (ch. 10, pp. 283–284).
- **The purpose-directed inventory is untested outside the author's practice.** The chapter 8 exercise is described with a fictional public library and screenshots from nypl.org "for illustrative purposes" (ch. 8, p. 190), and the chapter 9 color audit with the same fictional library; the one real run, FutureLearn's, is reported as an outcome, not shown.
