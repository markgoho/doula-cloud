## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the authors' frame, is that a digital sales funnel of five pieces of collateral — a one-liner, a wireframed website, a lead-generating PDF, a nurture email campaign, and a sales email campaign — is the foundation of a marketing plan for any business that sells with words, and that the plan fails on execution rather than on ideas (Introduction, pp. 8–9; ch. 1, p. 13; Part II opener, p. 39). The plan is presented as the letter Miller says he would send himself twenty years ago, built after he lost his savings in a failed investment and rebuilt by running the same five steps repeatedly (ch. 1, pp. 12–14). The claimed evidence is StoryBrand's work with more than ten thousand businesses (Introduction, p. 8; ch. 3, p. 35), a survey of StoryBrand customers by an unnamed independent research company, reported as a correlation between how much of the checklist a company implemented and its growth (ch. 3, pp. 36–37), and Peterson's doctoral dissertation, which the book says defended the framework as working for any business size and for both B2B and B2C while finding that success turns on execution (Introduction, p. 9; ch. 10, p. 152). The authors also assert that marketing is a science a reader can learn rather than an art (Part II opener, p. 39), and that marketing is an exercise in memorization: repeat the same words in the one-liner, landing page, and emails until the customer can repeat the offer back (ch. 3, p. 33).

The authors say what the claim is not. It is not a book about branding — fonts, colors, and how a customer feels are set against marketing, which communicates a specific offer (ch. 3, p. 31). It is not a theory book; the authors contrast it with business books they call long on theory (ch. 1, p. 13). It is not the messaging framework itself: a reader who wants to know how to invite a customer into a story is sent to Miller's earlier *Building a StoryBrand* (ch. 5, p. 95), and the free BrandScript tool is where the underlying language is generated (ch. 10, p. 155). It is not the whole of marketing — advertising is described as supporting the funnel rather than replacing it, and the conclusion calls the five pieces the basics (Introduction, p. 8; ch. 3, p. 34; Conclusion, p. 167). It is not sold as pushy: a repeated theme is that a direct request for the sale is honest about a relationship that is transactional by nature, and that the funnel controls the pace so the request is never premature (ch. 2, pp. 26–28).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction (pp. 8–10)

The opening claim is that marketing should be easy and should work, and that the easiest plan starts with a sales funnel, which is described as a way to capture and convert leads (Introduction, p. 8). The digital strategy — website, lead generator, email campaign — is offered as the foundation the rest of a company's collateral rests on, and advertising is positioned as support for the funnel rather than a substitute (p. 8). A free paper wireframe is offered at MarketingMadeSimple.com, and the authors say nearly all of the more than ten thousand companies StoryBrand has helped began by filling out those sheets (p. 8). Most marketing plans are said to fail in execution rather than in intent, which is the finding the book attributes to Peterson's dissertation (p. 9). The authors also make a moral claim about the trade: charging a client for marketing that returns nothing is called wrong, and so is a reader spending their own time on work that does not pay (p. 10). The reader is pointed to a directory of StoryBrand certified guides for hire (p. 10).

### Chapter 1: The One Marketing Plan You Will Never Regret (pp. 12–15)

Miller opens with his own story: a bestselling second book, ten years he describes as wasted resting on it, and then the loss of his life savings in a failed investment on a September morning (ch. 1, pp. 12–14). The five steps he says he should have taken are a BrandScript, a one-liner, a wireframed landing page, a lead-generating PDF, and an email campaign, run again and again (p. 13). The evidence offered is that Business Made Simple, the company he and his wife own, now gives more to charity each year than he lost, seven years on (p. 14). The chapter claims the plan scales: one funnel per product per audience, and a large company may run hundreds (p. 14). It closes with an unconditional promise that a reader who executes will succeed (p. 15).

### Chapter 2: The Actual Stages of a Relationship (pp. 16–30)

The organizing model: every relationship, including one with a brand, passes through curiosity, enlightenment, and commitment, in that order, and the stages cannot be rushed (ch. 2, p. 17). The worked example running through the chapter is Miller's own shopping for high-end audio equipment from Oswalds Mill Audio (pp. 17–18, 23–24).

**Curiosity** is framed as a survival judgment: a person gets curious about what might help them survive or thrive, the filter is described as running below conscious thought, and the decision is a snap judgment that sorts into keep and discard as a person scans what the book puts at three thousand pieces of marketing collateral a day (pp. 18–19). The practical instruction is that the website header, email subject line, opening of a proposal, lead-generator title, and elevator pitch each have to express, briefly, one way the brand helps a person survive (p. 20). What made Miller curious is given as status rather than sound quality, with the model in the photograph acting as a proxy for an aspirational identity, and the chapter concedes the mechanism is not rational (p. 20). Customers are said to be curious not about the seller's story but about their own (p. 21).

**Enlightenment** is the process by which a customer comes to trust the brand, and the chapter defines it narrowly: not the meaning of the universe, but understanding how something works (p. 21). The instruction is to answer the question of how the product solves the customer's problem, not how the product itself works (p. 22). The claimed mechanism is that a brain finds understanding pleasurable and confusion threatening, so people move away from confusion; the chapter uses politicians with repeatable simple messages as its illustration and asserts that a confused person's answer is always no (pp. 22–23). StoryBrand's own growth is credited to a lead-generating PDF, "Five Things Your Website Should Include" (p. 23). The audio company's video explaining sound-wave dimensions is the enlightenment example, and Miller's criticism of it is that it never connected the physics to his own survival (pp. 23–24).

**Commitment** is defined as the point where a customer is asked to make a risky decision, and the chapter gives two reasons orders do not arrive: the brand never asked, or asked too early (p. 24). A purchase is framed as giving up survival resources for a hoped-for gain, which is why haste feels threatening — the car-salesman-on-the-lot image (p. 26). Miller's counter-example is personal: not stating his intentions to the woman he later married cost him nearly three years (p. 25). The chapter's position is that a "buy now" or "schedule a call" button is not pushy but honest about a relationship that is transactional, and that a brand pretending to be a friend in order to sell reads as a user (pp. 26–27). Pace is quantified: about eight touchpoints before an order for most products, where a touch is an email, a site visit, an ad, or a talk, and reaching a person eight times may take dozens of sends because most are ignored (p. 27). Cheaper products need fewer touches; expensive ones need more (p. 28). Email is named the best way to stay in the relationship, and every product should have a campaign (p. 28).

### Chapter 3: An Introduction to the Marketing Made Simple Checklist (pp. 31–38)

Branding and marketing are separated: branding is how a customer feels, marketing communicates a specific offer, and confusing the two is said to cost companies millions (ch. 3, p. 31). Coca-Cola can afford branding because it has decades of familiarity; a company nobody has heard of cannot (pp. 31–32). The invented example is a year-long oil change sold under "save time, save money", which the chapter calls invisible language, against a billboard that names the product (p. 32). Most brands are said to make an "invisible first impression" — not bad, just unnoticed — with a nutritional-supplement client as the example, and Miller estimates anecdotally that more than half of all advertising makes this mistake (pp. 32–33). Marketing is then defined as an exercise in memorization, with Geico's fifteen-minutes-fifteen-percent line as the proof that a repeated offer gets memorized, and going viral is described as giving people something very simple to repeat (p. 33). A reader without a BrandScript is given four questions to answer instead: the problem solved, the life after purchase, the consequence avoided, and the action to take, with a dentist's three short lines as the model (pp. 33–34). The instruction is not to be cute but clear (p. 34). The five pieces are then mapped onto the three stages: one-liner and website under curiosity, lead generators and nurture emails under enlightenment, sales emails under commitment (p. 35). The chapter closes on execution: an unnamed independent research company surveyed thousands of StoryBrand customers, and the reported finding is that neither size, background, education, nor business type predicted growth — only how thoroughly the checklist was implemented, across every individual area and overall (pp. 36–37). Three graphs are named in the text — growth, team confidence, and time saved — and in this extraction only their captions survive (pp. 37–38).

### Part II opener (pp. 39–40)

The five tools are listed, and marketing is claimed as a science rather than an art, against what the authors say most marketing agents believe (Part II opener, p. 39). The reader is deputized a part-time marketer whatever their job title (p. 39).

### Chapter 4: Create Your One-Liner (pp. 41–55)

A one-liner is defined as a concise statement of what a company offers, the most powerful tool for making a customer curious, and the authors credit the form to Hollywood: the logline a screenwriter uses to sell a screenplay and then to sell tickets (ch. 4, p. 43). Its three parts are problem, solution, result (p. 43).

**Problem** comes first because it is the hook: a story with no problem has not started, illustrated by a deliberately dull coffee-and-newspaper paragraph and then the same morning with a squirrel dropping from the chandelier (pp. 44–45). Two further reasons are given: stating the problem raises the perceived value of the product, and it is how a brand gets remembered, on the headache-and-Advil association (p. 45). The cautions are to start with a real felt pain, to name only one problem, to name only a problem the company can actually solve, and — in a crowded industry — to name a problem the competition's own service creates (pp. 46–47). The StoryBrand one-liner's own first part is about business leaders struggling to talk about what they do (p. 46).

**Solution** should read as a reveal and must connect to the problem just stated; the vitamin example fails because a panel of nutritionists is not an answer to afternoon fatigue (pp. 47–48). Two failures are named: opening more story loops than it closes — the satellite-guided lawnmower against the same product described as working like a Roomba — and substituting cute language for clarity, with three flat product statements set against three slogans the authors say sell nothing (pp. 48–49). The cautions are to avoid insider language, to put the company name in the solution, and not to describe everything the company does (p. 50).

**Result** is the part that releases the tension, the equivalent of a film's obligatory scene (pp. 50–51). The Acorn Family Camp example shows a version where the three parts do not connect and a version where they do (p. 51). The technique offered for reaching a tangible result is to keep asking what a stated benefit results in — a good roof resulting in a worry-free home (pp. 51–52). The cautions: tie the success to the problem, make it about the customer rather than the company, keep it to one or two success points because commas dilute, and do not overpromise (pp. 52–53). Assembly advice is to read it out loud, keep the three parts in order, and make it memorizable (p. 54). The chapter's use instructions are to have the whole team memorize it, on the claim that a team that can repeat it becomes a salesforce, and to place it on business cards, in email signatures, on retail walls, in the about section, and in social profiles (pp. 54–55).

### Chapter 5: A Wireframed Website That Works (pp. 56–106)

The longest chapter, and the core of the checklist. Its premise is that words sell and design does not, and that most websites are designed by someone more concerned with color and feel than copy (ch. 5, p. 56). A list of nine avoidable mistakes opens it, among them insider language, too many words in the header, passive call-to-action buttons, calls to action not repeated down the page, images unrelated to the product, cute language, no lead generator, a header slide show, and a site that tells the company's story (p. 57). A website is said to serve a single purpose, creating sales, and the biggest mistake is complexity (p. 57). The designer's intake questions are reframed: not favorite colors and company history, but the problem solved, how the customer feels afterward, how people usually buy, and what unforeseen value the purchase added (pp. 57–58). The wireframe is a rough paper or digital draft of text and layout, produced before any money is spent on design, so the words can be settled and reviewed first (pp. 58–59).

**Nine sections** are given, with the header fixed at the top and the rest in any order the reader likes — the authors compare the sections to guitar chords and call the order hard to get wrong (pp. 59–60). They are the Header, the Stakes, the Value Proposition, the Guide, the Plan, the Explanatory Paragraph, the Video (optional), Price Choices (optional), and the Junk Drawer (pp. 59–60).

**The header** (pp. 60–70) is the first impression, and the chapter's one cited outside source sits here: Chao Liu and colleagues at Microsoft Research, via a Nielsen Norman Group article whose URL is printed in the text, for the claim that the first ten seconds decide whether a visitor stays, with the parenthetical addition that Liu also found the available time decreasing every year (p. 61). The test offered is the grunt test: whether an imagined caveman given ten seconds could grunt what is offered, how it makes his life better, and what to do to buy it (pp. 61–62). The authors suggest taking the header to a coffee shop and asking strangers (p. 62). On the first question, the instruction is to name the tangible thing sold in plain terms — lawn care, coaching, haircuts — and the chapter argues against using the header to differentiate, on the reasoning that clarity itself differentiates because competitors are confusing (pp. 62–63). On the second, one improvement is to be chosen and the rest saved for later sections (p. 63). On the third, the buy-now, schedule-a-call, or shop-now button is called the cash register of the store, and its absence is read by customers as the seller not believing in the product; the illustration is a clothing store that hides its cashier in a bathroom stall (pp. 64–65). Passive calls to action — learn more, find out about us, our process — are called weak and confusing, because a customer needs something to accept or reject (p. 65). Placement rests on Z- and F-shaped eye paths, stated as different studies revealing different patterns but agreeing that eyes do not move randomly, and the recommendation is two placements: top right, called the most valuable real estate on the page, and directly under the headline (pp. 66–67). Images: smiling people using the product are the default; header slide shows are to be avoided because text changes before it is read; looping film is fine if the text over it is fixed (pp. 67–68). The chapter claims a right header is up to half of what decides whether a visitor stays and buys (p. 70).

**The Stakes** (pp. 70–76) is the failure section, and the argument is that a story without conflict has not started — the Venice Beach volleyball paragraph is the deliberately flat example (pp. 70–71). Naming what it costs a customer not to buy is said to raise the perceived value of the product (p. 72). The evidence is an anecdote against the author's own instinct: an outside consultant wrote a stakes paragraph for StoryBrand's own site, Miller disliked it as off-voice and went in to have it removed, and the designer reported five new orders overnight; the paragraph is still there (pp. 72–73). The limit is immediately stated — negative stakes are the tablespoon of salt in a cake, not the flour — with Sarah McLachlan's ASPCA commercials as the case of too much, and the guess that happy dogs with a pinch of sadness would have done better (p. 74). A list of pains is offered, and three ways to show them: a few sentences, a testimonial about an overcome challenge, or bullet points (pp. 73–76).

**The Value Proposition** (pp. 77–81) follows the positive-negative-positive contrast the chapter borrows from screenplay structure (pp. 77–78). Its mechanism is stated as arithmetic on perceived value: an HVAC maintenance package priced at $200 is walked up to a perceived $500 by listing three benefits, so the customer is more likely to buy a $500 item for $200, and the chapter observes that words are free (pp. 78–79). The instructions are to say what the customer gets — money, time, reduced risk, quality, simplicity — and to be specific and visual rather than using words like fulfilling or satisfied (p. 79). Every section is to carry a headline, on the newspaper-article comparison (pp. 80–81).

**The Guide** (pp. 81–92) positions the company, not the customer, as the guide, on an oath the authors say StoryBrand's certified guides take to obsess over their customers' success (p. 81). A guide needs two things, empathy and authority, and the chapter's fitness-trainer pair shows each without the other failing (pp. 82–83). Authority is communicated by testimonials, client or press logos, and a simple statistic such as years in business or clients served, and the chapter says only a little is needed (pp. 83–84). Empathy is communicated by naming the primary pain point, by testimonials about care, or by plainly saying so, and the chapter offers a fill-in-the-blank sentence about knowing what something feels like (p. 84). Authority must relate to the customer's problem — a certified yoga instructor running a lawn-care business is told to leave the yoga off — and too much authority against too little empathy makes the story about the seller (p. 85). Testimonials get the most space: three or four on a site, collected the way a news editor cuts footage into soundbites, and three kinds are named — overcoming an objection, solving a specific problem, and adding value past a price hesitation (pp. 85–86). They should be short and scannable, and the chapter says a company may write them and send them to the customer for approval, explicitly not inventing them, on the grounds that customers are not writers (pp. 86–87). Head shots and real names are recommended where no NDA prevents it, because people trust those who stand behind their words publicly (p. 87). Logos are said to cost little room and to answer whether the seller works with companies like this one, and StoryBrand's own site uses a spread of sectors to defeat that objection (p. 88).

**The Plan** (pp. 90–97) tells a customer the path to doing business, on the claim that people will not walk into a fog and that an obvious process is not obvious to a stranger (pp. 90–91). Three steps are recommended, four at most, even where the real process has seven or eight, because more steps look more complicated (pp. 91–92); the caterer's three steps are the model (p. 92). The mechanism is borrowed from the instructions on a box of Pop-Tarts, by way of a Brian Regan stand-up bit: the three steps are not information but a signal that the thing is easy (p. 91). The chapter asks for each step to be a word or short phrase, with icons, bold headers, and a sentence of benefit underneath (pp. 92–93).

**The Explanatory Paragraph** (pp. 94–104) is the long-form section, placed deeper down the page where the authors say a hooked reader will give more time (pp. 94–95). It is also named as where SEO comes from, on the reasoning that long-form text using selling words helps whatever the algorithm is doing, and as the place a customer satisfies the feeling of having done due diligence (p. 95). The formula has seven parts — the aspirational identity, what the customer wants, the problem setting them back, the seller as guide, a plan, a call to action, and a vision for the customer's life — and is given as a fill-in-the-blank paragraph the authors advise following word for word on a first pass (pp. 95–97). The second option offered for this section is objection handling: list the top five reasons a person would not buy, write a sentence or two against each, and assemble them into a paragraph (pp. 97–100). Both may be used if separated, and the chapter states that no landing page is too long as long as the text and images stay interesting, while warning that a wall of text reads as work (p. 100).

**The Video** (pp. 101–102) is optional and should repeat what the page already says, because some visitors scroll straight to it and because repetition serves memorization (p. 101). The rules: keep it near three minutes, hook the viewer in the first thirty seconds — supported by an uncited claim that one study puts a third of viewers leaving by then — open on a problem, give the video a real title placed in bold above the play button, and consider putting a longer video behind an email address on its own landing page (pp. 101–102). The general rule is that the video is a sales pitch, not a brand-identity art piece (p. 102).

**Price Choices** (pp. 102–104) is optional too, and the chapter does not require a reader to publish prices at all — custom pricing and long catalogs are given as reasons not to (p. 103). Where prices are published, the chapter asks for bullet points of what is included under each one, and for every price or product to link to its own landing page built on the same formula (p. 103). Three options are recommended even for a company with one product, on the claim that customers like options and are then more likely to choose, and the authors report that StoryBrand's certified guides have found customers usually buy the middle item (p. 103).

**The Junk Drawer** (pp. 104–105) is the footer, and the chapter calls it the most important section because it is where everything previously thought important goes. Too many links at the top are said to cause decision fatigue, so contact, FAQ, about, and employment move to the bottom, where a visitor who wants them will scroll (pp. 104–105).

The chapter closes on process: write the wireframe on paper rather than in a digital tool, because handwriting slows a writer down and forces attention to word count, and because it keeps pretty images from distracting from copy (p. 106). A designer unfamiliar with the framework should not be allowed to talk the reader out of it (p. 106).

### Chapter 6: Lead Generator (pp. 107–125)

A lead generator is framed as the socially acceptable excuse to exchange contact details (ch. 6, p. 107). The chapter's valuation of an email address is that most people part with one about as readily as a ten or twenty dollar bill, that fewer people are willing to give one every year, and that this is good news because it makes those who do a better lead (p. 108). A good lead-generating PDF is sized at about twenty minutes of reading (p. 108). StoryBrand's own origin is repeated: a single PDF, "Five Things Your Website Should Include", downloaded by thousands, hundreds of whom came to paid workshops (p. 109). Six jobs are given: position the seller as guide, stake a claim to a territory, qualify the audience — with different generators for different segments, so each can feed its own campaign — create trust by solving a problem for free, create reciprocity, and carry an interesting title, since nobody downloads a white paper (pp. 109–111). On how much to give away, the chapter quotes the rule to give away the why and sell the how, then departs from it, saying some of the how should go too, and claims StoryBrand gives away more than most universities sell in an MBA (p. 110).

Ten formats follow, each with an exercise: an interview with an industry expert; a checklist; a repeatable worksheet; an educational event; a sampler or coupon; a webinar, which doubles as a PDF afterward; a keynote developed into a lead-generating event; a curiosity piece, where the example is a global pet-food brand's dog parks turned into a downloadable; a pitfall list; and an open house, which the chapter says has always been about the agent's relationships rather than the house (pp. 111–117). The advice is never to stop generating them, and to spend about as much time on lead generators as on products (p. 118).

The build guide names the title first and gives seven sample titles that worked, all of them numbered or specific (pp. 118–119). The content template is four sections: the problem plus an empathetic statement and a trust signal; agitation of the problem plus a solution; the step-by-step plan or list of tips as the main content; and the stakes plus a call to action (pp. 119–121). Distribution: promote it on the site, and use a pop-up, which the authors concede is annoying and defend as having a higher click rate than a regular ad (p. 122). Three pop-up tips: delay it about ten seconds or trigger it on exit intent; check the current search-engine rules, because they change and a penalty is possible; and do not offer an X to close, but a sentence the visitor has to read to decline — the chapter's own examples are confirm-shaming lines, and it acknowledges they may feel like clickbait (p. 123). More advertising money goes to promoting StoryBrand's lead generators than its products, and each gets its own landing page so links can be specific (pp. 123–124). Four writing mistakes are named: too many problems at once, too much text, vagueness, and a dull title (p. 124). The chapter's honesty about the hit rate is unusual: about 60 percent of the lead generators the company created found an audience and 40 percent were duds, and a PDF about preparing a business for a recession got almost no response (p. 125).

### Chapter 7: The Power of Email (pp. 126–129)

The two campaign types are defined: nurture, to keep in touch and earn trust, and sales, to close (ch. 7, p. 126). The chapter's answer to how many emails to send is as many as can be sent while adding value, with at least one a week recommended, and Miller's own weekday business tip offered as evidence that a high frequency can hold a list — tens of thousands subscribed and, he says, very few unsubscribes (p. 127). The writing advice is to read other companies' subject lines and magazine headlines, write conversationally, and keep asking what problem the email solves; four more rules are attributed to Ernest Hemingway — short words, short sentences, short paragraphs, and active language (pp. 127–128).

### Chapter 8: Nurture Email Campaigns (pp. 130–145)

Nurture emails are the drip campaign. The mechanism claimed is familiarity: a customer often has to hear about a product five or six times before buying, and the chapter walks a nine-touchpoint sequence in which emails supply three or four of them (ch. 8, pp. 130–131). The argument for consistency is timing — people buy when they are ready, not when the seller is ready, so the seller has to still be present — and the chapter adds that a weekly email arrives on the most intimate device a person owns (p. 131). It is explicitly the long game: the authors say a customer might receive weekly emails for seven years before buying (p. 132). Unsubscribes are called a friend, because the button's existence is what makes sending guilt-free, and unopened emails are defended as branding: a name seen while swiping to delete still registers (p. 132). Four jobs for an email: solve a problem, offer value, remind the reader there is a solution for sale, and send the reader back to the website, which the chapter calls the perfect elevator pitch (p. 133). The positioning goal is to be the first call in the seller's area of expertise (p. 133). The pattern given is to keep explaining why life without the product is not working while positioning the product as the how (pp. 133–134).

Three kinds of sequence follow. **Weekly announcements**: StoryBrand's Monday podcast email, where the real value is claimed to be the excuse to email the list every week, with a product advertisement at the bottom; a sample announcement is printed (pp. 134–136). **Weekly tips**: a recurring piece of useful content — recipes, leadership tips, seasonings — structured as a clear title, the problem, the tip, the guide's empathy and authority, and a mention of the product; a full sample gym email of ten weight-loss tips is printed, and the chapter notes each tip could become its own weekly email, making ten touchpoints (pp. 137–142). **Weekly notifications**: new inventory or promotions, where the examples are a shoe brand told to split its list four ways and send pictures of shoes, and a used-lifted-truck seller whose weekly Truck Tuesday email shows new inventory (pp. 142–143). One mistake is named explicitly: filling nurture emails with news about the company and its staff (pp. 143–144). The chapter's advice on starting is to start slow, repurpose existing content, and hire a copywriter, and it notes that unused lead-generator titles make good emails (pp. 144).

### Chapter 9: Sales Email Campaigns (pp. 146–151)

The sales campaign's purpose is to give the customer something to accept or reject (ch. 9, p. 146). The chapter makes an argument from belief: a seller who fears asking for money does not believe the product solves the problem, and should find a new product; where the product genuinely helps, selling it is called the right thing to do (p. 146). Passive-aggressive selling — mentioning products without ever asking — is read by customers as weak, and the dating analogy returns (p. 147). Rejection is expected and normalized (p. 147).

On order, the chapter gives two answers in two paragraphs. The first recommends running the sales campaign for about a week and then moving the person into the nurture campaign, on the argument that the one-liner, website, and lead generator already earned the right to ask (pp. 147–148). The second says the authors often recommend building the nurture sequence first and inserting the sales campaign later, because most companies grow without a sales campaign at all, while adding one has doubled sales for customers already doing well (p. 148). Four rules: sell one product per campaign; identify the problem it solves and repeat it; make the whole email a call to action, because in a sales context a polite request reads as weak; and give a short buying window, since a deadline forces action the way a deadline forces a film's plot (pp. 148–149).

The six-email sequence is: deliver the asset with the one-liner and nothing sold; problem plus solution, with the expectation that the third, fourth, or fifth email is what closes; a customer testimonial, kept short and full of soundbites, which the chapter says is often where results start; overcome an objection, on the reasoning that naming a similar objection gives a reader's own emotional resistance a focus; a paradigm shift, for the customer who believes they already tried this; and a plain sales email that only asks, with the limited-time offer in the postscript (pp. 149–151). The chapter calls the sequence more art than science, while saying this specific order has worked for thousands of clients (pp. 149, 151).

### Chapter 10: How to Execute the Marketing Made Simple Sales Funnel (pp. 152–166)

Execution is given a mechanism: six scheduled meetings, with designers, copywriters, approving managers, and support staff in the room, so the work has objectives, owners, and deadlines (ch. 10, p. 153). A reader working alone is told to keep the schedule anyway and to invite outside contractors (p. 153).

**Meeting 1, Goal**: decide which funnel to build first and what it sells. Miller's method is to ask which division or product is most profitable and which is least, on a sailing-ship analogy — shrink the sails that are not billowing and grow the ones that are — against what he says most leaders do, which is to ignore what works and try to make something else work (pp. 153–154). Three numbers are set: the goal, a failure number that triggers a review of the product or the campaign, and a stretch goal (pp. 154–155). **Meeting 2, BrandScript and one-liner**: three to four hours, using the free tool at MyBrandScript.com; the fill-in-the-blank BrandScript is printed, and the instruction is never to wander from the script once set (pp. 155–156). Four tests for the one-liner: does it sound normal aloud, can it be more conversational, is it memorizable, and would anyone need to ask what it means (pp. 156–157). **Meeting 3, wireframe**: the only agenda item is the wireframe, worked on a whiteboard with every person writing the agreed words on their own paper copy, so the team is literally on the same page; the chapter warns that creative is often confusion in disguise (pp. 157–159). **Meeting 4, lead generator and email sequences**: language overlaps, so they are done together; rejected lead-generator ideas are kept as nurture-email topics; the recommended shape is a sales sequence outlined first, but only if at least six or seven nurture emails will follow, so a person is not sold to and dropped (pp. 161–163). **Meeting 5, content refinement**: print everything and put it on a wall with tape and sticky notes, because the campaign will never again be visible at a glance, then read the emails aloud as a table read; Miller's own table read revealed the campaign had hardly mentioned customer problems, and he describes color-highlighting benefits and problems to check the balance; the launch calendar is set here (pp. 163–164). **Meeting 6, results analysis**: six review questions, and, where data exists, open rates, landing-page conversion, and which email is worth replacing (pp. 165–166).

### Conclusion (p. 167)

One page: imagine a year on with one or two funnels running, and the promise that a reader who follows the plan will see results, with the acknowledgment that these are the basics and there is more to marketing (Conclusion, p. 167).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the authors limit their own claim, admit a counter-case, or say a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** The book is the execution half of a framework it does not teach: a reader who wants to know how to invite a customer into a story is sent to *Building a StoryBrand* (ch. 5, p. 95), and the underlying language is generated by a separate free tool (ch. 10, p. 155). A reader with no BrandScript gets four questions instead of the framework (ch. 3, pp. 33–34). The conclusion concedes there are other aspects to marketing and calls these the basics (Conclusion, p. 167). Advertising is not replaced, only re-sequenced behind the funnel (Introduction, p. 8; ch. 3, p. 34).
- **Order of the website sections.** Only the header is fixed. The rest may go in any order, the authors call it hard to get wrong, and they compare the sections to chords a reader arranges into their own song (ch. 5, pp. 59–60, 70). Two of the nine, the Video and Price Choices, are marked optional (pp. 59–60).
- **Prices need not be published.** Custom pricing and long catalogs are given as reasons to leave prices off the site entirely (ch. 5, p. 103).
- **The three-price recommendation rests on practitioner report, not data.** The middle-item finding is attributed to what StoryBrand's certified guides have discovered, not to a study (ch. 5, p. 103).
- **Stakes can be overdone.** Negative stakes are explicitly capped — the salt-in-a-cake limit — and the ASPCA commercials are given as a campaign the author himself turned off, with the guess that a lighter version would have done better (ch. 5, p. 74). Their whole purpose is contrast with a happy ending, not weight of their own (p. 74).
- **Authority can be overdone.** Too much authority against too little empathy confuses the customer about whose story it is; authority unrelated to the customer's problem should be left off; and the chapter says only a little authority is needed (ch. 5, pp. 84–85). A reader with no testimonials and no awards is told not to worry and to move on (p. 89).
- **Testimonials may be drafted for the customer, but not invented.** The authors state plainly that this is not making something up, and that the text goes back for approval; names and head shots are used unless an NDA prevents it (ch. 5, pp. 86–87).
- **The grunt-test header is not the whole site.** The chapter opens the explanatory-paragraph section by answering clients who fear the header leaves their complicated product unexplained: deeper down the page, more text is allowed, and no landing page is too long while it stays interesting (ch. 5, pp. 94, 100).
- **Differentiation does not belong in the header.** The header is for clarity, and the authors argue clarity is itself the differentiator (ch. 5, p. 63) — while chapter 4 tells a reader in a crowded industry to differentiate inside the one-liner's problem statement (ch. 4, p. 47).
- **Pop-ups are conceded to be annoying**, defended only on click rate, and hedged twice: search engines change the rules and can penalize, so the authors send the reader to a professional first, and the no-X close is admitted to feel like clickbait (ch. 6, p. 123).
- **The lead-generator hit rate is given as roughly six in ten**, with one of the company's own thorough PDFs named as a dud (ch. 6, p. 125). The instruction is to test, keep what works, and start over on what does not (p. 125).
- **Videos are optional, and length is hedged.** The three-minute rule is offered as general, an interesting video may run five minutes or more, and the author immediately doubts most five-minute videos (ch. 5, p. 101).
- **Email frequency has no magic number.** The authors decline to give one, recommend at least weekly, and rest the high-frequency case on Miller's own daily list (ch. 7, p. 127).
- **The nurture campaign may be all a company needs.** The authors state that most companies grow without a sales email campaign, which is why they often recommend building the nurture sequence first (ch. 9, p. 148) — the opposite order to the one recommended a page earlier (pp. 147–148).
- **A sales campaign converts a minority.** Most people will still not buy, and rejection is presented as the expected outcome of asking (ch. 9, p. 147).
- **The limited-time offer is conditional**, phrased as something to do if it can be done rather than in every email (ch. 9, p. 149).
- **The sales sequence is called more art than science**, in the same passage that says this exact six-email order has worked for thousands of clients (ch. 9, pp. 149, 151).
- **The meeting schedule bends for a solo reader**, who is told to keep it anyway and fill the room with contractors (ch. 10, p. 153); meeting 4 does not need the whole team (p. 161).
- **Eye-path placement is hedged at its source.** The chapter states that different studies revealed different patterns, and keeps only the weaker claim that eyes do not move randomly (ch. 5, p. 66).
- **Funnels are plural.** One funnel is a starting point, not the plan: the reader is told to begin the next one immediately, and a large company may run hundreds (ch. 1, p. 14).

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **The central evidence is a survey nobody can inspect.** The claim that implementation predicts growth rests on an unnamed research company, an unstated sample, an undescribed instrument, and three graphs printed without axes or numbers (ch. 3, pp. 36–38). The text also names the finding correctly as a correlation — a striking correlation between success and implementation — and then states it causally in the next breath, that the more a company executed the checklist the more money it made (pp. 36–37). The sample is StoryBrand's own customers, so companies that bought the framework and abandoned it are the only counterfactual available, and survivorship is not addressed.
- **Correlation presented as cause, repeatedly.** The stakes paragraph on StoryBrand's own site is credited with five orders overnight on a single night's coincidence, with no control and no baseline (ch. 5, pp. 72–73). The value-proposition arithmetic — $200 to $500 in perceived value by listing three benefits — is invented numbers presented as a mechanism (ch. 5, pp. 78–79). The claim that a right header is up to 50 percent responsible for a purchase carries no source at all (ch. 5, p. 70).
- **A cited source does not say what the book says it says.** The parenthetical that Liu also found the time a page gets decreasing every year is not in the Nielsen Norman Group article the sentence cites, which is a 2011 summary of a single study and makes no claim about a yearly trend (ch. 5, p. 61; section 7). The one place the book reaches for outside authority is the one place it overstates it.
- **Unsourced figures used as rules.** Three thousand pieces of collateral a day (ch. 2, p. 19), about eight touchpoints before an order, offered explicitly as the author's opinion and then used as the design constraint for the whole funnel (ch. 2, p. 27), five or six exposures before a purchase (ch. 8, p. 130), a third of viewers gone at thirty seconds from an unnamed study (ch. 5, p. 101), and more than 50 percent of advertising making the invisible mistake, which the text itself labels anecdotal (ch. 3, p. 33).
- **Internal tension the book does not resolve.** The header must not be used to differentiate, because clarity differentiates (ch. 5, p. 63), while the one-liner's problem statement is where a reader in a crowded industry is told to differentiate (ch. 4, p. 47). The sales campaign should run first for about a week and then hand off to nurture (ch. 9, pp. 147–148), and then, a page later, the nurture sequence is what the authors often recommend building first because most companies grow without a sales campaign at all (p. 148). The six-email order is more art than science and has worked for thousands of clients, in the same two pages (pp. 149, 151). Every section is to carry a headline as a rule (ch. 5, pp. 80–81) while the section order is called nearly impossible to get wrong (pp. 59–60).
- **Tactics the book itself flags and recommends anyway.** The pop-up with no close button, declined only by clicking a sentence written to shame the person declining, is conceded to feel like clickbait and kept (ch. 6, p. 123). The deadline in the sales email is justified by its working in movies (ch. 9, p. 149). The book offers no test for when a technique that works has stopped being honest, in a text that elsewhere argues at length that a brand pretending to be a friend is a user (ch. 2, p. 26).
- **Claims the evidence does not reach.** The unconditional promise that a reader who executes will succeed (ch. 1, p. 15; ch. 3, p. 38; Conclusion, p. 167) is asserted and never bounded by market, product, or price. That nine sections work because hundreds of guides helping tens of thousands of businesses cannot be wrong is an appeal to volume, not a finding (ch. 5, pp. 105–106). The framework is said to work for any business, large or small, B2B or B2C (Introduction, p. 9), on a dissertation the book neither cites nor summarizes.
- **A model borrowed from fiction and used as psychology.** Curiosity, enlightenment, and commitment are grounded in survival instinct, primate behavior, and a brain that finds confusion threatening (ch. 2, pp. 18–23), with no study behind any of it; the same is true of the caveman standing in for the reader's customer (ch. 5, pp. 61–62).
