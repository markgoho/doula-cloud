## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the authors' frame, is that copy does not persuade alone: the color, typography, and images around it either support the emotions the copy is built to evoke or undercut them, and a conversion copywriter therefore has to be able to review and argue about design, not only words (Front matter, p. 3; Section 1, pp. 13–14). Its ground is Kahneman's two systems: design makes the first, fast impression on System 1, which decides whether the page is worth staying on, and the copy then does the slower work on System 2, so design has to "cater to both systems" (Section 1, pp. 6–8). The evidence offered is a set of GetUplift case studies in which a design change tied to researched emotions produced a measured lift: 97% more leads for a B2B commission-calculation product (Section 1, p. 12), 57% more sign-ups from a color change with "zero copy changes" (Section 2, pp. 20–22), and 12%, 18%, and 42% lifts in downloads, registrations, and revenue from an image change on an invitations product (Section 4, pp. 34–36). The book's own summary of what it is: an introduction to "the key theories behind emotion and persuasion" that lets a copywriter critique a client's design, discuss it with the design team, and charge more for it (Front matter, p. 3; Section 3, p. 32).

The authors say what the claim is not. It is not a set of universal rules: on color there is "no universal truth" and there are "no simple rules" that raise conversion (Section 2, p. 16), and the gender-preference charts "are not definitive" (Section 2, p. 19). It is not a claim that design alone converts: conversion is "a holistic process", never one element (Section 1, p. 13). It is not a treatise on typography: the copywriter has no need to memorize what every font group implies (Section 3, p. 23). It is not about copy or interaction design, which the editor's note says are treated as if distinct from visual design only to zero in on "design-specific problems" (Editor's note, p. 15). And it is "a beginning", an introduction that sends the reader to a paid workshop and two downloadable lists (Section 3, p. 32; Bonus section, p. 51).

## 4. The argument, section by section

### Front matter (pp. 1–4)

The cover subtitle sets the audience: a copywriter who wants to impress clients, "win designers over", and hold ground (p. 1). The author page introduces Talia Wolf as founder of GetUplift and creator of "The Emotional Targeting Framework", and Sophia Dagnon as a SaaS conversion copywriter (Front matter, p. 2). The premise: copy "isn't designed to go it alone", and design that is merely "cute" or "stylized" makes copy look good without helping it convert unless it rests on what the reader needs to see to "feel compelled to act" (Front matter, p. 3). Three promised outcomes for the copywriter: critique a client's design kindly, discuss design with the design team from case studies, and "win bigger projects" at higher rates (Front matter, p. 3). The book is split into "three pillars" of persuasive design, color, typography, and images, with a fifth section on using the knowledge (Front matter, pp. 3–4).

### Section 1: Why persuasive design matters (pp. 4–14)

The opening premise is that people buy "a better version of themselves", not things, restated as "We buy hope" (Section 1, p. 4). A grocery-store thought experiment argues that weighing every factor of every purchase would not fit in "the lifespan of a bowhead whale", so "our brains have shortcuts", the "hundreds of cognitive biases" that produce a gut feeling (Section 1, p. 5). The book adopts Kahneman's System 1 and System 2 from *Thinking, Fast and Slow*: System 1 is quick, intuitive, subconscious; System 2 is slow, careful, and "believes it's in charge" (Section 1, pp. 6–7). Evidence for System 1's primacy: in 2008 researchers with an fMRI scanner could "accurately predict" a subject's decision seven seconds before the subject was aware of making it (Section 1, p. 7). Evidence that decisions need emotion: Damasio's patients with brain damage that prevented feeling were "unable to make decisions", down to what to have for lunch (Section 1, p. 8). The mechanism on a page: design makes the first impression on System 1 "almost instantly", then as the reader scrolls, "System 2 kicks in", wanting "proof, reason and justification"; design must therefore be attractive enough to "warrant attention" and persuasive enough to keep it (Section 1, p. 8).

The case study, headed "Everyone has feelings (even B2B professionals)", argues that B2B buyers are the same people at the office as at home and that "we get push back" whenever emotion is proposed (Section 1, p. 9). Talia Wolf's client sold commission-calculation software at "tens of thousands of dollars" a year, entirely through a 60-minute demo, and wanted more and better paid-campaign leads (Section 1, p. 10). The original landing page (figure, p. 10) is faulted on four points: a business-focused headline with no reader outcome, a form focused on the business, no visual hierarchy, and a calculator graphic that "doesn't really connect" with the customer's intent, feelings, or stage of awareness (Section 1, p. 10). Reverse-engineering the page's "One Reader" (a Copyhackers term from *10x Landing Pages*, p. 11) gives "a stereotypical accountant-type"; research found instead an HR manager who "felt under-appreciated" and wanted impact and recognition (Section 1, p. 11). Three emotional triggers were tested through design and copy: empowerment, self-image, and social image (Section 1, p. 11). The variation (figure, p. 12) shows a "Lead Your Team to Success" headline over a photograph of a team, with a three-field demo form; it produced "a 97% uplift in leads", a full site redesign, and changed sales calls and email campaigns (Section 1, p. 12). The authors note the variation is not stunning, "not 'cool'", and follows no current trend, yet it lifted (Section 1, p. 12), and that conversion is never down to "just one element" and means "working within the client's sandbox" (Section 1, p. 13).

The section closes on how design decisions get made: many brands and designers choose colors, fonts, and layouts on "personal preferences, trends", what competitors do, and brand guidelines, without the customer, her stage of awareness, or what she has seen so far (Section 1, p. 13). Key takeaways: design captivates System 1 and keeps the reader long enough for copy to convince System 2; colors, fonts, and images support the copy's emotional notes; design choices rest on research and the page's goal (Section 1, p. 14).

### Editor's note, "But first… copy" (Joanna Wiebe, p. 15)

Wiebe traces creative leadership from copy in the 1960s to art in the 1980s to UX in the 2000s to today, when copy, visual design, and interaction design "collaborate toward a shared solution", with analytics having put the user at the center (Editor's note, p. 15). Nothing persuades until research on the user, product, and market is done, users have been observed and listened to, findings are married with "studies in human decision-making", a draft variation is made, and it is validated and iterated; research drives a hypothesis, the hypothesis drives the creative, and "Users validate creative". The note ends on the slogan "Copy is design" and its reverse (Editor's note, p. 15).

### Section 2: What you need to know about color (pp. 16–22)

Yves Klein's blue opens the point that color has meaning but not the same meaning for everyone, so there is "no universal truth" on color; color psychology is "matching appropriate colors" to the emotions customers feel and want to feel (Section 2, p. 16). Ruth Tofle is quoted that color theories have been "strongly normative and weakly analytic" (Section 2, p. 16). Three factors govern perception: culture, symbolism, and personal emotional experience (Section 2, pp. 17–18). Cultural examples: white is purity in the West and mourning in China; red is anger in Western and Japanese cultures but black is in Hindu culture; green is health in the US, yellow in China, pink in Japan; orange is fun for Nickelodeon and Fanta but religious in Ireland and spiritual in Hindu culture (Section 2, p. 17), with the Information is Beautiful "Colours in Culture" wheel as the figure (p. 17).

General trends exist "but wide differences between individuals" (Section 2, p. 18). A study by "Joe Hollack" is cited: 35% of women named blue their favorite and 23% purple; 57% of men named blue and 14% green; 33% of women dislike orange and 27% of men dislike brown (Section 2, pp. 18–19), with Help Scout's donut charts reproduced (pp. 18–19), and the charts called "a handy starting point" but "not definitive" (Section 2, p. 19). The copywriter's challenge is that a business already has colors, chosen by a designer, an agency, or "the client themselves", and colors are rarely chosen "with the customer in mind" (Section 2, p. 19); the advice is to go back to the "voice of customer research" and the emotions the reader needs to feel when reviewing colors for landing pages, ads, pricing pages, and call-to-action buttons, and, when a designer is on from the start, to help build a survey of underlying preferences (Section 2, p. 20).

The case study, "One color change. Zero copy changes.", concerns a web-analytics and heatmap product whose landing page had "a moving background" of shifting colors that distracted the reader while being "on trend", no trust elements, and images, colors, and fonts that did not convey value (Section 2, pp. 20–21; figure, p. 21). The copy claimed the product was user-friendly while "the dark overlay" undercut the claim; the variation was a "bright, clean design" in the brand's own color with social proof, a green hero with a "Trusted by" logo row (figure, p. 22). Result: a 57% increase in sign-ups and an 80% increase in code installs on users' sites, with no change to the interface (Section 2, p. 22). Key takeaways: preference varies by culture, symbolism, and experience; no universal truth; color choices affect conversion, so work with the designer on colors that support the copy's key message (Section 2, p. 22).

### Section 3: Your brief guide to typography (pp. 23–32)

Fonts "make your copy physically readable" and shape how it is interpreted, yet are usually "left for the designer to choose", taken from "old brand books", or, "if bootstrapping", they "hinge on the founder's preference" (Section 3, p. 23). Sarah Hyndman of Type Tasting is quoted, "Typography is storytelling", and fonts are "multi-sensory imagination grenades" (Section 3, p. 23). Her jelly-bean experiment: two identical jelly beans, "Eat me" set in a jagged font and in a rounded font (figure, p. 24); the audience rated the rounded pairing "17% sweeter" and the jagged pairing "11% sourer" (Section 3, p. 24). A figure of the Copyhackers wordmark set in a light outline, a horror-drip face, a brush script, and a swash italic asks how each makes the reader feel and how far each lets the reader "trust the copy that comes after" (Section 3, p. 25; figure, p. 25).

Five things to check. **Font type**: of four classifications (serif, sans serif, script, decorative), the book treats two; serif reads as "more traditional", sans serif "cleaner, modern"; for a SaaS company a sans serif "may be worth testing", while traditional industries or "an older audience" may suit a serif (Section 3, p. 25; figure, p. 26). The example is Lume, whose homepage uses Oswald for logo, navigation, and headlines and Lato for body, both "subtle, modern" and matched to a reader who arrives already attention-grabbed by the ad and now needs to trust (Section 3, pp. 26–27; figure, p. 26). Four questions for a font: does it support the reader's journey from how they landed, create the right emotion, match the brand personality, match the reader's expectations (Section 3, p. 27). **Font size**: readable "on all devices" first, consistent second; headlines larger than body for hierarchy and skimming; a rule of thumb puts body text "between 14pt and 18pt", checked with "a modular scale calculator" where "a ratio of 2:3" usually works (Section 3, pp. 27–28; figure, p. 29); older readers get larger print (Section 3, p. 29). **Kerning**: the typeface designer set it; Leadpages and Google Docs do not let a user change letter spacing; the check is that words are easy to read and letters and words evenly spaced (Section 3, p. 29). **Color contrast**: text must be seen "without straining your eyes"; Unbounce's pink hero with dark text is the example of a bold background that stays readable (figure, p. 30); rules of thumb are light on dark or dark on light, "a stark contrast", and an overlay over background images (Section 3, p. 30). **White space**: it "is magical", gives the mind "room to catch up", and is why short paragraphs work; "It sparks joy" (Section 3, p. 31).

Fonts do not "live in a vacuum" and come back to customer research (Section 3, p. 31). Key takeaways: fonts influence interpretation, so the font supports the message; fonts have personality that complements the brand; fonts work in harmony with colors, copy, and images (Section 3, p. 31). The section ends with a sales pitch: "the hidden superpower", selling with emotion, used by "Lego and Apple" and "PooPourri and FiberFix", hard in "the B2B space", tech, and insurance, with an invitation to Talia Wolf's emotional-targeting workshop (Section 3, pp. 31–32).

### Section 4: How image choice affects your copy (and your reader) (pp. 33–40)

Images "create an instant, guttural reaction" because the brain processes them "60,000 times faster than text" (Section 4, p. 33). The evidence offered is Roger Ulrich's 1984 *Science* paper, "View through a window may influence recovery from surgery", in which cholecystectomy patients facing a garden had shorter stays, fewer painkillers, and slightly fewer complications than those facing a brick wall, and a 1993 Uppsala study in which 160 heart-surgery patients randomly assigned one of six views were least anxious with "the open, tree-lined stream" (Section 4, p. 33).

The case study, "Power beyond words", is a downloadable, non-SaaS invitations product that was harder to use and "considerably more expensive" than SaaS competitors, whose landing page already followed the rules of thumb (human faces, matched colors, good quality) and showed a laughing woman, an "It's FREE - download now" button, and a McAfee badge (Section 4, p. 34; figure, p. 34). Customer conversations found the ideal customer, parents planning children's birthday parties, and four emotions: self-image, simplicity, social image, and inspiration/anticipation (Section 4, p. 35). The variation's image shows no people and no product, only a lawn, a picnic basket, and a dog under a headline tying "a great party" to a great invitation, with a three-step "How it works" row (figure, p. 36); it produced a 12% increase in downloads, an 18% increase in registrations, and a 42% increase in revenue (Section 4, p. 36).

Five questions for reviewing an image. Is it attention-grabbing, given "4,000 to 10,000 messages a day" (Section 4, p. 37)? Does it support the copy, the one promise, the offer, and the stage of awareness (Section 4, p. 37)? Is it authentic, so the customer can "see themselves" or the outcome they want in it; Everlane's flash-sale hero of a woman sitting on a wall is the example, with "a slight problem" of headline readability conceded (Section 4, p. 38; figure, p. 38)? Is it distracting; Cisco's homepage carousel of three images with "clashing themes, colors and intent" is the example (Section 4, p. 39; figure, p. 39)? Does it look equally good on all devices; two Drift homepage images whose embedded text is legible on desktop and not on a phone are the example, and it may be worth "leaving them out on mobile" (Section 4, pp. 39–40; figure, p. 40). A final sweep checklist: high contrast with page content, distractions removed, works on all devices, supports headline, body, and CTA copy, feels "real, relatable and authentic", and enhances the copy's emotions (Section 4, p. 40). The section's printed key takeaways are the three typography takeaways from Section 3, repeated word for word (Section 4, p. 40; see section 8).

### Section 5: Making the most of your strategic, persuasive design knowledge (pp. 41–50)

Knowledge alone does not get a copywriter where she wants to go; the aim is to leave "'pay for hire' jobs" for "the world of strategy" through "people wrangling" with clients, their designers, and the marketing department (Section 5, p. 41). Clients hire a copywriter to make or save them money, do not understand the process, and misunderstandings become "a source of conflict", so the copywriter walks the client through the process up front; the client also wants "someone they can trust" who makes them "look good" to the people they answer to (Section 5, p. 42), which moves the relationship from "just another contractor" to "trusted partner" (Section 5, p. 43). Designers matter because "even gorgeous design" that the client loves can lower conversion if it does not match the message, especially when copy is written after design is final; and feedback from outside a field lands on "someone's sense of self worth" (Section 5, p. 43). The remedy is to be the designer's version of a best client: walk them through the wireframe and the research in a meeting rather than sending a brief, so suggestions are visibly research-based rather than telling the designer "how to do their job" (Section 5, p. 44). An expert insight from Alex Harris of Perficient Digital: conversion-focused design "never ends", most companies optimize "only a few times a year", and experimentation "isn't a 'set it and forget task'" (Section 5, p. 45).

Getting buy-in: focus on ROI and the research data behind suggestions (Section 5, p. 46); "appeal to their competitive side" by finding admired companies and direct competitors doing it well and poorly (Section 5, p. 46); "make it about legacy" for founders and executives with "skin in the game", with the caution that it "won't work with everyone" (Section 5, p. 47). Three objections. "That's too much copy": length comes down to what the reader needs to see to convert, governed by stage of awareness, "the more aware the reader" the less copy, illustrated by a five-step awareness chart from little sense of pain (explanation and storytelling) to full belief in the solution (get to the point, "stay out of the way") (Section 5, pp. 47–48; figure, p. 48). "You're just a copywriter": answered with wireframing and customer research, which "is the shield", plus case studies including this book's (Section 5, p. 49). "I just don't like this": get to the bottom of the resistance, then propose an A/B test, which lets the client "save face" by showing both innovation and responsibility; if a full test is not doable, user testing through UserTesting.com or Usability Hub, or "five to 10 people" like the target audience in front of a screen (Section 5, pp. 49–50). Key takeaways: get the designer on board; research is "your rock"; position as a strategic partner from the start (Section 5, p. 50).

### Bonus section: Next steps (p. 51)

The book calls itself "a beginning" and offers two GetUplift downloads: "a complete list of psychological triggers" for optimizing copy at "all stages of awareness", and a cheat sheet for the color section (Bonus section, p. 51). The back cover repeats the title and authors (p. 52).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the authors limit their own claim, admit a counter-case, or say a rule does not apply.

- **No universal rules.** On color there is "no universal truth" and there are "no simple rules" that raise conversion or improve design outright (Section 2, p. 16). Color perception is not the same for everyone and varies by culture, symbolism, and personal experience (Section 2, pp. 16–18).
- **The gender charts.** They are "not definitive" though "a useful starting point" (Section 2, p. 19); "these are not absolute", generalities go only so far, and the final choice is "your reader's preferences and experiences" (Section 2, pp. 18–19).
- **Design is not the whole result.** Conversion is "a holistic process", never "just one element" (Section 1, p. 13). In the color case study the copy is "the one thing that didn't change", and the authors call it "an odd example" for a copywriting book (Section 2, p. 20).
- **The client's sandbox.** A lot of the work is pushing to the edge of what the client is comfortable with while staying "on brand" (Section 1, p. 13); the color variation used "the brand's chosen color" (Section 2, p. 21).
- **Serif and sans serif are tendencies, not rules.** Sans serif for SaaS "may be worth testing"; serif "could serve your copy better" for traditional industries or an older audience (Section 3, p. 25). The copywriter has no need "to know everything about font" (Section 3, p. 25) or to memorize what every font group implies (Section 3, p. 23).
- **Fonts and images do not decide alone.** Typography "doesn't live in a vacuum" (Section 3, p. 31); the image rules of thumb (faces, matched colors, brand mood) out of context "aren't enough", and the invitations client had followed them all (Section 4, p. 34). The winning invitations image shows neither people nor the product (Section 4, p. 36).
- **Kerning is usually not the copywriter's problem.** Most of the time a font will "follow its own rules", and the tools do not expose it, so "your job is pretty easy here" (Section 3, p. 29).
- **Bold backgrounds are allowed.** Text against a bold background is fine, but it "does need to be readable" (Section 3, p. 30). Everlane's hero is praised despite "a slight problem" with headline readability (Section 4, p. 38).
- **Images may be dropped.** If an image does not support the copy on every device, it may be worth "leaving them out on mobile" (Section 4, p. 40).
- **Legacy pitch has limits.** It "won't work with everyone"; it suits clients motivated by being recognized for what they build, founders and committed executives (Section 5, p. 47).
- **Copy length has no ideal.** Conversion copywriting comes with no "ideal length"; length follows the reader's stage of awareness, and "that decision can seem subjective" (Section 5, pp. 47–48).
- **Copy after design.** The copywriter will not always get copy done before design and will sometimes work "within an existing design" (Section 5, p. 44).
- **Testing when a test is not possible.** If a full A/B test "isn't doable", user testing or "five to 10 people" like the target audience stands in (Section 5, pp. 49–50).
- **Scope.** Visual design is treated as if distinct from copy and interaction design only to zero in on "design-specific problems"; in reality nothing persuades until research, observation, synthesis, drafting, and validation are done (Editor's note, p. 15). The book calls itself "a beginning" (Bonus section, p. 51).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, study, tool, or company the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure.

- **Kahneman's System 1 and System 2 (Section 1, pp. 6–7).** The frame is from *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) and is unchanged. The book calls it a loose division and uses it as such.
- **In 2008 researchers could "accurately predict" people's decisions seven seconds ahead with fMRI (Section 1, p. 7).** The study is Soon, Brass, Heinze, and Haynes, "Unconscious determinants of free decisions in the human brain", *Nature Neuroscience* 11 (2008), 543–545 (nature.com/articles/nn.2112). It reports decoding of a left-or-right button choice from prefrontal and parietal activity up to seven seconds, and by one measure ten seconds, before the reported decision, at accuracies the paper puts at about 60% against a 50% chance level; "accurately" in the book overstates it. The Max Planck Society's own summary quotes the authors that the study "does not finally rule out free will" (mpg.de). A later line of work, Schurger and colleagues (2012, 2016), reads the pre-decision build-up as background neural noise crossing a threshold rather than a decision already made (cell.com, "What Is the Readiness Potential?", 2021). The finding stands; its interpretation is contested.
- **Damasio's patients were "unable to make decisions", down to what to have for lunch (Section 1, p. 8).** The source is *Descartes' Error* (1994) and its patient Elliot, who after ventromedial frontal damage deliberated without end: an appointment took thirty minutes, choosing lunch "all afternoon", and which pen to use was "a chore" (psychiatrictimes.com review; blog.idonethis.com, "The Science Behind How Emotions Lead to Smarter Decisions"). Damasio's account is of endless deliberation among a small number of patients, not of a group who "all" could not decide. The direction of the claim holds; the wording is stronger than the source.
- **GetUplift's three case studies: 97% more leads (Section 1, p. 12), 57% more sign-ups and 80% more code installs (Section 2, p. 22), and 12%, 18%, and 42% lifts (Section 4, p. 36).** Unchecked. getuplift.co/case-studies returned an image rather than text when fetched, and no search surfaced these three figures. The invitations client is described in outline in Talia Wolf's Unbounce article "How to Create Landing Pages That Address the Emotional Needs of Prospects" (unbounce.com/landing-pages/landing-pages-emotional-needs) as "a software platform for creating invitations, greeting cards and slideshows" sold as an expensive one-time download, which matches Section 4's description, but that article was not read in full and the figures were not found in it. None of the three clients is named in the book.
- **Copyhackers, *10x Landing Pages*, and the "One Reader" (Section 1, p. 11).** Live: the course sells at copyhackers.com/10x-landing-pages and inside Copy School, listed at 87 lessons; 10xlandingpages.com resolves. Joanna Wiebe remains Copyhackers' CEO (copyhackers.com/academy).
- **GetUplift and the Emotional Targeting Framework (Front matter, p. 2; Section 3, p. 32).** Live at getuplift.co; Talia Wolf published a book titled *Emotional Targeting* (getuplift.co/book, announced for 2025), and a March 2026 interview is titled "Emotional Targeting & CRO Strategies In 2026" (youtube.com). The workshop the book pitches on p. 32 is now taliawolf.com/workshop.
- **Sophia Dagnon (Front matter, p. 2).** Writes for GetUplift and Copyhackers and co-founded Unmassmarket, an email-marketing training business (copyhackers.com/author/sophia; summit.productled.com/speakers/sophia-dagnon).
- **The Ruth Tofle quotation on color theories being "strongly normative and weakly analytic" (Section 2, p. 16).** The source is Tofle, Schwarz, Yoon, and Max-Royale, *Color in Healthcare Environments: A Critical Review of the Research Literature* (Center for Health Design and Coalition for Health Environments Research, 2004), live at healthdesign.org. The report's thesis matches the book's use: specifying colors "to evoke a particular mood or influence behavior is unsubstantiated by research evidence" (as quoted in a later EDRA paper, brikbase.org, EDRA36-Schwarz). The exact wording of the quotation was not located in the fetched text and is unchecked; the book gives no page or title for it.
- **Information is Beautiful, "Colours in Culture" (Section 2, p. 17).** Live at informationisbeautiful.net/visualizations/colours-in-cultures, by David McCandless with AlwaysWithHonor, page copyright 2026.
- **"Joe Hollack" on color preference by gender (Section 2, pp. 18–19).** The name is Joe Hallock, and the study is his 2003 "Colour Assignment" survey of 232 people from 22 countries, still published at joehallock.com/edu/COM498. The figures hold: 57% of men and 35% of women named blue, 23% of women named purple, and purple was near the top of men's least favorites. The Help Scout article the book charts come from (helpscout.com/blog/psychology-of-color) is live, dated July 30, 2025, and still cites Hallock, but the fetched text no longer shows the donut charts or the percentages; the article now says "most of his respondents were from Western societies".
- **Sarah Hyndman's jelly beans, "17% sweeter" and "11% sourer" (Section 3, p. 24).** Holds on Hyndman's own account: 100 people at a V&A talk during London Design Festival, reported in *Why Fonts Matter* (2016) and on her Medium page (medium.com/@shyndman/about; sambrinson.com, "Which Font Tastes Best?"). The book cites a student's WordPress post rather than Hyndman.
- **Lume's homepage, Oswald headlines over Lato body (Section 3, pp. 26–27).** Unchecked. A fetched page's text does not carry its font stack, and no source describing Lume's 2026 typography was found.
- **Body text "between 14pt and 18pt" (Section 3, p. 28).** The book gives no source, and none was found; unchecked as a rule of thumb.
- **The modular scale calculator at "a ratio of 2:3" (Section 3, p. 28; figure, p. 29).** Live at modularscale.com, built and maintained by Scott Kellum and Tim Brown, introduced in 2010 and relaunched in 2015; 2:3 is listed as "perfect fifth, 1.5" among sixteen preset ratios.
- **Leadpages and Google Docs offer no letter-spacing control (Section 3, p. 29).** Google Docs still has no native letter-spacing setting; 2025–2026 how-to guides describe add-ons and font swaps as the workaround (apexgear.blog; macmyths.com). Leadpages is unchecked.
- **Unbounce's pink hero, "Convert More Customers" (Section 3, p. 30; figure).** Changed. The hero now reads "Launch faster. Convert more." with the button "Start building for free" (unbounce.com, fetched September 2026).
- **The brain processes images "60,000 times faster than text" (Section 4, p. 33).** No research supports it. The figure traces to 3M promotional material of the 1990s and a 2001 brochure citing unnamed "behavioral research", and the University of Minnesota researcher whose 3M-funded work is sometimes cited has said it has no connection to the claim (policyviz.com, "The 60000 Fallacy", 2015; blog.learnlets.com, 2020; photutorial.com). See section 8.
- **Ulrich's 1984 *Science* paper and the 1993 Uppsala study of 160 heart-surgery patients (Section 4, p. 33).** Both hold. "View through a window may influence recovery from surgery" is *Science* 224 (1984), 420–421. The 1993 work, "Effects of exposure to nature and abstract pictures on patients recovering from heart surgery", randomized 160 intensive-care patients at Uppsala University Hospital to six simulated views and was presented to the Society for Psychophysiological Research with an abstract in *Psychophysiology* (healthdesign.org knowledge repository; scientificamerican.com, "Nature that Nurtures"). The tree-lined stream group was less anxious and needed fewer strong analgesics; the dark forest and abstract art did not help.
- **"4,000 to 10,000 messages a day" (Section 4, p. 37).** Unsubstantiated. The 5,000-a-day figure is usually attributed to Yankelovich's J. Walker Smith, who told *The Drum* he never said it; the American Association of Advertising Agencies found the estimates "unsubstantiated" in 2007; and the 4,000–10,000 range is traced to a Red Crow Marketing blog post with no study behind it (thedrum.com, "How many ads do we really see in a day? Spoiler: it's not 10,000"; adrenalinex.com, "Busting the 5,000 Myth").
- **Everlane's flash-sale hero and Cisco's three-image homepage (Section 4, pp. 38–39).** Unchecked as live pages: both were 2019 homepages and were not re-fetched.
- **Drift's homepage images (Section 4, pp. 39–40).** Historical. Salesloft acquired Drift on February 14, 2024 (salesloft.com newsroom; businesswire.com), and the Drift site of 2019 no longer exists as such.
- **Alex Harris, "CRO director at Perficient Digital" (Section 5, p. 45).** Still listed as Conversion Rate Optimization Director at Perficient (crunchbase.com/person/alex-harris-5; linkedin.com/in/alexdesigns); the "Perficient Digital" name has folded into Perficient.
- **UserTesting.com and Usability Hub (Section 5, p. 50).** UsabilityHub renamed itself Lyssna in October 2023 (lyssna.com/blog/usabilityhub-is-now-lyssna; prnewswire.com). UserTesting is unchecked: the session's search budget ran out before its current ownership could be confirmed.
- **The two downloads in the Bonus section (p. 51).** Unchecked: the text extraction carries the link text "Grab your copy here" and "Get more details about it here" with no URL, and the PDF was not opened for them.
- **Not checked:** Yves Klein's blue (Section 2, p. 16), Lego, Apple, PooPourri, and FiberFix as emotional sellers (Section 3, p. 31), and the cultural color meanings on p. 17, which are the Information is Beautiful chart's own claims and carry no further source in the book.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Correlation presented as cause.** Each of the three case studies is one before-and-after pair with no sample size, test duration, confidence level, or client name (Section 1, p. 12; Section 2, p. 22; Section 4, p. 36). Two of them changed headline, form, image, and layout at once and attribute the lift to the emotional design (Section 1, pp. 10–12; Section 4, pp. 34–36); only the color case isolates one variable, and even there the variation added a "Trusted by" logo row alongside the color change (Section 2, pp. 21–22), so "one color change" is not what the figure shows.
- **A false figure and an unsourced one.** "60,000 times faster than text" (Section 4, p. 33) has no research behind it; section 7 traces it to a 3M brochure. "4,000 to 10,000 messages a day" (Section 4, p. 37) has no study behind it either. "Between 14pt and 18pt" (Section 3, p. 28) is stated without a source.
- **Overstated science.** The 2008 fMRI study is said to "accurately predict" decisions (Section 1, p. 7) when its accuracy was about 60% against chance of 50%, and the book does not mention that its interpretation is disputed. Damasio's patients are said to be "unable to make decisions" (Section 1, p. 8) where the source describes endless deliberation. Both point the way the book wants; neither is as clean as printed.
- **A misspelled source.** The color-preference study is attributed to "Joe Hollack" (Section 2, p. 18); the researcher is Joe Hallock, and the study is a 2003 survey of 232 people, which the book does not say.
- **Examples from the authors' own portfolio.** All three case studies are GetUplift's, the co-author is GetUplift's founder, the lead author writes for GetUplift, and the book ends by selling GetUplift's workshop and downloads (Section 3, p. 32; Bonus section, p. 51). No case is from outside the consultancy, and no case that did not lift is reported.
- **An editing defect.** Section 4's "Key takeaways" (Section 4, p. 40) are Section 3's three typography takeaways (Section 3, p. 31) reprinted word for word, so the image section has no takeaways of its own.
- **Internal tension.** Color has "no universal truth" and "no simple rules" (Section 2, p. 16), and then gender-preference charts are offered as the starting point for a hypothesis (Section 2, pp. 18–19). Fonts depend on the reader (Section 3, p. 27), and then for "a SaaS company" a sans serif is worth testing as a category rule (Section 3, p. 25).
- **Evidence that does not reach the claim.** The hospital studies concern a real window and a large nature photograph in an intensive-care unit (Section 4, p. 33) and are used to argue for a marketing image's effect on a reader. The jelly-bean experiment measures perceived taste (Section 3, p. 24) and is used to argue for trust in the copy that follows a font. Neither transfer is shown.
- **Dated examples.** The Unbounce, Drift, Cisco, Everlane, and Lume pages (Section 3, pp. 26, 30; Section 4, pp. 38–40) were 2019 homepages; section 7 finds Unbounce changed, Drift absorbed, and the rest unchecked. Two of the figure sources are a student's blog and a design tool's support page rather than a primary source (Section 3, pp. 24, 26, 29).
- **Thin on accessibility.** Contrast is judged by "without straining your eyes" with no ratio and no standard (Section 3, p. 30), and the only note on readers who differ is that older readers want larger print (Section 3, p. 29). Nothing on screen readers, on text in images, or on motion beyond a distracting background.
- **The advice on people is the least evidenced.** Section 5's account of how clients and designers think, and the three buy-in tactics, rest on the authors' experience with no case beyond an interview quotation (Section 5, pp. 42–47).
