## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that a working software developer with no outside funding can launch a profitable product by inverting the order most developers use: find a small online niche market first, measure its demand with free keyword tools before writing code, price for that niche, build a sales website whose first job is to collect an email address, and then outsource or automate everything that does not need the founder (Preface, PDF 9–10; Ch. 1, p. 5; Ch. 2, p. 39). The author calls this the Market First Approach and the umbrella method the Micropreneur Methodology (Ch. 2, pp. 39, 55). He splits his readers into two types: Micropreneurs, who want to stay solo, with "no employees under any circumstance", and bootstrappers, who intend to grow and hire (Ch. 1, pp. 1–2). His stated track record is ten profitable software products and websites built or acquired over ten years, and a paid online community, the Micropreneur Academy, run with Mike Taber (About the Author, PDF 11).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a guide for venture-backed startups: it deliberately skips the investor pitch, securing funding, and what to do with investor cash, and assumes the reader has no $6M in the bank, cannot relocate to a startup hub, and will not work 70-hour weeks for stock options (Preface, PDF 9). It is not a book about writing software, because it assumes an experienced developer and does not cover the development process (What Does This Book Cover?, PDF 10). It is not anti-venture: he says there is "nothing wrong with" pursuing venture funding and that most people are simply not positioned to (Preface, PDF 9). It is not a promise of a big payday — a million-dollar exit is "most likely not in your future" (Ch. 1, p. 8) — and it is not a book to be read passively: the author sets the test that the reader should be underlining and taking notes (What Does This Book Cover?, PDF 10), while separately calling reading business books a distraction that gets a founder no closer to launch (Ch. 1, p. 21).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Front matter (PDF 9–11)

The Preface names the audience and the exclusions, and lists what the book will cover: the mindset of a self-funded startup, the Market-First Approach, finding and testing a niche, choosing platform, price and revenue model, building a sales website, driving the right traffic, outsourcing, virtual assistants, and what to do after launch (Preface, PDF 9–10). The author says he cut what early readers called filler — weak case studies, superficial interviews, and chapters that list ten options without saying which to pick (What Does This Book Cover?, PDF 10). "About the Author" offers the evidence for listening to him: ten profitable products and websites over ten years, a blog at www.SoftwareByRob.com, and the Micropreneur Academy, a paid community of several hundred members (PDF 11).

### Chapter 1: The Chasm Between Developer and Entrepreneur (pp. 1–38)

An entrepreneur is defined in three points: a technical visionary who builds for a niche market, someone who merges technical knowledge with online marketing knowledge, and a cross between a developer, a webmaster and a marketer (Ch. 1, pp. 3–4). Niches are the reason: they are too small for large competitors and leave a nimble founder room in an underserved audience (p. 3). The chapter's ordering claim is that marketing matters more than the product, expressed as putting product last and marketing first (p. 5). Self-funding is contrasted with venture funding — Netscape, Google, Travelocity, Yelp, Twitter and Facebook on one side; Joel Spolsky's Fog Creek Software and Eric Sink's SourceGear on the other — with the self-funded founder offered "a decent potential for a decent payoff" (pp. 5–6). Two live examples: Ruben Gamez, who launched Bidsketch in 2009 while keeping a development-manager job, and Harry Hollander, whose Moraware sells scheduling software to countertop installers and employed four people beyond the two founders after eight years (pp. 6–7).

Three wrong reasons to start: having a product idea, which the author calls "project/product confusion" and defines against a product as "a project that has a market" (p. 7); to get rich, since a lottery ticket or a venture-backed startup is the better bet for a million dollars (pp. 7–8); and because it sounds like fun, since writing code is about 30% of the work and the other 70% is debugging, installers, documentation, the sales website, the merchant account, advertising, support and a hundred other things (pp. 8–9). Goals are the antidote, and the evidence offered is a Dominican University study summarized by Gail Matthews, which the author reads as three factors — written goals, public commitment, and accountability through weekly progress reports (p. 11). He proposes one short-term goal, a startup generating $500 per month in profit (p. 12), and describes "The Dip": a worked scenario of a $25 product selling 100 copies a month against a $60k salary, where support runs 5–10 hours a week, the customer base grows from 100 to 1,200 in a year, and the product withers or ends up on Flippa (pp. 13–15).

Five roadblocks and their fixes: no market, answered by verifying demand before writing code (p. 18); fear, which the author says wears off quickly (pp. 18–19); lack of goals (pp. 19–20); inconsistency, where research and reading business books feel productive and are not, answered by an "information diet" (pp. 20–22); and believing you have to do everything yourself, answered by "drip outsourcing" — sending small tasks to a virtual assistant at about $6/hour overseas or $10–20/hour in the United States, which the author says saves him 60–100 hours a month (pp. 22–25). He lists 37 tasks between idea and the first week after launch, marking the ones easiest to outsource, and says to outsource graphic design and HTML every time (pp. 23–24).

"Dollarizing" your time is the foundational habit: set an hourly value from a freelance rate or from salary plus about 30% benefits divided by 2,000 hours, lean toward the higher freelance figure, and treat $100/hour as the long-term target against a reality nearer $25/hour for one-person software companies (pp. 26–28). Four realizations follow: outsourcing is a bargain; keep work and play separate; wasting time is expensive, so carry a notebook; and information consumption is only worth it when it produces "action notes" (pp. 29–32). Nine further realizations close the chapter: being a good technician is not enough, on Gerber's entrepreneur/manager/technician archetypes; market first, marketing second, aesthetic third, and functionality a distant fourth; things will never be as clear as a developer wants; you cannot specify everything but you do need a plan, and waterfall will be a disaster after launch; fail fast and recover; you will never be done; do not expect instant gratification, since the first month may not break $100 in revenue; process is king; and nothing about a startup is a one-time effort (pp. 33–38).

### Chapter 2: Why Niches Are the Name of the Game (pp. 39–90)

The single most important factor in a product's success is not the founder, the marketing or the product, but whether a group of people is willing to pay for it (Ch. 2, p. 39). Luck is named as the exception — Hot or Not, Plenty of Fish, Facebook apps — and put at roughly 1 in 10,000, a bet the author calls mathematically poor (pp. 40–41). Six reasons to go niche: a niche narrows the product; niche advertising is cheaper, with a full-page ad in a travel magazine at $106,000 against $2,897 in a regional one, and "travel" in AdWords at about $1.75 a click against about $1.25 for a state-level variant; niches have less competition, the example being Nota Bene, an academic word processor at $399 against $79 for Microsoft Office Home 2007; niches have higher margins; niche markets are not used to good marketing; and prospects trust you faster because they hear of you more than once (pp. 45–50). The choice offered is 100% of a 5,000-person niche over 10% of a 50,000-person one (p. 46).

Finding a niche starts with "warm niches" — an industry or hobby the founder has an association with, listed in a two-column exercise of people and their work or hobby (pp. 50–51). Cold sources follow: occupation lists, business-idea sites, and a list of idea blogs (pp. 52–53). Two questions decide a niche. Is the market large enough? The rule of thumb is a market with a magazine devoted to it whose full-page ad costs under $5,000, checked against rate cards, circulation, Compete.com traffic and labor statistics; the worked example is marriage and family therapists, 23,240 of them in the United States as of April 2009, and the caution is a market with no dedicated publication and fewer than 10,000 members (pp. 55–60). Is there an inexpensive way to reach them? Digg and TechCrunch traffic is not a market — more than 90% of the author's Digg traffic stayed under five seconds, and 50,000 visitors from a major media site might yield five sales (pp. 60–61). Vertical markets beat horizontal ones on four counts: similar behavior, members who talk to one another, members who gather in the same places, and similar needs (pp. 62–64).

Demand is then measured with math. Conversion rate is defined as the share of visitors who buy, with 1–4% for consumer products at $1–50 and 0.5–2% above $50 or on recurring pricing; the author's own numbers are just under 3% for a $2 service, about 2.5% for an e-commerce site with a $20 average order, and 0.5–0.7% for DotNetInvoice at $329 (pp. 66–68). Traffic splits roughly into thirds between search engines, incoming links and direct traffic, with advertising on top; ranking may take 12–18 months, which he notes is the disputed Google Sandbox (pp. 69–70). The Micropreneur Methodology for measuring demand uses the Google AdWords Keyword Tool on exact match, halved because the tool overestimates, then multiplied by four; the worked example is "attorney billing software" at 1,900 global monthly searches becoming about 3,800 visitors and, at 0.2% and a $500 price, $3,800 a month (pp. 78–79). Keyword difficulty is bucketed into free tools such as SEO Logs, a $97 desktop tool, Micro Niche Finder, and SEOmoz at $79–229 a month, followed by a manual sanity check of PageRank, page source, domain age and backlinks (pp. 73–81). The final test before building is the "Mini Sales Site": two or three pages, an AdWords campaign, and a tracked click on "buy now" or "download a free trial" that leads to a launch-notification signup, for $50–500; the author credits *The 4-Hour Workweek* for the idea and notes the Lean Startup method arrived at something similar (pp. 82–86). It does not work for network-effect products (p. 86), and the worked case study is a college coach finder whose design and HTML cost under $300 (pp. 87–89).

### Chapter 3: Your Product (pp. 91–111)

The Product Success Triangle makes the product one third of the equation, beside market and execution, and the author says a mediocre product with brilliant marketing and execution will make money while a brilliant product without either is dead (Ch. 3, p. 91). His estimate for building it yourself is 200–400 hours to version 1.0 by a page-count method, plus 100–200 hours for the sales website and everything else, which at 15 hours a week of spare time is four and a half to eight and a half months (pp. 92–93). Hiring it out is offered as the middle ground at roughly $6.5k–7.5k offshore against $14k–26k in the United States, and the author says this is the last time he will raise it (pp. 94–96).

Pricing gets explicit ceilings, which the author says none of the 25 articles he read would give: about $29 or $14/month for consumer hobby products, about $49 or $19/month where a consumer saves money, about $400 or $99/month for small businesses, and about $1,000 or $199/month for larger ones, each qualified by "unless you solve a serious pain" and limited to online sales with no salesperson (p. 97). A twelve-step process follows, by analogy with a home appraisal: know the market, ask what you would pay, look at competitors, compute the value delivered as a ceiling, combine into a range whose top is four times its bottom, lean high, use three tiers doubling each time, end every price in 7, 8 or 9, make each tier more than double the previous benefit, add an annual support fee of about 20% of a one-time price, divide a fixed price by 12 for the top recurring tier, and test (pp. 98–100). Price testing depends on the sales cycle: under a week, change it silently for one or two weeks; longer, raise it with a new version and offer the old price to the mailing list for seven days, or cut it and watch revenue, refunding the difference to anyone who writes in (pp. 100–101).

Six product types close the chapter, each with a pricing structure, benefits to the founder and the customer, downsides and a "when to use": hosted web applications, recommended as the default starting position for business software, on a recurring fee, with Joel Spolsky quoted as saying he would not build a downloadable product today (pp. 101–103); downloadable web applications, one-time fee plus about 20% annual support, for customers who must keep their data (pp. 103–105); desktop applications, for complex interfaces or local hardware access (pp. 105–106); mobile applications, then a land grab, with 10,000 apps in the iPhone store's first four months and a projection of 100 million mobile app users by 2013 (pp. 107–108); third-party plug-ins on platforms such as Facebook, then over 500 million active users, MySpace at over 125 million and Salesforce at over 1.5 million paying subscribers, with the warning that the platform vendor may absorb your feature (pp. 108–110); and community websites, which he says are not typical for this audience and are viable mainly at millions of page views (pp. 110–111).

### Chapter 4: Building a Killer Sales Website (pp. 113–144)

The sales funnel runs URL, visitor, prospect, buyer, and most of the group is lost at every step (Ch. 4, pp. 113–114). The arithmetic offered: at 1,000 visitors and 1% conversion, adding one sale a month means either 100 more visitors or 0.1 percentage points of conversion, and the second is easier (p. 115). The first rule he gives is that a founder should not plan to sell on a first visit, because the sites that manage it — SourceGear Vault, FogBugz, Basecamp — already have an audience (pp. 116–117). So the number one goal of the website, ahead of selling, is turning browsers into prospects by collecting an email address, which needs trust, relevance and a reward; the author's own example is a DotNetInvoice signup form that drew 3 signups in 8 months until it offered a chance to win a free copy, which drew 30 in a month (pp. 118–119). Customer profiles come next, as a list of questions about the ideal customer's life, what they want on arrival, and what keeps them awake at night (pp. 119–120).

Every page gets a single primary call to action, and the home page is not a collection of links (pp. 120–123). Seven rules of sales-site design: the home page will not necessarily be the most visited page; every page needs a call to action; every page needs a single focus; everything within two clicks including the submit button; accommodate skimmers with headlines and bulleted lists and keep paragraphs unnaturally short; make buttons look like buttons; and the seventh says nobody reads, so audio, video and images beat text (pp. 124–125). Five core pages are prescribed — Home, Tour, Testimonials, Contact Us, Pricing & Purchase. The home page's key metric is abandonment rate, which the author cites *Web Design for ROI* as putting at 40–60% before optimization (p. 125). The hook is a five-to-seven-word headline that says what the product does and for whom, or promises a benefit, or names the single most remarkable feature; the examples are DotNetInvoice, FogBugz, Basecamp, Bidsketch and the iPod (pp. 128–129). The Tour page gets medium screen shots filled with data and a one-minute screencast each (pp. 129–130). Testimonials are called one of the most important pages, and the author says not to launch without them (p. 130). Contact Us gets a form, a separate email address, a toll-free number even if it goes to voice mail, and a physical address, for which he suggests a mail-scanning service at about $20 a month (pp. 130–131). The pricing page gets large type, few words, a highlighted plan, an upsell, and, failing a free trial, a 30-day no-questions money-back guarantee (pp. 131–133).

The second half of the chapter is the mailing list. Email is called the most pervasive online tool, on a 2007 study putting personal email accounts at 88% of United States adults (p. 133). The list is built double opt-in and targeted, by giving something away — a 5–15 page report — or by offering a free five-day email course, tested against each other a month at a time (pp. 135–137). List management is never self-hosted, because deliverability is the priority and third parties staff it full time; the recommended provider is MailChimp, at a free plan, about $.03 per email, or $10/month for 500 subscribers and $30/month for 2,500 (pp. 137–138). Content is either relevant information every 2–4 weeks, ideally as an autoresponder series that new subscribers receive from the beginning, or product updates where the market is horizontal and there is little to say (pp. 138–140). Seven sending tips follow: test send times, with Tuesday through Thursday between 7am and 10:30am as the general rule; always send plain text; never send attachments; mind the "from" name; write the subject in seven words or fewer and watch the spam score; give each email one goal, with a sales message every fourth or fifth; and test everything (pp. 141–143). "The Ideal Launch" closes the chapter: drive traffic to a home page six months out that offers a deal for an email address, build buzz for six months, email the list a week before launch promising a 48-hour price, email again on launch day, and send a final 12-hour reminder 36 hours in; conversion on a targeted mailing can be 20%+, and without something like it the author says a founder will be lucky to sell ten licenses in the first week (pp. 143–144).

### Chapter 5: Startup Marketing (pp. 145–164)

Social media is demoted first: the companies profiting from Twitter have tens of thousands of subscribers built over years, none of it is a shortcut, and Twitter is unlikely to ever be a main sales channel (Ch. 5, pp. 145–146). Marketing is not one size fits all — many approaches will not work in a given market, and which ones do changes with the niche (p. 146). Traffic quality is defined as how close a visitor is to the ideal customer and how much relationship exists, which is why a mailing list can convert as much as 10x standard website traffic and why untargeted traffic is worth little; the instruction is to measure every source with analytics goals and stop pursuing the ones that do not convert (pp. 147–148).

Traffic strategies are then sorted into two shelves. Top Shelf, which can sustain a business: a mailing list, a blog or podcast or video blog, and organic search. Second Shelf, supplemental: social media, pay-per-click, forums, press releases, guest blogging, affiliate programs, banner advertising and everything else (pp. 148–149). The author declines to cover the Second Shelf at all, on the grounds that it is unbounded, time-intensive for the return, and varies too much by market (p. 149). Pay-per-click gets a specific role: use it to find keywords that convert, then rank organically for them, rather than as a long-term channel (p. 150).

Each Top Shelf strategy is then worked. The mailing list is called the most effective marketing tool a founder will have, and the best revenue day is the day a time-limited promotion cashes in some of that list equity (p. 151). Blogging serves two audiences, readers and search engines, and the chapter argues a blog has no SEO advantage from the software, only from what blogs tend to be: updated, large, page-per-theme, and clearly linked (pp. 151–154). Podcasting and video blogging reach smaller audiences with weaker competition and a stronger bond, at 30 minutes of podcast for about 90 minutes of preparation plus 2–3 hours of editing to outsource (pp. 154–156). Organic search is split into on-page factors, which the author calls the simple part and refuses to cover, and incoming links, which he calls the hard part (pp. 156–157). Four link-building guidelines: not all links are equal, favoring aged, high-PageRank, few-outbound, topically relevant pages; vary link text, with about half on the main term; build links over time rather than in a burst; and stay out of "bad neighborhoods", with reciprocal links called dead (pp. 158–159). Eight approaches to build them: general directories such as DMOZ, JoeAnt and Gimpsy; niche and local directories; competitors' backlinks found through Yahoo! Site Explorer; Google Alerts; publishing 500–700 word articles to EzineArticles, GoArticles and iSnare, hired on Elance for $3–8 each; offering a free academic or non-profit version in exchange for a link; giving testimonials in exchange for links; and buying links, either rented monthly through Text Link Ads or bought permanently at $5–20 each, which the author flags as controversial and notes Google will penalize (pp. 159–163).

### Chapter 6: Virtual Assistants and Outsourcing (pp. 165–179)

A virtual assistant is any remote contract worker, and the value proposition is arithmetic: time monetized at $50/hour against a VA at $6/hour makes doing the task yourself a poor use of an entrepreneur's time (Ch. 6, pp. 165–166). The chapter's central case study is a hosted version of DotNetInvoice estimated at 160 hours, which fell to about 10 by dropping multi-tenancy, dropping automated signup and provisioning, and using PayPal subscriptions, with a VA creating each hosted account by hand; the author says he will automate it on the day the VA bill for that task exceeds a threshold (pp. 166–168). The lesson generalized: what can be left unautomated before launch — reminder emails, monthly billing — because an hour spent writing code is wasted if a person can do the same task until the product proves itself (pp. 168–169). Manual scaffolding is also said to reduce development time by 50–80%, and the written process becomes the blueprint for the eventual automation (p. 170).

Two points in a startup's life where a VA helps most: proving out the product, and after launch, when support, sales questions, live chat, directory submissions, HTML tweaks, keyword research, link building, follow-up on cancellations and month-end reports pile up (pp. 169–171). Three case studies: a VA finding 28 startup blogs with contact details for $12; an e-commerce site whose four drop-shippers had no API, where the author judged a screen scraper not worth 50+ hours and never revisited it; and a niche job board kept populated for $60–100 a month, which he says would have been impossible to prove out in code (pp. 171–173). Hiring is described in three arrangements with rates — task-based at $3–10/hour overseas and $12–50 in the United States, part-time at $2–7 and $10–40, and full-time at $1–5 and $8–35, the last needing 160+ hours of work to fill (pp. 173–174). The author says he has had his best results in the Philippines, gives his reasons, and names oDesk as his current favorite, with Elance and Google searches as alternatives (pp. 174–176). Screening is deliberately light: reliability cannot be tested before hiring, so his test is an exchange of a few emails with three to five candidates at once, rather than a request for writing samples, which can be edited or borrowed (pp. 176–177). For the first task he prescribes a backup first, numbered steps written for a non-technical reader, screenshots or screencasts, and a timebox, on the assumption the VA will take several times longer at first (pp. 177–179).

### Chapter 7: Grow It or Start Over (pp. 181–200)

After launch a founder faces two paths: grow the product into a business, or find closure and start over (Ch. 7, p. 181). Growing means crossing Geoffrey Moore's chasm, and the chapter's answer for learning is peers rather than books — a list of conferences, meetups, blogs, online communities and podcasts, including Business of Software, Meetup.com, Patrick McKenzie's and Jason Cohen's blogs, Eric Ries on lean startups, the Micropreneur Academy, Mixergy and the author's own podcast with Mike Taber (pp. 182–184).

Starting over means either automating the product or selling it, and the author recommends automating first (p. 185). Serial Micropreneurship is the name he gives to running several small products one after another, keeping each on autopilot rather than selling it, which he distinguishes from a parallel effort he calls unworkable (pp. 185–186). Six advantages: the ability to take niches worth $500–2000 a month that nobody else will attack; income diversification against a single product's failure or a search-algorithm change; attention diversification, which lets a founder take riskier bets; economies of scale, where the second setup takes about a third the time; the ability to keep doing the parts you enjoy; and transferable experience (pp. 187–189). Two pitfalls: task switching is genuinely inefficient, and outsourcing and automation are non-negotiable prerequisites (pp. 189–190). The formula is build or buy, launch or revamp, grow to the natural plateau, outsource and automate ruthlessly, repeat (p. 190). Maintenance falls into support, where his rule is that every request triggers the question of how never to receive it again and where front-line email can go to a domestic VA, new features, which may be deferred at the plateau, and marketing, which he argues is the easiest to put on autopilot because conversion holds and PPC, SEO and incoming links keep running (pp. 191–193).

Selling gets the deflating numbers. Without profit, an exit above a few thousand dollars is remote (p. 194). Websites sell for 6–24 months of net profit with 12 a reasonable start; parked domains can reach 24–36 and maintenance-heavy sites 2–3; software products run 6–15 months because the buyer pool is limited to developers who know the language (pp. 194–195). Startups are "much better bought than sold", because an approach signals a specific desire that beats a profit multiple (p. 195). Since valuation tracks profit and automation rather than features, traffic or design, the exit-strategy tactics and the day-to-day goals point the same way (p. 196). The metrics to track are traffic, revenue and expenses computed monthly, the product and business names through Google Alerts, every hour spent broken into development, support and marketing, hosting kept separate from other sites, and licensing kept clean (pp. 197–198). The worked case is a theming service bought for under 2x monthly profit, paid off in four months, and sold nine months later at a gain because the hard part had been outsourced and the hours had been tracked, so the seller was offering a business rather than "a job like the previous owner" (p. 198). Selling process, as he lays it out: the professional network and LinkedIn first, a price range named, an NDA required above $10k, a PDF sales kit with a fixed list of contents, escrow above $1,000, no rush, and an auction on Flippa as the fallback (pp. 199–200).

### The Postlude (pp. 201–202)

The author repeats his test for the book, invites email, and points at www.StartupBook.net and at the Micropreneur Academy, whose first month is offered free to owners of the book (Postlude, pp. 201–202).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** The book is for a developer launching without outside funding, and says there is "nothing wrong with" the venture path, only that most readers are not positioned for it (Preface, PDF 9). It does not cover the development process, because it assumes an experienced developer (What Does This Book Cover?, PDF 10).
- **Luck.** The whole market-first argument is suspended for the lucky case: where an idea goes viral the market does not matter, and the author puts that at roughly 1 in 10,000 (Ch. 2, pp. 40–41). He concedes the emotional case for the venture path — excitement, passion and pride — while calling the bet poor (p. 41).
- **Marketing beats product, but only so far.** The product still "has to be good", or the business fails (Ch. 1, p. 5). Selling something poor to a hungry market makes money and is explicitly "not a sustainable strategy" (Ch. 2, p. 40). The aesthetic-over-functionality ordering is suspended when the market is other developers (Ch. 1, p. 34).
- **Magazine advertising.** The sub-$5,000 full-page ad is a sizing rule, not a recommendation: the author says twice that he does not advise magazine advertising, because prospects are hard to track, the content cannot be changed mid-run, and readers rarely switch media (Ch. 2, pp. 47, 57).
- **The magazine test does not run backwards.** A niche with no magazine devoted to it is "not necessarily too small" (Ch. 2, p. 58). Labor statistics are a sanity check on the finding, not the finding (pp. 58–59).
- **Warm niches are preferred, not required.** A cold niche can be entered by working your network to warm it (Ch. 2, p. 51), though he calls the chances without an insider dubious at best.
- **Horizontal markets.** Not forbidden, only discouraged; the advice is to look again at your warm niches first, with horizontals described as generally too large and expensive to navigate (Ch. 2, pp. 64–65).
- **Keyword tools.** They are "flawed" and nothing more than estimates from extrapolated data, which is why the chapter adds the AdWords test and the Mini Sales Site (Ch. 2, p. 83). The free difficulty tools are to be "taken with a grain of salt", and most of the free ones are called junk (pp. 75–76). Search queries are only one way to gauge demand, and a survey of an existing audience is better where one exists (p. 71). Even after all of it, the author says marketing is fuzzy and imprecise and there is a leap of faith at the end (p. 51).
- **The Mini Sales Site.** It does not work for social networks, social media, or anything resting on a network effect, which cannot be tested without a full marketing effort (Ch. 2, p. 86). It also cannot give 100% certainty either way (p. 90). The author raises the ethical objection himself and answers it with a "Coming soon" emblem (p. 85).
- **Building it yourself.** Hiring out is presented as the better odds, and then qualified: with no money and plenty of time, building your own product is "a great option" (Ch. 3, p. 94). The author says this is the last time he will raise hiring out, and accepts most readers will not do it (p. 96).
- **Under 200 hours.** A short build is "a good sign" and also a warning, because it may mean a tool rather than an application, and tools are hard to charge for (Ch. 3, p. 93).
- **Price ceilings.** Every band is a ballpark that varies considerably by market, is qualified by "unless you solve a serious pain", and applies only to online sales without a salesperson; firms with sales staff can charge much more (Ch. 3, p. 97).
- **Three tiers.** The reason for tiers is the author's own admission that there is no single best price for a product (Ch. 3, p. 99). The ending-in-7-8-or-9 rule is introduced as "stupid" and kept because it works (p. 99).
- **Price testing.** The technique depends on the sales cycle, and the longer-cycle variants exist because a silent change risks existing customers finding out; a price cut comes with an offer to refund the difference to anyone who writes in (Ch. 3, pp. 100–101).
- **Hosted applications.** The default is reversed into a list of four cases where a hosted application is the wrong shape: a platform-specific community, enterprise clients who will not let data leave, interfaces the web cannot support, and applications needing hard-drive or peripheral access (Ch. 3, p. 103). The same four reappear as the case for desktop and downloadable builds (pp. 104–106).
- **Two clicks.** The everything-within-two-clicks rule "might not hold 100% true" for larger companies or multi-product sites (Ch. 4, p. 124).
- **First-visit sales.** The rule against expecting them is explicitly contradicted by SourceGear Vault, FogBugz and Basecamp, and the author's answer is that those sites already have a returning audience (Ch. 4, p. 116).
- **Blogs over mailing lists.** In technical markets the ordering flips, because developers avoid inbox clutter and prefer feeds; for everyone else email is called the better channel (Ch. 5, p. 152).
- **Blogging for an audience.** Not for everyone, being time-consuming and demanding original writing; blogging for search engines alone is offered as the lower-effort alternative and still called valuable (Ch. 5, p. 154).
- **A blog has no inherent SEO advantage.** The author answers his own question both ways, and says a ten-page static site and a ten-page blog that never changes look the same to Google (Ch. 5, pp. 153–154).
- **HTML email.** Whether to bother "depends" on the audience — designers and marketers yes, technical readers and lay-people probably not — and plain text is always sent regardless (Ch. 4, pp. 141–142).
- **Buying links.** Presented with its own penalty attached: Google will penalize or drop a site found buying or selling links, and the author calls the approach controversial and questionable to some (Ch. 5, p. 163).
- **Directories.** DMOZ's importance is described as already diminished, and directories in general as having lost a lot of impact as algorithms improved (Ch. 5, pp. 159–160). Automated submission packages are called not worth $5 (p. 160).
- **Article repositories.** The repository pages themselves do not typically rank, because the articles are reprinted everywhere and Google discounts duplicate content; the benefit claimed is the reprints and the links (Ch. 5, p. 161).
- **Outsourcing.** A learned skill the reader is likely to get wrong the first time, so the book starts the reader on non-critical tasks with very specific instructions (Ch. 1, p. 25). Product architecture is outsourced "only for small applications" (p. 24). Marketing is named as one of the hardest things to outsource, and support and feature development the easiest (p. 38). Finding a VA is trial and error — the author worked through more than six (Ch. 6, p. 179) — and a VA will never be as fast as the founder (p. 178).
- **Solo versus team VAs.** Solo assistants are cheaper and fine for work that is not time-sensitive; for recurring critical work the advice reverses toward a team or firm, and beginners are told to start with a larger firm (Ch. 6, p. 176).
- **Automating later.** The case for manual scaffolding is bounded by a threshold the author sets in advance: the day the VA bill for a task exceeds a certain amount, he automates it (Ch. 6, p. 168).
- **Serial Micropreneurship.** Gated behind a stern warning that no second product is built or bought until the first is outsourced and automated as far as possible, and that the path is not for a founder unwilling to do that (Ch. 7, p. 190). Task switching is conceded as genuinely less efficient than a single product (pp. 189–190). And no product sells at the same level forever; every one needs updates eventually (p. 192).
- **Autopilot has a floor.** Bugs are still fixed in a timely manner even on a product receiving no new features (Ch. 7, p. 192), and competition makes the autopilot period shorter, which the author ties back to the case for low-competition niches (p. 192).
- **Selling.** Recommended only after automation has been attempted and failed (Ch. 7, pp. 195, 200). Valuations carry many adjustments in both directions, from 2–3x for maintenance-heavy sites to 24–36 months for a parked domain (pp. 194–195), and the six-figure-profit threshold is what separates the millions from the dream (p. 195).

- **Unchecked figures with no outside source at all:** the $106,000 and $2,897 magazine ad rates and the AdWords click costs (Ch. 2, pp. 46–48), the 23,240 marriage and family therapists as of April 2009 (p. 59), the 90%-under-five-seconds Digg figure and the 50,000-visitors-five-sales figure (pp. 60–61), the conversion rates of the author's own sites (p. 68), the 3-in-8-months versus 30-in-a-month signup result (p. 118), the 88% email-account figure from a 2007 study (p. 133), the 40–60% home-page abandonment rate attributed to *Web Design for ROI* (p. 125), the five-copies-in-a-day and 6% list conversion (p. 135), the 10x mailing-list conversion multiple (p. 147), and the 50–80% development-time reduction from manual scaffolding (p. 170). Each is the author's own experience, a secondary citation, or a rule of thumb, and none carries a source that could be opened.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **A toolchain presented as method.** The demand-measurement chapter is the book's central contribution and is built entirely on named third-party tools: the AdWords Keyword Tool, SEO Logs, Micro Niche Finder, SEOmoz, Compete.com, Yahoo! Site Explorer, the Google Toolbar's PageRank meter and a whois lookup (Ch. 2, pp. 58, 73–82). Section 7 found that most of them no longer exist. The reasoning survives the tools; the printed steps, thresholds and screenshots do not, and the book offers no tool-independent statement of the method.
- **Numbers without a source.** The conversion bands of 1–4% and 0.5–2% (Ch. 2, p. 67), the traffic split into thirds (p. 69), the halve-then-multiply-by-four correction (pp. 78–79), the difficulty bands of 39 and 60 (p. 80), the competition threshold of 100 and 1,000 (p. 81), the 2–5% and 40% completion assumptions in the Mini Sales Site (p. 86), the 200–400 hour build estimate (p. 93), the price ceilings (p. 97), the four-times-the-low-end range (p. 99) and the valuation multiples (pp. 194–195) are all stated as rules of thumb from the author's experience, with no dataset and no sample behind any of them.
- **Correlation presented as cause.** The signup result of 3 in 8 months against 30 in a month is attributed to the contest alone, with no control and no mention of traffic changes over the intervening year (Ch. 4, p. 118). The five-copies-in-a-day mailing is read as proof that a list of 500 of the same quality would produce a huge month, which assumes conversion holds as a list grows (p. 135). The theming service's resale is credited to outsourcing and time tracking, in a case where the author also changed the buyer pool by removing the skill requirement (Ch. 7, p. 198).
- **An evidence base of one founder's portfolio.** Nearly every worked number comes from the author's own products, and the two outside examples in chapter 1 are a reader of his blog and an acquaintance (Ch. 1, pp. 6–7). The book promises no filler and no weak case studies (What Does This Book Cover?, PDF 10), and the way it keeps that promise is by citing almost nobody but himself. Failures are described in the abstract, in the Dip scenario (pp. 13–15), rather than as a named product of his that did not work.
- **A study that does not carry the weight put on it.** The three-factor goals claim rests on one research summary with 149 completers, a four-week horizon and self-rated achievement, published on a university website rather than in a journal (Ch. 1, p. 11; see section 7). The book calls it science and tells the reader to trust it.
- **A secondary citation used as a benchmark.** The 40–60% home-page abandonment rate is attributed to another book without a figure, a date or a sample, and is then used to justify the prescribed home-page design (Ch. 4, p. 125).
- **Advice the author's own pages contradict.** Link buying is recommended with the penalty attached in the same paragraph (Ch. 5, p. 163), and Google's policy now names it as spam outright. Article-repository submission is recommended while conceding that the repositories themselves do not rank (p. 161). The book tells the reader to stay out of "bad neighborhoods" one page after describing a large link economy flying under Google's radar (pp. 159, 163).
- **Internal tension about reading and audience.** Reading business books is a distraction that gets a founder no closer to launch (Ch. 1, p. 21), in a business book whose stated goal is that the reader underline it (What Does This Book Cover?, PDF 10) and whose chapter 7 is a reading list. Social media is demoted as Second Shelf (Ch. 5, pp. 148–149) while building an audience is Top Shelf, with no account of how the second is reached without the first.
- **Marketing advice aimed at a market that has moved.** The chapter's Top Shelf is a mailing list, a blog and organic search, and the search half assumes on-page work plus link building is the whole of the discipline (Ch. 5, pp. 156–157). It predates Panda and the collapse of the directory and article-repository economy it recommends; section 7 has the date.
- **A claim the evidence does not reach.** The recurring assertion that the single most important factor is a market willing to pay (Ch. 2, p. 39) is supported by examples selected after the fact, and the book's own luck exception (pp. 40–41) removes the counter-cases from the sample rather than weighing them.
- **Figures a reader could mistake for current.** The platform user counts, mobile projection, VA rates, offshore development costs, MailChimp prices and mail-service prices are printed without a date on the page, and several are now off by an order of magnitude or attached to a company that no longer exists under that name (Ch. 3, pp. 95–96, 107–109; Ch. 4, pp. 131, 138; Ch. 6, p. 174).
