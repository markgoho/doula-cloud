# The SaaS Playbook, read back

## 1. Source

*The SaaS Playbook: Build a Multimillion-Dollar Startup Without Venture Capital*, Rob Walling, first edition, 2023. Self-published; the copyright page names no publisher and points to saasplaybook.com. ISBN 979-8-9877465-2-3 (ebook). Read 2026-09-10 from the `pdftotext -layout` extraction of the 201-page PDF, front to back.

## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, page by page, with its own exceptions, what has changed since 2023, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that a founder can grow a B2B SaaS product to seven or eight figures of revenue without venture capital by following a repeatable playbook of rules of thumb, frameworks, and four "SaaS Cheat Codes" (expansion revenue, virality, net negative churn, dual funnels) drawn from the author's own companies, more than 125 investments, and the MicroConf community (The Playbook, pp. 29, 31, 35, 40). The book assumes the reader already has "some semblance of product-market fit" and is trying to reach what the author calls escape velocity, the point where one or more repeatable growth channels exist (The Playbook, p. 39). "Bootstrapped" is defined broadly to include self-funded and "mostly bootstrapped" companies that raise $100,000 to $500,000 without joining the venture track (The Playbook, pp. 32–34).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a guide to raising venture capital, honing a pitch deck, or evaluating a term sheet (Introduction, p. 25). It is not for a business meant to stay small, which he sends to his first book, *Start Small, Stay Small* (Introduction, p. 26). It is not "feel-good entrepreneurial tropes" such as build an audience or follow your passion (Introduction, p. 26). It is not anti-funding: "I run a fund that invests in startups, for crying out loud" (Introduction, p. 24; Mindset, p. 171). And it is not a treatise on marketing implementation, organizational structure, or positioning, each of which is sent to another book (Marketing, p. 95; Team, p. 123; Market, p. 60).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Foreword (Jason Cohen, pp. 13–16)

Cohen frames the reader as someone who has already decided to found a company and argues that market choice and pricing are where early incompetence most often kills a bootstrapped company; in his own company, "hyper-growth ignited" at the moment of a customer-inspired pricing revamp (Foreword, p. 15). Startups are "equal parts skill and luck", like poker, so the aim is the best possible decision in the areas the book covers (Foreword, p. 16).

### Introduction (pp. 19–26)

The author opens with the 2016 sale of Drip, signed from a cello camp, and the stress that did not lift afterward (Introduction, pp. 19–21). The argument: funding is treated as the assumed path, but "more than 99% of companies that seek funding do not receive it", and the bootstrapped path trades a ".01% chance of making $100 million" for "a 20% or 30% chance of making hundreds of thousands or millions" (Introduction, pp. 22–23). Two claimed superpowers of bootstrapping: no permission needed, and "your business doesn't die until you quit"; bootstrappers "run out of motivation", not money (Introduction, p. 23). The evidence is the author's six startups and his community, MicroConf (Introduction, p. 23).

### The Playbook for Building a Multimillion-Dollar SaaS (pp. 29–40)

The Stair Step Method: step 1 is a simple product on one organic channel (a WordPress plugin, a Shopify app), step 2 repeats it until the founder owns their time, step 3 is a standalone SaaS product; this book is about step 3 (The Playbook, pp. 29–30). The author's own path went from consulting to small products at $500–$5,000 a month to a SaaS at $25,000–$30,000 a month, and he put $150,000–$200,000 of prior profits into Drip before it was profitable (The Playbook, p. 32). Examples of "mostly bootstrapped": Customer.io, which raised $250,000 in 2012 and became "a highly profitable, eight-figure SaaS company", and Castos, which took $120,000 from TinySeed and then $756,000 (The Playbook, pp. 32–33).

Why SaaS: recurring revenue, called "a business cheat code" (The Playbook, p. 35); recession resistance, evidenced by the author's $295 one-time product losing 80% of revenue in one month in 2008 while subscription products held (p. 36); less dependence on luck than social networks or ride-sharing (pp. 36–37); no two-sided marketplace to seed, with the advice to founders who want one being "Don't" (p. 37); no capital requirement, with Zoom, Slack, and PagerDuty cited as going public with much of their venture capital unspent (pp. 37–38); gross margins of 90% and net margins of 50% or more at scale (p. 38); and exit multiples of "four to eight times top-line revenue" for a growing company at $1–2 million ARR, so $1,000 of MRR is "$60,000 of value" at five times (p. 38). Product-market fit in SaaS "often begins to strengthen" around $10,000–$20,000 MRR (p. 39).

### Market (pp. 43–64)

Strengthening product-market fit rests on conversations with prospects, customers, people who declined, and people who canceled; the example is Ruben Gamez of SignWell learning that customers wanted to send their own link rather than a third-party email (Market, p. 44). Ask open-ended questions; a mock-up invites politeness, not data (p. 45). Feature requests sort into "Crackpots", "No-Brainers", and "In-Betweens", roughly 10–15%, 20%, and the rest (pp. 47–48); the three questions for an in-between are the use case, the percentage of customers who would use it (5–10% only if they are power users, and then hidden from the standard UI; 20% or more worth considering), and fit with the founder's vision (pp. 49–50). Integrations are offered as the answer to many big requests (p. 51).

Competing in a mature market: on price, "more than 80% of the product for half the cost" (p. 52); on sales model, with pricing on the website and a low-touch trial where incumbents demand a demo and a setup fee, the example being Drip against Infusionsoft (p. 53); on product, with modern UX against ten-year-old code, the examples being Xero, Pipedrive, and Close (pp. 53–54). Marketing against large competitors is judo: find the frustrated users of the incumbent and help them migrate; the Avis "We're #2" ads and Drip's "Doesn't Suck" headline are the examples (pp. 54–55). On competition, only two things matter: high-level announcements and deals actually lost; funding rounds, polish, and copying are to be ignored, with a caveat for a rival raising $5 million or more who may drop prices (pp. 55–57).

Four moats: integrations as network effect, with Zapier's "over 3,000 apps" and its nonpublic API integrations (pp. 58–59); a strong brand, meaning reputation rather than logo, including positioning to a segment such as museums (pp. 59–60); owned traffic channels, with the caveat that algorithm changes have "tanked businesses overnight" (p. 60); and high switching costs, with Stripe, Twilio, SendGrid, and Slack as examples and social-media schedulers and one-click analytics as the low end (pp. 60–61). Unique features are a "false moat", "a hamster wheel of features" (p. 61). Siren songs: translating the product, white labeling, adding verticals too early, and underpricing, where "if you're charging $10 instead of $100 a month, you have to find 10 times as many customers" and cannot afford most channels (pp. 62–64).

### Pricing (pp. 67–85)

Pricing is "the biggest lever in SaaS" and most founders price too low (Pricing, p. 67). Price bands follow the sales motion: $10–15 a month for consumers with high churn, $50 a month or more for breathing room, $250 and up in niches or where a demo is needed, $1,000 and up for multi-call sales, $30,000 a year and up for enterprise procurement (pp. 67–68). Adjacent and complementary tools are the pricing signal, compared on how they are sold, not only their features (p. 68). "If no one's complaining about your price, you're probably priced too low" (p. 68). Segmentation by size and usage is shown through SquadCast's four tiers at $20, $40, $80, and Contact Us (pp. 69–70).

Expansion revenue comes from a value metric (subscribers at MailChimp, seats at Salesforce, hours at SquadCast) or from feature gating, and the two can combine (pp. 70–72). Seat-based pricing is recommended "only if two users from the same company see different things when they log in"; otherwise "they might as well just share a login" (p. 71). Enterprise plans are charged 10–20 times the standard plan (p. 73).

Freemium: "66% of SaaS products offer a free trial, but only 17% offer a forever-free" plan, per the State of Independent SaaS Report; freemium "is like a samurai sword" and suits a simple, low-support, viral product such as e-signature, not a construction CRM (pp. 73–74). Competitors' freemium does not oblige a founder, and Bounce Exchange launched at $2,995 a month with no free plan (pp. 74–75). A credit card up front is the author's default: it is "a qualifying event", ten times as many trials arrive without one, and the support burden multiplies (pp. 75–76). The exception is products adopted by employees without company cards (Slack, Trello, Dropbox) (p. 76). Founders who later "open the floodgates" share five traits, including more than $20,000 MRR and metrics known by heart; Castos saw no significant change in growth rate but a positive overall result (pp. 77–78). Change one variable at a time; a seven-day trial runs four cohorts in the time of a 30-day one, and "no trial at all but just a refund policy during the first 30 days" is faster still (p. 78).

Raise prices every six to twelve months; the second-order effect is that a higher ARPA opens more marketing approaches, "maybe five" at $20 a month and every one at $5,000 (p. 79). "Aspirational pricing": Drip launched at $49 a month while it was only an email capture widget and the author built until it was worth that (p. 80). Ways to raise: shrink the value metric per tier, hide the lowest tier, raise across the board, or multiply by ten to change segment, the example being Gather going upmarket over eighteen months (p. 81). Treat an uncertain increase as an experiment, the "poor person's split test"; only Zapier is known to have split-tested price (p. 82). Grandfathering: "Rob's Rule of 10", grandfather unless the increase grows MRR by at least 10%; never promise it for life; enterprise customers expect 5–10% annual increases (pp. 83–84). Announce two to four months ahead with a six-part template; the examples are CartHook's announcement and Gymdesk, whose increase of more than 50% cost "only a few" of 600-plus customers and raised MRR by 25% (pp. 84–85).

### Marketing (pp. 87–115)

A great product does not sell itself; Apple and Basecamp are "so good at marketing that it's invisible" (Marketing, p. 87). Marketing strategy cannot be hired out early because the founder must be able to judge the hire; implementation can (pp. 88–89). Three funnels: high-touch (booth, lead, demo, close), which "work best for companies that charge a minimum of $500 a month" and lean outbound, with Drip closing 25–35% of demos (pp. 90–91); low-touch (visit, trial, self-serve purchase), a volume game with lower conversion and higher churn (pp. 92–93); and the dual funnel Cheat Code, a wide low-touch funnel feeding a high-touch one, with SignWell as the example (pp. 93–94). Rules of thumb for funnel metrics are deliberately not printed because they change; they live on saasplaybook.com (p. 93). A broken funnel is diagnosed from the bottom up: churn first, then trial conversion, then visitor sign-up, and "everything at the top of the funnel is a vanity metric when the bottom of your funnel isn't healthy" (pp. 94–95).

Twenty B2B approaches, of which the "Big 5" are SEO (including app stores and plugin repositories), PPC, cold outreach (Drip pinged Infusionsoft customers eight months into their annual contract), integration marketing (build only if both partners will promote), and content marketing (pp. 96–98). Others: affiliates at 10–30%, in-person events, free tools, hangouts, Q&A sites, virality, other people's audiences, daily-deal and launch sites (pp. 99–100). Lesser-used: audience building, which only 5% of TinySeed companies did before launch, display, stunts, speaking, PR, offline ads (p. 101). The Three Factor Framework scores each approach on speed, cost, and scalability, and the advice is to run one fast and one slow approach at once (pp. 102–103). A table maps approaches to ACV bands: high ACV (about $25,000 and up) gets cold-calling, events, PR; medium (about $7,500 and up) gets cold email, PPC, integrations; low ACV gets content, SEO, virality, founder-managed affiliates and partnerships, other people's audiences, launch and deal sites (pp. 103–104). Prioritize with ICE (impact, confidence, ease) (pp. 104–105). Keep a marketing changelog, measure every channel, and do not try too much at once; companies reach seven and eight figures on one or two channels (pp. 105–106). Talk to non-competing founders who market to the same audience, and to competitors' former employees (pp. 106–107). Do not pour money into marketing before product-market fit (pp. 107–108).

Word of mouth is usually the answer of a founder who does not know where customers come from; at Drip it drove "15% to 25% of new users"; ask at sign-up, and, per Gamez, set a cookie with the referral source and landing page and save both to the database at sign-up (pp. 108–109). Sales demos, for high-touch funnels only: the seller is "an unpaid expert", qualifies before the demo, has a script, says no to poor fits, follows up, takes payment on the call, and asks about the problem, the current tool, organization size, sophistication, and the decision process (pp. 109–113). Drip's hack: a "Book a Demo" pop-up asked for the value metric and sent low numbers to a ten-minute video, high numbers to a calendar (p. 114). A sales hire comes when demos crowd out founder work, typically by five or six employees; with warm inbound leads, sales and customer success can be one role (pp. 114–115).

### Team (pp. 117–141)

"Delegate roles, not tasks" (Team, p. 117). Departments and likely first hires: product manager above $1 million ARR, UX designer, software engineer, marketing individual contributors then a manager, an account executive, a support representative, a customer success manager, and an operations role for HR, legal, and finance, with legal and finance outsourced until then (pp. 118–119). Which role next: track a week of tasks, group them, and ask which the founder is bad at, does not enjoy, could stop, could hand off, or would grow the company if leveled up; support is usually early (pp. 119–120). Roles can be combined below ten or fifteen employees, with a table of pairings that work (customer success plus AE only with warm inbound; engineer plus product manager; legal, finance, and HR as operations) (pp. 121–122). Use standard job titles; Drip's "Senior Scaling Architect" could not be salary-benchmarked, and an early "Head of" title creates a later demotion problem (pp. 122–123).

A technical founder is told to learn sales and marketing and hire developers, because "developers are gonna develop" and coding needs a maker's schedule (pp. 123–125). Ninety percent of bootstrapped SaaS companies have a technical founder, per MicroConf's survey; a nontechnical team should treat its first developer as near-cofounder (pp. 125–126). A founder who does not plan to hire is challenged to hire at least a support person, because burnout kills bootstrappers (p. 126). "Be a team, not a family"; nobody has said "I fired that person too soon" (pp. 126–127). Management splits into supervision and leadership; leads come at two or three people in a department, managers at four or five; the first managers must also be individual contributors; praise in public, correct in private (pp. 127–131). Task-, project-, and owner-level thinkers are defined, with the shift to project-level hires placed at $20,000–$40,000 MRR (pp. 128–129).

Hiring: hire slow, fire fast; use a flat-fee recruiter such as Remote First Recruiting rather than 15% of salary; write job descriptions as sales letters with personality ("Crappy code makes you want to flip a table"); lean into remote work, no politics, and founder access; an audience helps hiring though the author calls audience-building "a waste of time for most people"; retention matters more than hiring; job boards live on the companion site (pp. 132–135). Incentives: bonuses are arbitrary and can be litigated; equity in an LLC passes a K-1 on paper profits and is "best for founding employees"; stock options suit a grow-and-exit plan; profit sharing suits a run-it-forever plan, structured as a pool, with Balsamiq's 10%, later 15%, and "I believe he's now up to 20%" as the model, split 25% equally and 75% by seniority (pp. 135–139). A cofounder is not needed: 56% of respondents to the 2022 State of Independent SaaS Report were single founders, and a cofounder is the largest equity a founder ever gives away; vest everyone; too many founders is also a failure mode (pp. 139–141).

### 80/20 SaaS Metrics (pp. 143–162)

Two north-star metrics, MRR and month-over-month growth, both lagging (80/20 SaaS Metrics, p. 144). The 3 High/3 Low framework: keep CAC, sales effort, and churn low, and ACV, expansion revenue, and referrals high; ProfitWell, ChartMogul, or Baremetrics can compute them from the payment processor (pp. 144–145). CAC is judged by payback: venture companies spend up to one-third of LTV, but "most successful bootstrappers I know are in the two- to six-month payback period", and Drip at its peak ran seven or eight months (pp. 145–146). Sales effort is days and calls to close, reduced by self-serve and one-call closes (pp. 146–147). Gross revenue churn bands for bootstrapped B2B: above 10% "Catastrophic", 8–10% "Not Good", 6–7% "Meh", 4–5% "Fine", 2–3% "Good", below 2% "Great", with lower bands for contracts above $25,000 (p. 148). The plateau formula is new MRR per month divided by churn rate (p. 149). ACV is preferred to LTV because a bootstrapper cannot wait eight years for a $5,000 LTV at 1% churn (pp. 149–150). Referrals are asked for by an automated email at 60 or 90 days for low-touch products (p. 151).

Virality: strong loops where the product connects people (Slack, SavvyCal links, SignWell) and weak loops from "Powered by" badges (Drip, Mailchimp, Veed.io); virality is hard to retrofit and a company can reach millions with a zero viral coefficient (pp. 152–153). Vanity metrics (visitors, free users, subscribers) matter only as conversions (pp. 153–154). Churn early is too small a number to read; before product-market fit, the reason matters more than the rate; gaming churn with cancel-by-email or annual-only hides the reason (pp. 154–155). Target gross revenue churn "certainly under 3% per month"; TinySeed companies serving hobbyists sit near 5% (p. 155). Segment churn by pricing tier (a TinySeed company: 11% net churn at $30, −4% at $100), by marketing channel (AgentMethods found PPC leads had far lower LTV, tracked in ChartMogul; SegMetrics or Mixpanel are named), and by cohort with a retention grid (pp. 155–158). First-sixty-day churn means people never set up; fixes are onboarding emails (Val Geisler's Dinner Party Strategy), a customer success hire at higher price points, and finding the "minimum path to awesome", which Drip tracked on an internal dashboard (pp. 159–160). Drip sent a founder-signed email within ten minutes of every cancellation asking why (p. 161). Net negative churn needs gross churn in the 0–3% range plus expansion; HitTail's 8% churn and 3% expansion never got there, while an API-credit company at 2% churn and 3% expansion reached −1% (pp. 161–162).

### Mindset (pp. 165–188)

Success is hard work, luck, and skill; Jason Fried credited Basecamp's growth to "luck and timing"; the founder controls work, skill, and mindset (Mindset, pp. 165–166). Successful founders have a bias toward action, develop "founder gut" by learning from others, and manage their own psychology, which the author calls "more than half" of the job (pp. 166–168). Early on, do things that do not scale; later, be effective rather than efficient, and delegate certainties (known work) while keeping risks (unknowns such as finding a channel) for founder-level thinking (pp. 168–171). Funding "is a tool"; today's non-venture capital (angels, TinySeed) can buy time, and "in your business, money saves you years"; have a plan, do not raise before product-market fit, and do not "torch your cap table" by giving 70% to an agency or 60% to a $50,000 investor; vest founder equity; Castos, SignWell (SOC 2 and HIPAA compliance), Rally (a chief of staff from day one), and SavvyCal (early SEO spend) are the examples of funding well spent; the drawbacks are time, complexity, and dilution (pp. 171–176). Speed bumps masquerade as roadblocks; the author's biggest regret is stress over "things that were going to work out", and his remedy is to map three or four non-fatal options (pp. 176–179). Masterminds give growth, accountability, and support; MicroConf's matching was "close to matching 1,000 founders across 50 countries with more than $200 million in collective ARR" (pp. 179–181). Choose one or two mentors, no more (pp. 181–182). Burnout: the author's 2015 descent at Drip, Jason Cohen's joy, skill, need diagram from SaaStr 2018, and the regret of not hiring an operations person (pp. 182–185). Founder retreats of 48–72 hours, from which the author decided to launch TinySeed rather than leave the startup space (pp. 186–188).

### Afterword, Acknowledgments, Appendix A (pp. 191–198)

The afterword points to the podcast, the YouTube series, and saasplaybook.com (Afterword, p. 191). Acknowledgments credit Ruben Gamez, Craig Hewitt, Aaron Kassover, and Derrick Reimer as draft reviewers (Acknowledgments, p. 193). Appendix A lists communities (MicroConf, Indie Hackers, Dynamite Circle, Rhodium), podcasts, mastermind matchers, and books by theme, including Dunford's *Obviously Awesome*, Miller's *Building a StoryBrand*, Dib's *The 1-Page Marketing Plan*, and Kalbach's *The Jobs To Be Done Playbook* (Appendix A, pp. 195–198).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** The book is for a founder with some product-market fit who wants seven or eight figures; a lifestyle business gets "mixed value" and is sent to *Start Small, Stay Small* (Introduction, p. 26; The Playbook, p. 39). The Stair Step Method can be skipped: "it's possible to skip the stairs and still achieve success" (The Playbook, p. 31).
- **Competitive markets.** Do not start a first startup in a highly competitive market "without prior experience or funding—or both" (Market, p. 52). Competing on price is not a permanent position: "I'm not recommending that you remain the low-cost provider indefinitely" (Market, p. 52). The product advantage of modern UX "is a temporary advantage" that ages (Market, p. 54).
- **Competition worth watching.** The rule to ignore competitors has two exceptions, big announcements and lost deals, and a third caveat for a rival raising $5 million or more who may give the product away (Market, pp. 55–57).
- **Moats.** Owned traffic "can be a bit dicey to maintain" because algorithms change (Market, p. 60). Unique features are a differentiator "for a few months" and growth can still be found that way (Market, pp. 61–62).
- **Translation.** Advised against, with one counter-case the author saw work: Spanish-speaking end users whose managers, the buyers, spoke English, so only the app was translated (Market, pp. 62–63).
- **White labeling.** "Not a fan" in most situations, but a large player is worth the conversation, at an up-front fee of "$30,000 to $50,000 at a minimum" (Market, p. 63).
- **Verticals.** Adjacent audiences with the same needs (wedding photographers to videographers) can work; audiences with different needs in the same industry (wedding coordinators) usually do not (Market, pp. 63–64).
- **Price bands.** A $10–15 consumer price can work "if you have a no-touch sign-up process with a product that sells itself"; Castos and Snappa are named (Pricing, p. 68). The author cannot give "a single 'correct' structure" (Pricing, p. 67).
- **Seats.** Seat pricing is limited to products where two users see different things (Pricing, p. 71), yet "per-seat pricing with two or three levels of feature access is not a bad way to go" (Pricing, p. 72).
- **Freemium.** It works with a simple product, low support, some virality, and low per-user cost, not with complex onboarding or free users in a different market from paid ones; if competitors' free plans become "a sticking point with the prospects you talk with", a founder "might consider" one (Pricing, pp. 73–75).
- **Credit card up front.** Drop it when the product is adopted by employees without company cards (Pricing, p. 76), or once the company is established, above $20,000 MRR, with metrics known and support capacity in hand; then change one variable at a time (Pricing, pp. 77–78). Not every industry can run a seven-day trial (Pricing, p. 78).
- **Raising prices.** "It's possible to raise your prices higher than what the market will bear"; churning customers who say it is too expensive "might still be right", and the answer offered is to build the product up to the price rather than cut it (Pricing, pp. 79–80). Below the Rule of 10, grandfather; at a jump such as $9 to $99, grandfathering "is not economically viable" (Pricing, p. 83). "Nothing in this book should be considered legal or tax advice" (Team, p. 136).
- **Funnel numbers.** Rules of thumb "change over time", so the book prints none and sends the reader to the companion site (Marketing, p. 93). The same is true of job boards (Team, p. 135).
- **Approaches by ACV.** The mapping is "a starting point"; costs "vary widely" by space and by who runs the effort; free tools "might get lucky" at low ACV; affiliates and partnerships only count as low-ACV approaches if founder-managed (Marketing, pp. 103–104). A media brand alongside the product is "only for very well-funded companies" (Marketing, p. 98). Audience building preceded launch at 5% of TinySeed companies (Marketing, p. 101).
- **Word of mouth.** It is real once a brand exists, "generally... about seven figures of revenue", and even at Drip it was 15–25%; full attribution is impossible, "any attribution is better than none" (Marketing, pp. 108–109).
- **Sales.** The demo section may be skipped by a low-touch funnel (Marketing, p. 109). Not every product is a one-call close (Marketing, p. 111).
- **Team.** The advice to hire for what the founder is bad at reverses for a technical founder, who is told to keep sales and marketing and hire developers (Team, pp. 123–124). Combined roles are for teams below ten or fifteen (Team, p. 121); customer success plus AE works "only with warm, inbound leads" (Team, p. 121). A nontechnical founding team is "very much in the minority" and most who try either raise early or fund a senior developer from another business (Team, p. 125). The management ladder "doesn't have to be the case"; principal and distinguished titles exist (Team, p. 123).
- **Incentives.** Equity is "best for founding employees (cofounders)"; stock options suit "growth and an exit"; profit sharing "is likely not your best option" for a grow-and-exit plan (Team, pp. 137–139).
- **Cofounders.** Not needed, and single founders are 56% of the 2022 survey, yet "there can be enormous value in having a partner", tested by complementary skills, prior work together, vesting, and whether the person adds value (Team, pp. 139–141).
- **Metrics.** In the early days churn numbers are "too small to be meaningful" and a $200 cancellation on $1,000 MRR is "just a single customer" (80/20 SaaS Metrics, p. 154). The churn bands drop for contracts above $25,000 (p. 148). Hobbyist-market companies at 5% churn are "okay" in massive markets with low CAC (p. 155). CAC payback can stretch to seven or eight months with cash in the bank (p. 146). A company can reach millions "with a zero viral coefficient" (p. 153). Rolling one's own attribution "is a foolish decision" (p. 158). Pushing churn below 2–3% "is a long road" (p. 161).
- **Funding.** The author is "not anti-funding" but against absolutes; he does not recommend raising before product-market fit; raising "can make the downsides of your product or business model worse" (Mindset, pp. 171–174).
- **Roadblocks.** Some obstacles are real roadblocks, a platform shutting its API or a lawsuit, "although these things happen, they are much less common than speed bumps" (Mindset, p. 177).
- **Mentors.** "Find a mentor or two. But no more" (Mindset, p. 181). Burnout beyond the chapter is sent to the author's third book (Mindset, pp. 185–186).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter or the ticket that holds it. ## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Correlation presented as cause.** The Gymdesk story attributes a 25% MRR rise and a 70% rise in growth rate to the price increase alone (Pricing, p. 85), with no control and no mention of the rebrand the same interview covers. The TinySeed tier-churn example, one company with 11% churn at $30 and −4% at $100, is generalized to "in almost every case, your low-paying customers will churn faster" (80/20 SaaS Metrics, pp. 156–157). Cohen's foreword credits a pricing revamp with igniting hyper-growth on the coincidence of timing (Foreword, p. 15).
- **Figures without a source.** "More than 99% of companies that seek funding do not receive it", the ".01% chance" against "20% or 30%", and "more than 99% of new companies started every year take no funding" (Introduction, p. 23) carry no citation. "More than 80% of the product for half the cost" (Market, p. 52), the 10–15% and 20% feature-request shares (Market, p. 48), the 5–10% and 20% build thresholds (Market, p. 50), and the churn bands (80/20 SaaS Metrics, p. 148) are stated as rules of thumb from experience. The CAC payback of two to six months (p. 146) is the author's circle, and section 7 found industry medians two to four times longer.
- **A dated fact.** The Avis "We try harder" campaign is placed "in the 1980s" (Market, p. 54); it began in 1962.
- **Examples from the author's own portfolio.** Castos, Gather, Gymdesk, SquadCast, Customer.io, SignWell, SavvyCal, and Rally are TinySeed companies, the author's investments, or companies of his draft reviewers, and the book discloses only the SquadCast stake (Pricing, p. 69). The sample is not random, and the counter-examples, companies in the same portfolio that did not work, do not appear.
- **Numbers that have moved.** The 4–8x exit multiples (The Playbook, p. 38), the SquadCast tiers (Pricing, p. 70), and the mastermind ARR figure (Mindset, p. 181) were true or close to true in 2022–2023 and are not now; section 7 has the later figures. The freemium and credit-card statistics (Pricing, p. 73) describe what founders do, not what worked, and the 2024 edition of the same survey reports higher LTV for the no-card trial the author advises against by default.
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** The metrics chapter is defined in subscription terms, MRR, churn, expansion, ARPA, and the only non-subscription model it describes is an API-credit company at 2% churn in one paragraph (80/20 SaaS Metrics, p. 162); the book offers no way to compute its 3 High/3 Low metrics for a product without a recurring plan. The recession-resistance argument rests on one product in 2008 (The Playbook, p. 36). "Bootstrappers don't run out of money; they run out of motivation" (Introduction, p. 23) is asserted, not shown. The word-of-mouth figure of 15–25% comes from one company and is used to set a general expectation (Marketing, p. 108).
- **Internal tension.** Seat pricing is limited to products where users see different things (Pricing, p. 71) and then recommended with feature tiers a page later as "not a bad way to go" (Pricing, p. 72). The book calls audience-building "a waste of time for most people" (Team, p. 134) and lists it among lesser-used approaches (Marketing, p. 101) while the author's own path ran through a podcast, a conference, and four books (Mindset, pp. 187–188).
- **Deferred content.** The funnel rules of thumb and the hiring boards are moved to the companion website because they change (Marketing, p. 93; Team, p. 135); a reader of the book alone has the shape of the funnel and no numbers, and the site's numbers are undated.
- **An unlabeled table.** The retention grid (80/20 SaaS Metrics, p. 158) gives monthly cohorts with values such as "$3.995" and percentages above 100% with no unit, no company, and no explanation of the figures above 100%.
