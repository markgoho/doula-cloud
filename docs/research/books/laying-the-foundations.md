## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues about building and running a design system, chapter by chapter with its own exceptions, what has changed since 2019, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis is that a design system is the product of two things done in the right order: laying "Digital Foundations" — brand, tone, formatting, and accessibility guidelines for digital work, distinct from a print brand book — and only then building foundations, components, patterns, templates, and pages (or, for a product rather than a website, features and screens) on top of them, using a simple shared model and vocabulary so a team stays on the same page (Chapter 4, pp. 80–84). The author's own frame: "a systematic approach to design," not a deliverable (Introduction, p. 15). Two ways to build one are offered — iterative (gradual, sprint-by-sprint replacement of what exists) and wholesale (a from-scratch redesign) — and the author recommends a mix rather than a pure choice of either (Chapter 5, pp. 112–113). Documentation is what separates a design system from a UI kit or a simple style guide, and it is supposed to be built as you go, not at the end (Chapter 9, pp. 181–183). A system is never finished; it requires ongoing maintenance, shared assets, and team process to keep design and code in sync (Chapter 11, pp. 242–243, 259).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a technology or tool guide, deliberately avoiding specific tools and software because "technology moves too fast for print" (Introduction, p. 14). It is not a development book, and does not aim to cover "development processes" in depth (Introduction, p. 13). It carries "no company hat" and pointedly avoids the varnish of accounts written by people "wearing a company hat" (Introduction, p. 12). And it does not claim every project needs a design system — the reader is told to still take a "systematic approach to design" even without building one (Chapter 1, p. 22).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Foreword (Meagan Fisher Couldwell, pp. VIII–IX)

Frames the book as free of "company hat" varnish, written by someone who did "system design" before the industry had the term (p. IX).

### Introduction (pp. 10–19)

The author's thesis in miniature: over 15 years designing solo, on small and large teams, in-house and freelance, he distills recurring failure patterns into a book that keeps jargon out (pp. 11–13). System design is framed as nothing new or scary — "nobody invented system design," only a name the industry gave existing good practice (p. 14). Even without building a formal system, a team may already be designing systematically "without even realising" — using two typefaces, a consistent grid, defined text styles — and doing so makes a designer better regardless of whether a company ever commissions a system (p. 15). Not every project needs a design system, but every project benefits from systematic thinking (p. 22).

### Chapter 1: What is a design system? (pp. 20–25)

Defines a design system as something that brings order and consistency to digital products, a "source of truth" for design decisions that is never "set in stone" (p. 20). Seven claimed benefits: efficiency and speed, consistency and user experience, stronger brands, freedom to focus on real problems instead of "debating padding, colours, and type choices," organization, a shared vocabulary, and a source of truth (pp. 21–23). Need varies: agencies and small companies often ship without one and that is "fine" (p. 22); startups can treat a system as low priority early because they face "radical and fast changes" of direction, while still designing systematically (p. 23); larger, longer-lived, multi-team products need one most, quoting Karri Saarinen of Airbnb on how divergence "multiplies exponentially" as headcount grows (p. 23). <!-- spelling:ignore: verbatim quotation of the book -->

### Chapter 2: Selling a design system (pp. 26–37)

Selling is "ongoing," never a completed stage, and often the hardest part (pp. 26, 33). Worked example: colleague Nick Stamas sold engineers on a centralized code base before pitching "design system" as a phrase, building a code-first prototype called Plasma while the author built brand-level "Digital Foundations" in parallel, and the two converged (pp. 27–29). Pitch by audience: product/business leaders care about shipping speed and business impact; engineers care about a unified code base and performance; designers care about aesthetics and freedom from rogue elements — each audience gets a different argument for the same system (pp. 30–31). A stakeholder pitch example is reproduced as a deck screenshot (fig 2.1, p. 31) framed around what the system saves, what it makes the company look like, and what a competitor is or isn't doing. Smaller organizations sell more easily than larger ones, because there is less hierarchy to move through (p. 35).

### Chapter 3: Laying the Foundations (pp. 38–79)

Introduces "Digital Foundations" — the author's name for digital-specific brand guidelines, distinct from a traditional print brand book — as the layer that must exist before a design system, because without a unique brand an off-the-shelf system "would do the job" (pp. 40–43). Marketing and product surfaces of the same brand look different (Dropbox's loud marketing site versus its quiet product, figs 3.1–3.3) yet share enough — a blue, a logotype, sentence case — to read as one brand (pp. 41–42). Digital Foundations, unlike a print brand book, cover mission/values/principles, brand identity, tone of voice and copywriting, formatting (date/time/currency conventions, sentence vs. title case, American vs. British English), accessibility and inclusivity, responsive design, process, and design-system terminology itself (pp. 44–68), each illustrated by a named company's public guidelines (Mozilla, Firefox, Atlassian, Uber, WeWork, IBM). Accessibility is framed broadly — age, language, situational impairment, the example of "a young, athletic parent" managing a device one-handed — not only permanent disability, quoting Don Norman (pp. 62–63). The chapter closes by telling the reader to just start writing — the author's own Digital Foundations began as a Google Doc, not a designed artifact (pp. 73–74) — and that publishing them publicly is optional PR/recruiting value, not the point (pp. 74–78).

### Chapter 4: Design system model (pp. 80–105)

Presents "the Foundations Model": foundations (brand-level guidelines), components (buttons, inputs — the smallest reusable UI pieces), patterns (navigation, cards, modals — larger recurring assemblies of foundations and components), then templates and pages for a website, or features, screens, and user journeys for a more dynamic product (pp. 84–94), worked through a real case study of the Adobe Portfolio product at each layer (pp. 95–101). The model's terminology is explicitly not fixed: teams are told to adapt it to "suit your team's preferences" (p. 84). A system needs "room for creativity"; the language of "guidelines" rather than "rules" is deliberate, because rigid rules push designers to go rogue, while some elements (global nav, footer) do warrant rigidity for consistency (pp. 101–103).

### Chapter 5: Getting started (pp. 106–113)

Before design work starts, identify the problems a system is meant to solve, as a team exercise, documented centrally (pp. 107–108). Two paths follow: the iterative approach (small, sprint-sized replacements of what exists) and the wholesale approach (a from-scratch redesign), and the choice depends on product risk — an iterative approach is safer for a product people use daily (Amazon is the example of an iteratively-evolving giant), while a wholesale relaunch is a gamble that can backfire badly, citing Snapchat's 2018 redesign, which lost roughly 2% of daily active users (about 3 million) after over 1.25 million people signed a petition against it (pp. 111–113). The two approaches are not mutually exclusive; the author recommends blending them (p. 113).

### Chapter 6: An iterative approach (pp. 114–127)

Start by finding a small, willing team, then run "interface audits": color audits, code audits, and visual audits (also called interface inventories) to find inconsistencies before fixing them (pp. 115–121). A color audit at Hipmunk found a large, chaotic set of near-duplicate colors that was consolidated into a semantic palette (fig 6.1–6.2, pp. 116–117); the author's own visual audit once found "26 different styles of buttons," which were not collapsed to one but sorted by actual function into named variants ("Primary," "Secondary," "Tertiary") once documented (pp. 121–123). Work in sprints, with a portion of every sprint reserved for system work, ordered by importance or by disruption (least-disruptive foundations first, most-disruptive patterns last) (pp. 124–125). The risk named "the Frankenstein monster effect": iterating piece by piece without holistic review produces an incoherent whole, illustrated with a "refinement vs. exploration" diagram credited to Bill Buxton's *Sketching User Experiences* (fig 6.6, pp. 126–127), and a quote from Intercom's Des Traynor warning that iterating can lead "to lots of places, including mediocrity" (p. 126).

### Chapter 7: A wholesale approach (pp. 128–139)

Design with real, unruly content, not idealized short copy — the advice is to use "unreasonably long (and short) titles" — because a system that only survives on convenient content will break in production (pp. 129–130). Airbnb's "Building a Visual Language" is used as the model case: they audited old and new designs side by side, focused on product flows rather than isolated components, and had already established color/type/spacing foundations before starting (pp. 130–132, quoting Karri Saarinen). A launch decision follows design: build everything and hard-launch (the Nike/Apple pattern, appropriate to brands "under the microscope"), or build and roll out iteratively, which the author illustrates from his own work taking Adobe Portfolio through staged betas rather than one "big reveal" (pp. 133–136). The chapter's closing image is a jigsaw-puzzle metaphor: pieces designed in isolation that later "don't fit together" (p. 138).

### Chapter 8: Systematising the design (pp. 140–179)

A design system only works if the design team actually reaches for it instead of drawing new one-off elements ("unintentional design"), which requires an accessible, editable shared library ("design library") of foundations, components, and patterns, with named, systematic naming conventions matched exactly across design, code, and documentation (pp. 141–151). Color is built as a system, not a swatch: pick a base hex per brand color and generate light/dark steps from it programmatically (via Sass in the book's example), naming each step numerically ("$blue50" as the base, "$blue60" darker, "$blue40" lighter) rather than with vague or arbitrary names like "Ocean Blue," citing Hipmunk's confusing "Warning Red"/"Warning Pink"/"Hip Error" as the failure case and Google Material's numeric system as the model (pp. 152–156). Text styles should be kept few and each given a defined, documented use case (pp. 157–159). Components should be built as editable symbols covering every state — hover, disabled, error, placeholder — with only their content, not their styling, left open to a designer's edit (pp. 159–162). Design tokens are distinguished from generic Sass variables — a token names a specific use ("$color-background-light") rather than a raw value ("$white"), so a token can change without side effects elsewhere a raw variable is reused (pp. 165–170), quoting Salesforce Lightning's own definition of a design token (p. 165), and the same token mechanism applies beyond color to spacing, e.g. GitHub Primer's 8-point spacing scale (pp. 174–175). The chapter closes arguing for designers having at least some code access, since design tokens make small changes (a color, a spacing value) achievable without "bugging" developers (pp. 175–176).

### Chapter 9: Document everything! (pp. 180–219)

Documentation, not a style guide or UI kit alone, is what makes something a design system: a UI kit "could be applied to anything," and a style guide documents what exists but not why, when, or how to use it — the book's own image is a style guide next to "IKEA furniture without the instructions" (pp. 181–182). Document as you build, not after — Airbnb is quoted regretting deferred documentation, and the author gives his own Adobe Portfolio project as a counter-example: fully designed and built with detailed "red-line" specs but no written rationale, which the next team had no way to recover (pp. 185–187). What to document: do's and don'ts with example imagery (BBC, Google Material); color with hex, name, and intent (OpenTable's "Early Girl"/"Blueberry" naming, with a written rationale for each color, pp. 190–195); brand identity/logo usage rules; typography with font-size/line-height/letter-spacing and intent per style; copywriting tone and formatting; components and patterns with all states, responsive behavior, and edge cases (the BBC's Welsh-language long-word example, p. 207); grid, layout, and spacing; and design tokens (pp. 190–211). A living style guide — documentation pulling live components directly from production code — solves the problem of static documentation drifting from the real system, at the cost of needing developer involvement to build (pp. 216–217). The author's own path went from a Google Doc (fast, accessible, but not searchable or embeddable) to a GitBook-based site built with developers (pp. 212–219).

### Chapter 10: Gallery (pp. 220–241)

A curated tour of public design-system and brand-guideline sites — Basic Culture, BBC GEL, Airbnb Design, Salesforce Lightning, Trello's Nachos, Marvel, GitHub Primer, IBM Carbon, Sprout Social's Seeds, BuzzFeed's Solid, and Thumbtack's Thumbprint — each annotated for one thing it does well: BBC GEL's black-and-white, content-first documentation and its "how-to" articles (pp. 222–226); Lightning's living, token-driven documentation with accessibility notes (pp. 228–229); Nachos' detailed static image specs as a lower-effort alternative to a living style guide (pp. 230–232); Carbon's split of "Usage" from "Code" documentation with an embedded CodePen per component (pp. 236–237); Seeds keeping brand and design-system documentation in one hub rather than two (pp. 238–239); Solid's changelog/"Release Notes" practice (pp. 239–240).

### Chapter 11: Maintaining a design system (pp. 242–259)

Naming the failure modes that kill a design system after launch: designers and developers drifting onto different versions, local files instead of shared ones, undocumented changes, and team members feeling excluded from the system's evolution (p. 242). The fix is shared, syncable design assets (Sketch Library is the book's example, syncing a master component file to every designer's working file, figs 11.1–11.4, pp. 243–246) plus a defined process for any change: update the library, track the code work (e.g. a GitHub Issue), update documentation, and tell the team before the change ships (pp. 247–251). Ownership models are offered on a spectrum: an individual design system lead, a dedicated design systems team, or a distributed network of per-team "ambassadors" reporting back to a central lead, quoting Sprout Social's own ambassador program (pp. 254–257). Closing claim: a design system is "a marathon, not a sprint" and is never finished (p. 259).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** A project too small or short-lived can skip a design system entirely and still benefit from designing systematically (Chapter 1, p. 22). Startups specifically are told a system can be a low priority given how often their direction changes, and are urged instead to be "more realistic about their priorities" (Chapter 1, p. 23).
- **Terminology is not fixed.** Teams are told to feel free to change the model's terms to "suit your team's preferences" (Chapter 4, p. 84); if "features," "screens," or "user journeys" don't fit a team's product, the advice is simply to "re-work the model" (Chapter 4, p. 100).
- **Guidelines, not rules.** Some elements benefit from rigidity (a global nav, a footer, text styles) but the system overall should read as "guidelines," because rules read as "laws that can't be broken" and push designers toward going rogue (Chapter 4, pp. 101–102).
- **Iterative vs. wholesale is not a strict choice.** The two are explicitly not exclusive — the author's own recommendation is "a mix of the two" (Chapter 5, p. 113). A wholesale redesign is right for a brand "under the microscope" (Nike, Apple) and risky for a daily-use product, since "humans are creatures of habit" (Chapter 5, pp. 111–112, 133).
- **The 26-buttons finding was not "reduce to one."** After auditing, the goal was not to erase every variant — the answer was not to "replace 26 with 1" — but to keep, name, and document the variants tied to real distinct functions (Chapter 6, pp. 122–123).
- **Tool references are explicitly time-bound.** The book states outright that "technology moves too fast for print" (Introduction, p. 14); on every tool besides Sketch, his own working choice, the author admits to having "no experience" and passing along secondhand impressions only — Framer X, Adobe XD, Figma (Chapter 8, p. 146).
- **Design tokens vs. Sass variables is a trade-off, not a universal rule.** Plain Sass variables alone are called "a big improvement" even without full design tokens (Chapter 8, p. 165); tokens add value specifically where a value's scenario needs to change independently of other uses of the same raw value (Chapter 8, pp. 168–169).
- **Public documentation is optional and not the point.** Publishing Digital Foundations publicly is framed as a choice whose real purpose is internal, and "outside attention is not the core reason" for doing it (Chapter 3, p. 77); some companies deliberately keep parts private, e.g. Sprout Social password-protects internal-only content while keeping the rest public (Chapter 10, fig 10.19, p. 238).
- **Living style guides are not strictly better.** They solve drift but require designers to have "a basic understanding" of code, called "a barrier to entry" for most teams (Chapter 11, pp. 249–250); static documentation (Trello's Nachos) is offered as a legitimately "more designer-friendly approach" needing less developer support (Chapter 10, p. 230).
- **The author's own failure is named as the cautionary case**, not a hypothetical: he did not document Adobe Portfolio's design system as he built it, and says plainly "I should have" (Chapter 9, pp. 185–187).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter, then nothing else.

1. **The Foundations Model: foundations, components, patterns, then templates/pages or features/screens, as a shared vocabulary** (Chapter 4, pp. 84–94). Record: [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93) adopted a custom Atomic Design library (atoms → molecules → organisms); [ADR-0018](../../adr/0018-templates-are-a-design-system-layer-with-two-named-exits.md) later added Templates as a fourth tier, superseding #93's original exclusion — "Templates/pages stay as ordinary SvelteKit routes, not design-system artifacts" — on [#410](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/410).
2. **Templates map to product archetypes (the book's "features"/"screens" terminology for dynamic products)** (Chapter 4, pp. 93–94). Record: ADR-0018's archetype table — `EntryPage`, `OverviewHub`, `ListPage`, `RecordDetail`, `FormPage`, `QuestionPage`, `CheckAnswers` — each with a named, typed set of Snippet regions.
3. **Digital Foundations: brand, typography, color, formatting, and accessibility guidelines that precede a design system** (Chapter 3, pp. 43–68). Record: `docs/design/brief.md`, decided on [#409](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/409) — mood, color, typography, density, depth, and motion rules stated "in words and numbers" before any component is built.
4. **Naming conventions must match exactly across design, code, and documentation, and be semantic rather than arbitrary** (Chapter 8, pp. 146–151). Record: `app/src/lib/styles/tokens.css`'s header comment: "Color names follow Material Design 3's roles, used as M3 defines them," with the mapping corrected on [#417](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/417) once contrast arithmetic was actually run.
5. **A color system generates light/dark steps from a base value rather than picking colors ad hoc** (Chapter 8, pp. 152–156). Record: `tokens.css`'s five-step OKLCH surface ladder (`surface-bright` through `surface-container-highest`) plus `primary`/`primary-hover`, all derived and checked in `tokens.spec.ts`; the brief's rule that "the accent appears on primary actions and active state, and nowhere else."
6. **Design tokens are more specific and scenario-bound than a generic reusable variable, and extend beyond color to spacing and size** (Chapter 8, pp. 163–169, 174). Record: `tokens.css`'s fluid `clamp()`-based type and spacing ramp, decided on [#531](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/531) and [#540](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/540).
7. **Limit typefaces and font-weights; give each an intent** (Chapter 9, pp. 196–198). Record: the design brief's Typography section — "One family: Hanken Grotesk... There is no second family and no display serif" — decided on #409.
8. **Keep the number of text styles small, and document a use case for each** (Chapter 8, pp. 157–159). Record: the design brief's eight-step type scale (`display` through `meta`), each row naming its use.
9. **Consistent, documented spacing logic (the book's example: an 8-point grid)** (Chapter 8, pp. 174–175). Record: the design brief's Density section — the 4px `--space-*` ramp, 40px table rows, 40px form controls — and [#406](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/406)'s "compact rows, airy forms" finding that it implements.
10. **Document a system's philosophy for shadow and radius, don't leave them implicit** (Chapter 9, pp. 207–209). Record: the design brief's Depth and shape section — "No drop shadows anywhere... Radius is 8px... Borders are 1px."
11. **A component's documentation should cover every state — placeholder, hover, error, disabled** (Chapter 8, pp. 160–162). Record: [#104](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/104)'s living style guide decision, which requires "every documented variant/state (default, focus, invalid, disabled)" per component and fails CI on a missing style-guide page.
12. **A shared component library with constrained, not unconstrained, editability** (Chapter 8, pp. 143–144). Record: [#97](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/97) — typed Svelte props are the sole external configuration surface, no `class`/`style` passthrough, with a narrow, explicitly named "style hook" exception only for state-driven cascade needs.
13. **Prefer conventional, instantly recognisable components over bespoke ones** (Chapter 4, pp. 86–88). Record: the design brief's governing principle — "Conventional in pattern and behavior. Distinctive in execution" — decided on #409.
14. **A visual/interface audit surfaces rogue, inconsistent elements to be consolidated** (Chapter 6, pp. 120–123). Record: [#593](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/593)'s grep survey (`grep -n '<h[1-6]\|<p' src/routes/**/+page.svelte`), which found un-ticketed raw HTML on pages already marked "done," splitting into [#598](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/598) and [#599](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/599).
15. **The iterative approach: small, sprint-sized replacements, one area at a time** (Chapter 5, p. 109; Chapter 6, pp. 124–125). Record: [#185](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/185)'s confirmed working rhythm — "build a component, retrofit its consumers, then survey again for what to build next."
16. **Consolidating variants: ask whether each is intentional before merging; a lone consumer can stay a raw exception, but a repeated one earns a documented variant** (Chapter 6, pp. 122–123). Record: [#196](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/196), which cleared its extraction bar on two byte-for-byte identical consumers, against [#187](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/187)'s diverging pair, which did not; [#189](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/189) kept `invite`'s inline `<code>` as a single-consumer raw exception on the same precedent.
17. **A living style guide keeps documentation in sync with code automatically; static documentation risks drift** (Chapter 9, pp. 216–217; Chapter 11, pp. 248–249). Record: #104 — the style guide is local-dev only, one page per component, with a Vitest test that "fails CI if a component has no matching style-guide page."
18. **Granting designers/agents access to code, and treating code as a design tool, closes the gap between the design surface and the shipped product** (Chapter 8, pp. 175–176; Chapter 11, pp. 249–251). Record: [ADR-0019](../../adr/0019-pen-dev-is-the-working-surface-and-code-is-the-truth.md) — the canvas is the working surface, but "code is the truth... When the two disagree the code wins, and the canvas is re-imported from it."
19. **Responsive behavior belongs in a pattern's own documentation, not left to be discovered** (Chapter 3, pp. 65–66; Chapter 8, pp. 163–164). Record: [ADR-0003](../../adr/0003-css-layout-primitives-as-native-custom-elements.md)'s container-query-first "intrinsic design" default, and [ADR-0023](../../adr/0023-layout-is-intrinsic-and-320px-is-the-floor.md)'s 320px conformance floor.
20. **Document how a layout responds at different widths** (Chapter 9, pp. 208–210). Record: ADR-0023's "rendered width matrix" style-guide surface and [ADR-0025](../../adr/0025-layout-is-verified-across-the-continuum.md)'s continuum check against a drag surface.
21. **Accessibility belongs inside Digital Foundations from the start, not as an afterthought — labels, contrast, keyboard navigation** (Chapter 3, pp. 62–64). Record: `CLAUDE.md`'s standing cross-cutting Accessibility expectation, and the design brief's WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3/1.4.11 contrast floors, checked in `tokens.spec.ts`.
22. **A dedicated design library location where a whole team can access and sync shared design assets** (Chapter 11, pp. 243–246). Record: [ADR-0019](../../adr/0019-pen-dev-is-the-working-surface-and-code-is-the-truth.md)'s single committed `docs/design/doula-cloud.pen` file — "one file for the whole application," chosen because pen.dev "cannot reference components across files."

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, or company the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure.

- **Sketch as the recommended design tool, with Figma among the ones the author has "no experience" using (Chapter 8, pp. 145–146).** Reversed. Figma's Wikipedia article records Adobe's $20 billion acquisition of Figma terminated in December 2023 after EU/UK regulators would not clear it (Adobe paid a $1 billion breakup fee); Figma IPO'd on the NYSE on July 31, 2025 (ticker FIG), and the same article notes Adobe XD — one of the book's other "I've heard good things" tools — "had begun to lose market share to Figma by 2021." Sketch is not mentioned in Figma's current Wikipedia article at all (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figma).
- **Design tokens as a Sass-variable-adjacent, per-team convention (Chapter 8, pp. 163–169).** Standardized since. The W3C Design Tokens Community Group's format specification reached its first stable release, 2025.10, on October 28, 2025, with explicit support for OKLCH and Display P3 color spaces and adoption across Figma, Sketch, Penpot, Framer, and Style Dictionary (w3.org/community/design-tokens/) — this repo's own `tokens.css` is OKLCH-only, a color space the book's Sass-based token examples never anticipated.
- **Trello's Nachos design system (Chapter 10, pp. 230–232; the book's own caption already reads "RIP Nachos website").** Confirmed dead as a standalone site: `design.trello.com` now 301-redirects to `atlassian.design`, Atlassian's own unified design system (Atlassian acquired Trello in 2017).
- **BuzzFeed's Solid design system (Chapter 8, fig 8.20; Chapter 10, pp. 239–240).** Confirmed dead. `solid.buzzfeed.com` redirects to a static archive page (`buzzfeed.com/static-assets/solid-docs/`) that itself states: "IMPORTANT: Solid is no longer actively maintained, and you should not rely on it being available in the future."
- **OpenTable's color-naming documentation, "Early Girl" and "Blueberry" (Chapter 9, pp. 190–195).** The site is live but relocated: `brand.opentable.com` now redirects to `opentable.design`, which links an "OpenTable Brand Guidelines April 2025" document, so the guidelines are still maintained; whether the specific color names survive the 2025 update is unchecked — the fetched page excerpt did not surface them.
- **Salesforce Lightning Design System's design-tokens documentation (Chapter 8, fig 8.17; Chapter 9, fig 9.28).** Versioned forward. `lightningdesignsystem.com/design-tokens/` redirects to `v1.lightningdesignsystem.com`, meaning the documentation the book screenshots is now an archived v1 sitting behind a live, newer version.
- **GitHub's Primer design system (Chapter 9, fig 9.12; Chapter 10, pp. 234–236).** Still live and actively maintained: `primer.style` presents itself as "The design system for GitHub," with current Product UI, Brand UI, and Octicons sections and a linked "Design at GitHub" blog.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **All positive examples are drawn from the author's own past employers, clients, or personal projects.** Plasma (his own work with Nick Stamas), the WeWork Digital Foundations, Adobe Portfolio, and Club of the Waves recur across nearly every chapter as the worked examples (Chapter 2, pp. 27–29; Chapter 3, pp. 44, 55; Chapter 4, pp. 95–101; Chapter 9, pp. 185–187), and the book offers no example of a design system attempt that failed outright, only ones the author considers incomplete (his own undocumented Adobe Portfolio system, Chapter 9, pp. 185–187).
- **Figures without a source.** The "26 different styles of buttons" finding (Chapter 6, p. 122) and the general claim that visual audits' results "may shock you" (Chapter 6, p. 121) are the author's own recollection, uncited to any measurement methodology. Karri Saarinen's quote that inconsistency "multiplies exponentially" as a team grows (Chapter 1, p. 23) is asserted with no supporting data on the shape of that curve.
- **The "Gallery" chapter's sample skews toward large, well-resourced organizations.** BBC, Airbnb, Salesforce, IBM, Atlassian/Trello, and GitHub (Chapter 10, pp. 220–241) all had, or built, dedicated design-systems teams; the book offers no example from a one- or two-person team of the kind its own "Selling a design system" chapter (Chapter 2, pp. 35–36) says finds systems "easier to implement" — the claim that smaller teams benefit is made without a small-team case study to check it against.
- **A dated fact acknowledged nowhere in the text itself.** Chapter 10's own caption for the Nachos design system reads "RIP Nachos website" (fig 10.11, pp. 230–231) — the book, as printed, already records a case example as dead at the point it is being used as a positive model, without flagging the tension to the reader.
- **Tool-specific claims sit uneasily against the book's own stated principle.** The author states "technology moves too fast for print" and declines to make tool recommendations for that reason (Introduction, p. 14), then names Sketch as his working recommendation throughout Chapter 8 and Chapter 11 (pp. 145–146, 243–246) and builds several worked examples (fig 8.12, figs 11.1–11.4) entirely around Sketch-specific features (Symbols, Sketch Library) that section 7 above shows have since been overtaken by a different tool.
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** The claim that a living style guide risks requiring designers to code (Chapter 11, pp. 249–250) is illustrated only by one unnamed open-source Airbnb experiment rendering React components into Sketch files, generalized to "more realistically speaking" advice for any team. The recommendation to grandfather naming conventions numerically (Chapter 8, pp. 153–155) rests entirely on two examples, Google Material and the author's own prior audits, with no comparison to a named alternative that failed.
