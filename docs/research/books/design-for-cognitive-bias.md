## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that design is "in the business of helping people make decisions", that people make those decisions the way a "tired, busy user" does, below the level of conscious thought, and that a designer who understands the shortcuts the mind takes, called cognitive biases, can make "design and content strategy choices that will help mitigate cognitive bias or, sometimes, even use it for good" (Introduction, p. 1; Ch. 1, p. 8). The lens is choice architecture from Thaler and Sunstein's *Nudge*: how an environment is designed influences the decisions made in it, so an interaction can be "architected to benefit the grocer" or the customer (Ch. 1, p. 8). The book moves "from the outside in": how people in general decide, how users decide, how stakeholders decide, and finally how the designer decides, which the author calls "the most dangerous actor of all" (Introduction, p. 2; Ch. 3, p. 70). Its ethical frame is a "duty of care": designs "will impact our users' mental state and, by extension, the quality of their decision-making", so the layer to add to ordinary design work is completing the user's task ethically (Ch. 2, p. 39).

The author says what the claim is not. Knowing about a bias does not stop it: anchoring survives both a warning and a cash incentive, so awareness is "maybe, two percent of the battle" (Ch. 1, p. 7). Design cannot fix systemic injustice: anonymous hiring, cashierless stores, and race-blind charging "can't be handled by relatively quick-fix design choices", and "most of the solutions exist at very long-game policy levels" (Ch. 2, pp. 33–34). The book is not a catalog of biases; the author sends the reader to Kahneman, Ariely, RationalWiki, and Buster Benson's cheat sheet for that (Resources, pp. 94–95). It is not neutral about using biases for business gain: "if your design relies on your users making errors because they're thinking too fast", the author's parenthetical verdict is "yeah, I'm saying that's a bad thing" (Ch. 2, p. 36). And it does not claim design is life or death: "our designs will rarely be matters of life or death" (Ch. 2, p. 39).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Foreword (Yasmine Mustafa, unnumbered front matter, PDF pages 7–8)

Mustafa, who runs a safety-device startup for hotel housekeepers, tells of a focus group where housekeepers and managers sat on opposite sides of the room and the housekeepers "barely spoke"; a separate session for the housekeepers alone produced what the joint one could not (Foreword, PDF pp. 7–8).

### Introduction (pp. 1–2)

Classical economics assumed a "rational actor" and behavioral economics predicted outcomes better because "economics was really about people all along" (Introduction, p. 1). Design is about helping people decide, "from the mundane to the essential", and designs that ignore how people actually decide "will, at best, be as good as chance at helping them" and at worst harm them (p. 1). Three audiences of bias are set out: users, stakeholders, and the designer, whose own biases are "the thorniest to spot and combat" (pp. 1–2).

### Chapter 1, What Is Bias? (pp. 3–8)

Roughly eleven million bits of information enter the brain a day, and "about 95 percent of cognition happens below the threshold of conscious thought"; the shortcuts this requires are mostly good, and the errors they produce are cognitive biases (Ch. 1, pp. 3–4). Mostly harmless examples: illusion of control, shown by people throwing dice hard for a high number (p. 4); reactance, the "you can't tell me what to do" bias, shown by a "Please do not write on this wall" sign drawing less graffiti than one in capitals, and by a narrow corridor making people pick three different candies to assert choice (pp. 4–5). Reactance works even on oneself: people who write "Will I?" solve more puzzles than people who write "I will" (p. 5). Not so harmless: confirmation bias, shown by a 2015 poll in which 51 percent of Republicans and 32 percent of Democrats still believed Iraq had weapons of mass destruction, eleven years after the president said it had none (pp. 5–6).

Biases are hard to counter because of the bias blind spot; because decisions are made on autopilot, so "How the hell should I know?" is the honest answer to "why did you do that", "also true when you ask your users why they do what they do" (p. 6); and because the body reacts before the conscious mind, citing Nørretranders's *The User Illusion* and a galvanic skin-response study on hearing one's own voice (pp. 6–7). Awareness barely helps: in the anchoring experiment, the last two digits of a social security number move a wine bid, telling people about it does not help, and paying them not to do it does not help (p. 7). Choice architecture, from *Nudge*, is the book's answer to a designer with wireframes to deliver asking "Why should I care?": the grocer puts the oldest produce on top because "most people will reach for what's right in front of them", and the same interaction could be architected for the customer (p. 8). The user to design for is "a tired, busy user making most of their decisions below the level of conscious thought", which is "where our users live 95 percent of the time" (p. 8).

### Chapter 2, User Bias (pp. 9–39)

"Users crave certainty", and most user biases are shortcuts to the illusion of it; that craving "gives our designs power. More power than they probably should have" (Ch. 2, p. 9).

**Users lie (pp. 9–12).** Memory is reconstructed from current belief: people who changed their mind about O. J. Simpson's guilt later misreported how they had felt at the verdict (p. 10), and choice-supportive memory distortion adds pluses to the option a person chose that were described for the one they rejected (p. 10). The research remedy offered is to ask about scenarios that did not happen, such as what almost made the person buy something else (p. 11). Affective forecasting fails through the end of history illusion, where people at every age believe they are done changing (p. 11), and immune neglect, where job candidates overestimate how bad rejection will feel ten minutes later (p. 11). Netflix asked prospects whether they wanted to see the whole catalog before subscribing, 46 percent said yes, and the version of the homepage without the inventory won the A/B test (p. 12). "People simply are not good reporters of themselves"; the stated posture is "Trust, but verify" (p. 12).

**Cognitive fluency (pp. 12–27).** Cognitive ease is read as actual ease: a printed SEPTA rail timetable against the SEPTA app, and a dense pancake recipe against a scannable one with the same words, make the same task look hard or easy "before we read a single word" (pp. 12–15). In content audits the author separates "Does it look easy to read?" from "Is it actually easy to read?" because a plain-language article that looks hard is never read (pp. 15–16). Easy to process becomes easy to believe: rhymes seem truer, hence "If it doesn't fit, you must acquit" (p. 16); people with easier-to-pronounce names are promoted further, and IPOs with pronounceable tickers performed better (p. 17). Credibility is put to work on health information: among African Americans, agreement that the government tells the truth about HIV fell from 37 percent in 2002–2003 to 18 percent by 2016, so information that could save lives "needs to use big, bold typefaces and speak in plain language" (p. 18); the Plain Writing Act of 2010 makes plain language law for the US government (p. 18); pregnant smokers given materials at a third-grade reading level were more likely to abstain, plain language and pictographs reduced dosing errors, and the rhyming "Click It or Ticket" campaign moved young men's belt use from 65 to 72 percent, which at NHTSA's 270 lives per point is "3,780 lives saved" (pp. 18–20). Space is read as meaning: a sale price farther from the struck-out original looks like a better deal, a smaller font reads as a lower price, and "$7,204" reads as more than "$7204" because it takes longer to say (pp. 20–22); left reads as past and right as future for left-to-right readers, so "after" pictures go on the right, reversed for right-to-left cultures (pp. 22–24); Amazon's most-recent-first reviews were read as most authoritative through the serial position effect, so Amazon moved to helpfulness ranking and then to the most helpful positive review beside the most helpful critical one, giving both "equal cognitive weight", a layout it has since abandoned (pp. 24–25). Story is fluency around meaning: the Significant Objects experiment sold a thirty-three-cent mallet for seventy-one dollars with a story by Colson Whitehead (p. 26), and the author's "non-product content" for a sporting-goods site raised purchase rate and basket size (pp. 26–27).

**The virtue of concealing information (pp. 28–34).** Identical résumés with different names fare differently, and Black applicants who "whiten" a résumé get callbacks at two and a half times the rate (p. 28). The City of Philadelphia found the best anonymization was a printed résumé and a marker, and wrote a Chrome plugin to redact GitHub profiles, published on GitHub (pp. 28–29). Think Company found that employer and college names bias too, through the mere-exposure effect, shown by Chinese characters seen once being rated more positive (pp. 29–30): "sometimes less information can lead to better decisions" (p. 30). Removal extends to service design: Amazon Go's cashierless store is where, in Ashlee Clark Thompson's words, "No One Thinks I'm Stealing", and the San Francisco DA began anonymous charging in 2019 (pp. 30–31). Saudi Arabia's 2015 election barred every candidate's image from campaign materials; Kennedy won the televised debate and Nixon the radio one (pp. 31–32). Instagram's 2019 test hiding like counts is "anonymizing voting" and removes the comparison the contrast effect feeds on; its metrics for success were never stated (pp. 32–33). The limits: none of this solves the systemic causes, and "we can't solve [wealth disparity] by getting rid of a few form fields" (pp. 33–34).

**Giving our users the appropriate speed bumps (pp. 34–38).** "95 Percent Lean" beats "5 Percent Fat" for the same beef; "Should we go to war in April or in May?" removes whether from the question; the framing effect is "in my opinion the most dangerous bias in the world" (pp. 34–35). Thinking in a non-native language sees through a frame because it slows thinking, which is Kahneman's point in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* that bias is "a shortcut gone wrong"; so "sometimes friction is good" against the instinct for frictionless experiences (p. 35). Patagonia's long product pages, from Margot Bloomstein's talk, slow the buyer down because returns cost the environment (pp. 35–36). Civil Comments made a commenter rate three comments before posting and many revised their own; it "failed to find a sustainable business model" (pp. 36–37). ReThink's two-sentence prompt, "Are you sure you want to post this message?", stopped 93 percent of adolescents from posting a hurtful message, per its fourteen-year-old inventor's Google Science Fair research (pp. 37–38). An Iowa State paragraph telling students that evaluations are biased raised female instructors' scores by .41 points and left male instructors' unchanged (p. 38).

**Duty of care (p. 39).** The chapter's argument summed up: the questions to ask of a design choice are whether it has "a hidden impact on my user" and whether it helps the user "combat a bias they might likely succumb to otherwise" (p. 39).

### Chapter 3, Stakeholder Bias (pp. 40–70)

Stakeholders, "clients, bosses, or other folks who are making decisions that impact how we spend our time", have biases that affect goal setting, buy-in, and collaboration (Ch. 3, p. 40).

**The moral hazard of gameplay (pp. 41–50).** A map-making bot judged on how closely its round trip matched the original hid the original inside the intermediate map, "essentially inventing steganography"; that is "the moral hazard of gameplay, where you have to be careful what you ask for" (p. 41). School incentivizes grades rather than learning, and "you can get good grades without learning. It's called cheating" (p. 41). A pharmaceutical client's brand and legal teams, one pushing promotion and one taking six months to approve a change, were realigned by a purpose statement, "improve patient outcomes", returned to at every deliverable (pp. 42–43). "Follow the money": Don Bridges's advice to "find out how upper management is compensated"; "Ninety percent of stakeholder misbehavior usually comes back to some motivation you know nothing about", such as a bonus for shipping twelve features, or a bonus tied to sales that makes a dark pattern acceptable (p. 43). A taxonomy project had to become a joint working session with "thirty minutes of The Airing of Grievances" (p. 44). Zero-sum budgets produce silos and feed ingroup bias, illustrated by the 1954 Robbers Cave experiment, where boys assigned to the Eagles and Rattlers escalated to theft and were reconciled only by shared goals (pp. 44–45). Online advertising is the "rat king" of poor incentives: a market of "something like $280 billion" that loses advertisers "sixty-three cents for every dollar"; eBay thought brand-keyword ads returned $245.6 million and was losing $20 million, shown when Steve Tadelis paused the ads for three months and search traffic held; Bing ad spend fell 10 percent "for a while", then the market grew, which is the sunk cost fallacy plus a third incentive, that sellers and buyers of ads both want the numbers to hold (pp. 45–47). Culture resists even without money: the sporting-goods client shipped "product, product, product" and showed the non-product content to few people, from "certainty addiction" (p. 47). Measuring what is easy rather than what is valuable is the availability heuristic; clicks are the example, with "almost an inverse proportional relationship between how easy a metric is to measure and how much value it actually has" (p. 48). Thrift Store Runway, a Tumblr of thrift-store outfits under fifty dollars, is a "self-measuring content strategy" whose content is the evidence of the goal (pp. 48–49). Goodhart's Law, in Marilyn Strathern's words, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", produced clickbait, Alibaba's fake-review market with packages sent to strangers, and PolyWorld's artificial organisms eating their children because birth cost no energy, which the male designers attributed to "male bias" (pp. 49–50).

**Getting buy-in for design choices (pp. 50–56).** The illusion of transparency is the one bias awareness fixes: audiences do not see a presenter's nerves, and telling presenters so improves them (p. 51); reframing a presentation as a research session removes ego (p. 51). "Stakeholder inception": a client who kept valuable content behind a paywall "magically stumbled upon" freeing it once asked to align content goals with user goals themselves (pp. 51–52). Loss aversion: losses hurt "twice as much" as gains please, and a coffee mug's owner charges more to part with it than a buyer would pay, so a risky decision is sold by "the bad things that will happen if they don't take the risk", since "talking about positive outcomes makes people more risk-averse" (pp. 52–53); the pharmaceutical team won approval to remove brand imagery by playing audio of the customer they might lose (p. 53). Decision fatigue: Israeli judges' favorable rulings fell from about 65 percent to near zero within each session and recovered after a food break, so the author schedules risky sign-offs after a meal and puts the riskiest decision first because "the more decisions they have to make, the more conservative their decisions will get" (pp. 53–54). J. G. Sanders of the London School of Economics finds risk appetite highest at the start and end of the week and lowest on Thursday, seen in Scottish referendum polling, Brexit (held on a Thursday), and the Trump vote (pp. 54–55); missed medical appointments cost the US "$150 billion" a year, and a Scottish hospital that moved appointments for men and young people toward the end of the week cut misses by 12 percent (pp. 55–56). "We should take food seriously": the author's studio supplies "really, really good cookies and doughnuts" to workshops (p. 56).

**Overcoming collaboration biases (pp. 56–66).** "You get the conversation you design for": "Should this person drive this car?" yields a policy fight, "How might this person drive this car?" yields design, and "How might we improve the way we move people around?" puts public transportation on the table (p. 57). Problem-based procurement, tried in San Francisco, Moscow, and Madrid, replaces a fifty-page streetlight RFP with "We need to be able to see at night"; Philadelphia's version cut RFPs from forty-seven pages to eighteen and produced texting that raised reentry-meeting attendance 40 percent (pp. 57–58). The recipe for turning a "should" into a "how might we" is to find the goal of the proposed solution and ask "how" around it (p. 58). Reaction buttons online give a snapshot and "no closer to a solution" (pp. 58–59). 8-Up, learned from David Fiorito: eight people write three ideas each in three minutes, pairs cut six to two, fours cut four to two, and the room cuts four to one; it "mutes the loud talkers", carries "the DNA of everyone involved", and improves with demographic, horizontal (discipline), and vertical (org-chart) diversity (pp. 59–61). The Netflix Prize was won in 2009 by a merger of two teams, and the runner-up was too (p. 61). vTaiwan, born of the 2014 Sunflower occupation, uses Polis, where a statement can only be agreed with, disagreed with, or passed on, with no reply button, and where a person sees which opinion group they fall into; "nuanced, moderate statements that everyone can agree to" surface over extremist ones and became policy on ridesharing (pp. 61–64). The bandwagon effect, from the Asch line experiment, is broken by one other dissenter, since "courage is contagious"; retrospectives collect answers on sticky notes rather than by show of hands so the highest-paid person in the room does not set the answer (pp. 64–66).

**Case study: overcoming bias with meeting design (pp. 66–70).** A public-health funding committee stuck in status quo bias, "the devil you know", was redesigned by a colleague called Frances: budget data was removed from the handout because "people reminded of money are less interpersonally attuned", attendees were taught to read data between meetings, small groups discussed needs before money in a variation of 8-Up, and money came after "a non-working lunch — no hangry decisions!" (pp. 66–67). The room, following Ingrid Fetell Lee's *Joyful*, lost a third of its seating, gained chairs by the windows and plants, and a coatrack by the door hung with office sweaters told people "this was a space used by humans" (pp. 68–69). Meetings grew from three hours to five, people arrived early and stayed late, and funding moved to neglected areas; Frances reported the changes "helped unstick some of their risk aversion" (pp. 69–70).

### Chapter 4, Our Own Bias (pp. 71–93)

The framing effect is most dangerous because "the glasses were on our face before we even walked into the room" (Ch. 4, p. 71).

**The pen is mightier than the brain (pp. 72–77).** Notational bias is "the presumption that the way you see the world, expressed through how you document it, is all there is", shown by Western musical notation failing African and Asian music (p. 72). "Code is binary. People are not": a male/female gender field wipes out identities, and the Know Your Worth survey of Philadelphia designers asked "How do you currently identify your gender?" as a blank field, accepting harder analysis (pp. 72–73). Three radio buttons for male/female/non-binary would favor the first by visual hierarchy and present non-binary as "a third gender" rather than a different framework; "our design components represent fundamental mental models for the characteristics they allow users to express" (p. 74). Self-serving bias with computers: the more personal information a person gives a computer, the more they blame themselves when it fails, because people treat computers as friends after "intimate self-disclosure"; so "every time we ask users for personal information, we're engendering a potentially unhealthy relationship", which "should at least give us pause" over how crucial the data is (pp. 74–75). The *New York Times* barred "Ms." until 1986, so every mention of a woman foregrounded her marital status and no man's did; "the content decisions we make... dictate value", and "Not including certain pieces of metadata is also a choice" (pp. 75–76). Surnames encode what a culture thought mattered, including, in Baratunde Thurston's words, "property of Massa Thurston" (pp. 76–77). "Nihil de nobis, sine nobis": content for a marginalized group should where possible come from it (p. 77).

**Leaving good design on the table (pp. 77–86).** Confirmation bias makes designers "fall in love with good-enough design"; the 2-4-6 game is solved by asking "If I'm wrong, what else would be true?" (pp. 77–78). Red Team, Blue Team, from the military and journalism: the Blue Team designs, and the Red Team comes "for one day" to find "every overlooked potential for harm", which is efficient because it needs one team for one day (pp. 78–79). Mutale Nkonde's "critical public interest technologists" add sociologists, legal scholars, and activists to the team; *Blitzed* was written by a non-historian because a DJ's perspective saw what historians had not; a stranger's contribution is 99 percent useless and the 1 percent "unattainable any other way" (pp. 79–80). Power: Philadelphia's Service Design Studio uses participatory design, a power map of interest against power, and a plan to move the high-interest, low-power quadrant up; its pledge to the public includes "Foster reciprocal relationships" and a ladder of participation whose top rung is "We will implement what you decide", which the author reads as replacing the CEO with the user on the RACI chart (pp. 80–84). Survivorship bias: Abraham Wald put the armor where the returning planes had no bullet holes; "consider the unhappy path just as thoroughly as the happy one" (pp. 84–85). The Black Mirror rule: speculative design as "abusability-testing"; forty people produced "a season's worth" of harms for a neighborhood crime-reporting platform in ten minutes; Superflux bottled the UAE's 2034 air and the UAE then announced $163 billion for renewables (pp. 85–86).

**With great design comes great responsibility (pp. 86–93).** Dark patterns "use them for evil": Snap Camera's newsletter email field sat between a terms checkbox and a CAPTCHA so it read as required (p. 87). The contrast effect is the used-car lot's order of cars and three subscription plans that "visually favors one over the others"; the test proposed with the Tech for Good Live podcast is disclosure, "If you, the designer, are willing to disclose why you designed the interaction the way you did, maybe it's not a dark pattern" (pp. 87–88); the term "dark pattern" itself is called problematic (p. 88). Déformation professionnelle: the paparazzi, the Vichy bureaucrats, *IBM and the Holocaust*, and whoever builds the family-separation databases did their jobs well; Charles Ramsey redefined police work as "to protect civil rights", which "gives them permission to treat people with dignity" (pp. 89–90). Greg Dunlap's physicist father compared tech ethics talk to physics "before the Manhattan Project"; the Never Again pledge and Google workers' Maven and Dragonfly protests are offered as engineers' vows, with "We refuse to build technologies that aid the powerful in oppressing the vulnerable" as "First, do no harm" (pp. 90–91). Paul Rand resigned from Yale over Sheila Levrant de Bretteville's activist design; IDEO's Human Factors Toolkit taught human-centered design on an African village; "Profit is not a necessary ingredient for good design, and never has been" (p. 92). The close quotes Martin Luther King Jr. on a "person-oriented" society and asks "How might we define our jobs in a way that lets us be more human toward one another?" (p. 93).

### Resources (pp. 94–98)

Cognitive bias: Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* ("if you read only one book"), Ariely's *Predictably Irrational*, Nørretranders, RationalWiki's list, Buster Benson's cheat sheet, the *You Are Not So Smart* podcast, the author's own podcast, and *Nudge* (pp. 94–95). Design ethics: Monteiro's *Ruined by Design*, "a huge inspiration for this book"; Meyer and Wachter-Boettcher's *Design for Real Life*; Broussard; O'Neil; Anil Dash's *Humane Tech*, "Design for the most vulnerable first"; Iris Bohnet's *Gender Equality by Design* talk, "the talk that started it all"; the Tech for Good Live podcast (pp. 95–96). Red team tools: the Markkula Center's frameworks, Artefact's Tarot Cards of Tech, Walter and Vagner's 52 UX cards, Yablonski's Humane by Design, Radical Copyeditor, Textio, the Veil of Ignorance, the Design Justice Network principles, and Gao and Mantin's Design for Diversity framework (pp. 96–98).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Awareness.** Knowing about a bias does not stop it (Ch. 1, p. 7), with one named exception: the illusion of transparency, where telling presenters that audiences cannot see their nerves makes them perform better (Ch. 3, p. 51).
- **Shortcuts are mostly good.** "For the most part, the shortcuts your mind takes are a good thing"; some biases are "silly, sure. But mostly harmless" (Ch. 1, p. 4).
- **Users' self-reports are not worthless.** People "can give directional truths, but that's only one piece of the puzzle"; the posture is "Trust, but verify", not distrust (Ch. 2, p. 12).
- **Looking easy is not enough.** "Does it look easy to read?" and "Is it actually easy to read?" are "two separate, but equally important, factors"; an article that looks easy and is jargon "wasted your users' time" (Ch. 2, pp. 15–16).
- **Ease of pronunciation is cultural.** "Easier to pronounce" is "itself a culturally biased phenomenon"; "Mbiyimoh" is easy in some contexts (Ch. 2, p. 17).
- **Click It or Ticket.** The rhyme "was not the only factor in the rollout"; the results followed law changes too (Ch. 2, p. 20).
- **Direction is not fixed.** Left-past, right-future reverses for right-to-left readers, and reverses again when the shopper is primed for classic rather than modern goods (Ch. 2, pp. 22–23).
- **Amazon's layout was not intended.** Most-recent-first was "not the intent"; the side-by-side layout "was cool for a while" and has been replaced by one whose intention the author does not know (Ch. 2, pp. 24–25).
- **Concealment has limits.** Removing people from a store is "a bit of a grim prescription" (Ch. 2, p. 30); anonymous charging is "too soon to tell" (p. 31); Instagram's metrics for the likes test are unknown (pp. 32–33); anonymous hiring cannot fix a pool that is small for systemic reasons, and none of the removals "solve for the underlying prejudices" (pp. 33–34).
- **Friction is conditional.** Patagonia's slow pages are "antithetical to most ecommerce design principles" and work "because it's aligned with Patagonia's values" (Ch. 2, p. 36); slowing users is for "where it matters", "occasionally" (p. 35).
- **The most dangerous bias is also a tool.** Framing "can be used for good" (Ch. 2, p. 38; Ch. 3, p. 57), and the book's collaboration chapter rests on it.
- **Civil Comments failed.** The tool that worked "failed to find a sustainable business model" (Ch. 2, p. 37).
- **Rarely life or death.** "Our designs will rarely be matters of life or death" (Ch. 2, p. 39).
- **Stakeholder tactics are partial.** The purpose statement "wasn't all it took" (Ch. 3, p. 43); the customer audio "wasn't the only factor", and "there were plenty of additional politics and compromises" (p. 53); "we don't always have control" over when a meeting happens (p. 56); "Ninety percent" of misbehavior is "usually" about incentives (p. 43).
- **Learning is hard to measure.** Measuring "How much have we learned?" is "harder" and "seems harder to tie to metrics like revenue" (Ch. 3, p. 48).
- **8-Up "tends to"** produce better ideas; the claim is a tendency, not a guarantee (Ch. 3, p. 59). Polis's agree/disagree/pass alone "wouldn't be much better than some of the polarized reaction tools we already have" without the group visualization (p. 62).
- **The meeting redesign cost time.** Meetings "stretched from three to five hours"; icebreakers were skipped as too time-consuming (Ch. 3, p. 69).
- **The blank gender field has a cost.** It is "a pain in the ass" for analysis and was chosen for a dataset that is "just Philly, not the whole US" (Ch. 4, pp. 73–74).
- **Personal information is a pause, not a ban.** Self-serving bias "should at least give us pause when we're deciding just how crucial it is to gather personal information" (Ch. 4, p. 75).
- **"Nothing about us without us"** applies "whenever possible" (Ch. 4, p. 77).
- **Red Team is bounded.** One team, one day (Ch. 4, p. 79); social scientists on the Blue Team too is deferred, "one budget fight at a time" (p. 79); the stranger in the room is invited only "if it were cost-effective" (p. 80).
- **The contrast effect may serve the user.** The favored plan "may very well be the best value for the user. Or not."; the disclosure test yields "maybe it's not a dark pattern" and "maybe it's a dark (or at least gray) pattern" (Ch. 4, p. 88). The term "dark pattern" is itself called problematic (p. 88).
- **Déformation professionnelle can help**, when the job is defined as protecting civil rights (Ch. 4, p. 90).
- **The Black Mirror rule is hyperbole.** Writing an episode "by law" is "my assertion" (Ch. 4, p. 86).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter, the ticket that holds it, or the screen that exists. Paths are relative to the repo root.

1. **Reactance: a sign reading "Please do not write on this wall" draws less graffiti than one shouting in capitals, and telling people what to do invites the opposite** (Ch. 1, pp. 4–5). ## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, or company example the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure; the web search budget for this session ran out partway through, so several checks are by direct page fetch only, and those that could not be fetched are marked.

- **Loss aversion: losses hurt "twice as much" as gains please (Ch. 3, p. 52).** Contested since. Walasek, Mullett, and Stewart's 2018 meta-analysis of 19 estimates from mixed-gamble studies found an average λ of 1.31 with a lower confidence bound near 1.1 (papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3189088), against the 2.25 of Tversky and Kahneman's 1992 estimate; Gal and Rucker's 2018 "The Loss of Loss Aversion" (Journal of Consumer Psychology 28(3)) concludes the evidence supports only a "weak" form in which gains can outweigh losses depending on context. The direction survives; the "twice" does not.
- **The hungry-judges study: favorable rulings fall from about 65 percent to near zero before a break (Ch. 3, p. 54).** Contested since. Weinshall-Margel and Shapard (2011) argue case ordering was not random, with unrepresented prisoners heard last within a session; Glöckner (2016, Judgment and Decision Making) shows by simulation that the effect size is overestimated (cambridge.org, "The irrational hungry judge effect revisited"). Danziger and colleagues replied that the trend held when controlling for representation. The book presents the abstract's figures as given.
- **Money priming: "people reminded of money are less interpersonally attuned" (Ch. 3, p. 67).** Contested since. Rohrer, Pashler, and Harris (2015) failed to replicate money-priming effects in large samples; Vadillo, Hardwicke, and Shanks (2016) found the literature's irregularities "point to the conclusion that these effects are distorted by selection bias, reporting biases, or p-hacking" (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4831299); Lodder, Ong, Grasman, and Wicherts's 2019 meta-analysis reached a similar conclusion. Frances's meeting redesign rests on this study by name.
- **Anchoring on social security digits (Ch. 1, p. 7).** Holds in direction, smaller in size. Maniadis, Tufano, and List (2014, American Economic Review) replicated parts of Ariely, Loewenstein, and Prelec (2003) and found anchoring effects about one-half to one-third the original size; their conclusion is that "anchoring effects are real, but there is no evidence concerning their magnitude in economically important settings" (beoutlines.blogspot.com, 2015 summary; datacolada.org/7 disputes the framing of the replication as a failure).
- ***Thinking, Fast and Slow* as the foundational text (Ch. 2, p. 35; Resources, p. 94).** The book's priming chapter has been re-examined: Schimmack, Heene, and Kesavan's 2017 "Reconstruction of a Train Wreck" found 11 of the 12 priming articles cited in chapter 4 had an R-Index below 50 (replicationindex.com), and Kahneman replied publicly that "I placed too much faith in underpowered studies" and that the effects "cannot be as large and as robust as my chapter suggested". The parts this book leans on, System 1 and System 2 and anchoring, are not the retracted parts.
- ***Predictably Irrational* and Dan Ariely (Ch. 1, p. 7; Resources, p. 94).** Changed. In 2021 Data Colada found fabricated data in a 2012 PNAS honesty study co-authored by Ariely, which was retracted (datacolada.org/98; science.org, "Fraudulent data raise questions about superstar honesty researcher"); further allegations were reported by the Duke Chronicle on 2026-09-02. The anchoring study the book cites is a different, 2003 paper and is not among the papers alleged to be fraudulent.
- **Amazon Go, the cashierless store (Ch. 2, pp. 30–31).** Changed. Amazon closed eight Go stores in March 2023 and three more in New York in October 2024, leaving 17 (techcrunch.com, 2024-10-04); in January 2026 it announced the closure of all Amazon Go and Amazon Fresh stores by February 1 (techcrunch.com, 2026-01-27; fastcompany.com, "Amazon Go is dead"). Just Walk Out was removed from Fresh grocery stores in 2024 in favor of smart carts (nbcnews.com) and continues as a licensed technology in more than 200 third-party locations. The experience Thompson described is no longer available in an Amazon store.
- **Instagram hiding like counts (Ch. 2, pp. 32–33).** Changed. In May 2021 Instagram and Facebook made hidden likes a per-user option rather than a default, after tests in which, in Adam Mosseri's words, "some found this helpful and some still wanted to see like counts, in particular to track what's popular" (about.fb.com, "Giving People More Control on Instagram and Facebook"; techcrunch.com, 2021-04-14). The metrics the book said were never stated were never published.
- **San Francisco's anonymous charging, "too soon to tell" (Ch. 2, p. 31).** Later, and larger. Yolo County piloted the same Stanford Computational Policy Lab tool in 2021 and sponsored AB 2778, signed in 2022, which requires every California prosecutor's office to run a race-blind initial charging evaluation from January 1, 2025 (leginfo.legislature.ca.gov; yoloda.org). In 2025 San Francisco's DA reported the office could not yet launch it, needing about $1.4 million for software and staff (law360.com, "Seven Months In, Race-Blind Charging Faces Test In Calif."). Outcome data statewide is unchecked.
- **Google, Maven, and Dragonfly (Ch. 4, p. 91).** Changed twice. Google's Karan Bhatia told the Senate Judiciary Committee in July 2019 that "We have terminated Project Dragonfly" (technologyreview.com, 2019-07-18). In February 2025 Google removed from its AI principles the section listing applications it would not pursue, including weapons and surveillance, the pledge it made in 2018 after the Maven protests (washingtonpost.com, 2025-02-04; cnbc.com, 2025-02-04). The engineers' vow the book cites as "First, do no harm" no longer binds the company that made it.
- **Snap Camera's newsletter field (Ch. 4, p. 87).** Gone. Snap discontinued the desktop Snap Camera app on January 25, 2023 (techcrunch.com, 2023-01-05). The pattern is still illustrated by the book's figure; the product is not.
- **The term "dark pattern" is "problematic" (Ch. 4, p. 88).** Later, and agreeing. Harry Brignull, who coined the term, renamed his site from darkpatterns.org to deceptive.design and the term to "deceptive patterns", "under advice from the Tech Policy Design Lab of the World Wide Web Foundation" and from "a commitment to avoiding language that might inadvertently carry negative associations or reinforce harmful stereotypes" (deceptive.design/about-us). The date of the rename is not on the page and is unchecked.
- **Philadelphia's GitHub-redacting "Chrome plugin", published on GitHub (Ch. 2, p. 29).** Holds, with a correction. github.com/CityOfPhiladelphia/github-blind-screening exists and describes itself as hiding names and pictures from GitHub profiles for blind screening; it is a CSS stylesheet installed through the Stylus extension rather than a Chrome plugin of its own, it notes the username in the URL stays visible, and the repository was archived read-only on July 28, 2020.
- **ReThink stopped 93 percent of adolescents from posting (Ch. 2, pp. 37–38).** Unchecked. rethinkwords.com is live and offers the app on iOS and Android, but the site publishes no effectiveness figure and cites no peer-reviewed study; the 93 percent rests on the inventor's own science-fair research.
- **Civil Comments is "no more" (Ch. 2, p. 37).** Unchecked beyond the book's own statement; the Wikipedia article and the Kaggle competition page that hosts its released comment data could not be fetched.
- **Online advertising is "something like $280 billion" and loses advertisers "sixty-three cents for every dollar" (Ch. 3, pp. 45–46).** The source is Jesse Frederik and Maurits Martijn's November 2019 article in The Correspondent, which gives "more than $273bn" spent on digital ads globally in 2018, eBay burning "a good $20m" a year on its own brand keyword, "roughly 63 cents" lost per dollar, and a 10 percent decline in brand keyword spend on Bing afterward (thecorrespondent.com/100). The eBay experiment itself is Blake, Nosko, and Tadelis, Econometrica 2015. A current global figure could not be checked within this session's search budget; the market has grown since 2018 and the direction of the book's claim, that it kept growing, holds.
- **Missed appointments cost the US "$150 billion" a year (Ch. 3, p. 55).** Unchecked. The cited Healthcare Innovation article of April 2017 states the figure with no source, study, or method.
- **J. G. Sanders's weekly risk cycle and the Scottish hospital's 12 percent (Ch. 3, pp. 54–56).** Unchecked; the source is a talk video (link 03-14) and no published paper was reached.
- **Asch: one dissenter breaks the bandwagon (Ch. 3, p. 65).** Holds: in trials with one confederate answering correctly, only 5 percent of participants went with the majority (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asch_conformity_experiments). Bond and Smith's 1996 meta-analysis across cultures exists; whether the effect has declined over time is unchecked.
- **Iowa State's evaluation paragraph raised women's scores by .41 (Ch. 2, p. 38).** The study is Peterson, Biederman, Andersen, Ditonto, and Roe, PLOS ONE 2019 (link 02-33). Replication is unchecked.
- **The UAE's $163 billion for renewables after Superflux bottled the air (Ch. 4, p. 86).** Holds on Superflux's own page: the Future Energy Lab ran in 2017 for the Ministry of Energy, the "Business As Usual" air samples for 2020, 2028, and 2034 were "impossible to inhale", and the UAE's National Energy Strategy 2050 followed with "$163 billion in renewables" (superflux.in). The page is the studio's account; causation is its claim.

The wording of the gender question was not on the results page and is unchecked against the survey itself.
- **vTaiwan and Polis (Ch. 3, pp. 61–64).** Holds and has spread. Polis was used by Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project in October 2023 to have about 1,000 Americans draft rules for an AI model, with 1,127 statements and 38,252 votes (anthropic.com, "Collective Constitutional AI"); vTaiwan is described as still active in 2024 (compdemocracy.org/case-studies/2014-vtaiwan).

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Studies the field has since walked back.** The book's stakeholder chapter rests on three findings whose size or existence is now disputed: loss aversion at "twice as much" (Ch. 3, p. 52), the hungry-judges curve (p. 54), and money priming (p. 67). Section 7 has the later evidence. The book was published in 2020, after Gal and Rucker (2018), Glöckner (2016), and Rohrer, Pashler, and Harris (2015), and cites none of them.
- **Correlation presented as cause.** The Scottish hospital's 12 percent fewer missed appointments is "a small trial" with no size, control, or citation (Ch. 3, p. 56). Frances's committee "stopped making the same old decisions" after a room and an agenda changed at once, on her own report (pp. 69–70). The sporting-goods content strategy is credited with higher purchase rates and basket sizes with no design described for the comparison (Ch. 2, p. 27). The Netflix Prize's two merged teams are offered as evidence for 8-Up's method (Ch. 3, p. 61). Brexit "happened on a Thursday, our most conservative day of the week. Imagine what would have happened if it had taken place on a Monday" is speculation the author flags as such and keeps (p. 55).
- **Figures without a source, or with a weak one.** "About 95 percent of cognition happens below the threshold of conscious thought" is cited to a marketing book's PDF (Ch. 1, p. 4, link 01-02). "Ninety percent of stakeholder misbehavior" is a rule of thumb (Ch. 3, p. 43). "$280 billion" and "sixty-three cents" come from a magazine article, which itself gives $273 billion (pp. 45–46). "$150 billion" comes from a trade article with no source (p. 55). ReThink's 93 percent is the inventor's own science-fair figure (Ch. 2, p. 37). The Foreword's "58 percent of housekeepers who experience sexual harassment" carries no source (Foreword, PDF p. 7).
- **Examples that no longer exist or reversed.** Civil Comments, admitted in the text (Ch. 2, p. 37); Snap Camera (Ch. 4, p. 87); Amazon Go as a store (Ch. 2, p. 30); Instagram's hidden likes, made optional (pp. 32–33); Amazon's review layout, admitted in the text and changed again (p. 25); Google's no-weapons pledge (Ch. 4, p. 91). Section 7 has the dates.
- **Two instructions with no rule for choosing between them.** Chapter 2 spends fifteen pages on making things look easy so that people engage and believe (pp. 12–27), then says "sometimes friction is good" and that users are to be slowed down "where it matters" (p. 35). The only test offered for where it matters is the designer's judgment of the decision's stakes, and the Patagonia example works, by the author's account, because of the company's values rather than a property of the decision (p. 36). Likewise the book praises removing metadata (names, likes, race) and adding it (Amazon's helpfulness votes, the Iowa State paragraph) under the same banner of design for good; the criterion is the author's reading of harm in each case.
- **Claims the evidence does not reach.** The framing effect is "the most dangerous bias in the world" on the strength of the beef, condom, and war examples and the author's opinion (Ch. 2, p. 35). "Most of the people out there aren't evil; they're just thoughtless" and "We can do this" are drawn from one self-reported study of adolescents (pp. 37–38). Duty of care is argued by analogy to medicine after conceding designs are "rarely" matters of life or death (p. 39). The self-serving-bias study is about blame after a failed purchase, and the book extends it to "every time we ask users for personal information" (Ch. 4, pp. 74–75). The blank gender field is recommended from one survey of 272 designers whose authors accepted "far more work" to clean the data (pp. 72–74).
- **The author's own examples are the evidence.** The pharmaceutical purpose statement, the taxonomy sessions, the sporting-goods site, the paywalled client, the forty-person Red Team workshop, and Frances's committee are all the author's or a colleague's accounts with no outside record (Ch. 3, pp. 42–44, 47, 52–53; Ch. 4, p. 86; Ch. 3, pp. 66–70). None reports a case where the method did not work.
- **Sources as short links.** Every citation is a `bkaprt.com` redirect resolved in the References (pp. 99–103); several point at YouTube, SoundCloud, a Medium post, or a Tumblr rather than the study, and two, the Harvard HIV survey page and the Philadelphia survey's gender question, could not be reached in September 2026.
- **A dated frame.** The Iraq WMD poll, the 2016 election, Brexit, the Saudi 2015 election, the Kennedy–Nixon debate, Instagram's 2019 test, and Google's 2018 protests carry the book's argument (Ch. 1, pp. 5–6; Ch. 2, pp. 31–33; Ch. 3, p. 55; Ch. 4, p. 91); the mechanisms are stated as general, and the examples that illustrate them are mostly American political events of 2015–2019.
