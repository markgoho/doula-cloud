## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that a small or medium business can be grown fast by writing a direct response marketing plan onto a single-page canvas of nine squares, and that the plan, not any one tactic, is the thing most small businesses are missing. The author summarizes the book in one phrase, "the fastest path to the money", and puts that phrase on its opening page so that readers who want something else stop there (Introduction, p. 9). The canvas is presented as an implementation breakthrough rather than a new marketing idea: the author says he collected the direct response concepts from other people, names nine of them, and credits Dean Jackson with the before / during / after structure (Acknowledgments, pp. 6–7; Introduction, p. 42). The mechanism claimed is the 80/20 rule applied to itself, which the author calls the 64/4 rule and attributes to James Schramko: 64% of results come from 4% of actions, marketing is the highest-leverage 4%, and a marketing plan is the 4% of business planning (Introduction, pp. 18–19, 22). The canvas takes as little as thirty minutes to fill in and is meant to live on an office wall as a document that is refined over time (Introduction, p. 22).

The nine squares sit in three phases of one journey. **Before**, where the reader is dealing with prospects who do not know the business exists, holds target market, message, and advertising media. **During**, where the reader is dealing with leads who have shown interest, holds lead capture, lead nurturing, and sales conversion. **After**, where the reader is dealing with customers who have paid, holds delivering a world-class experience, increasing customer lifetime value, and orchestrating referrals (Introduction, pp. 39–42; the blank canvas is the figure on p. 38, and the phase-and-goal table is on p. 42). The goal of each phase is stated as know, like, and trust in turn (Introduction, p. 42).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a business plan: his own ran to hundreds of pages, went into a drawer, and was thrown away, and only the marketing section of the process turned out to be worth anything (Introduction, p. 17). It is not large-company marketing, which the author calls mass marketing or branding and rules out for anyone without millions in budget, because a large company's priorities and budget are both different (Introduction, pp. 30–33). It is not the "bright shiny object syndrome" of chasing whatever tactic is currently hot, which the author frames as tactics without strategy (Introduction, pp. 25–28). It is not sales training: the sales chapter sends a reader wanting that to Neil Rackham's *SPIN Selling* (Chapter 6, p. 199). It is not a technical guide to any medium, and the author tells the reader to hire specialists for whichever media the campaign uses rather than learning them (Chapter 3, p. 112). And it is not a book of original ideas, by the author's own statement (Acknowledgments, p. 6; Conclusion, p. 284).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Acknowledgments (pp. 6–8)

The author opens by saying he invents little and copies what is known to work, quoting a mentor, Mal Emery, on never having had an original idea, and the Picasso aphorism about great artists stealing (Acknowledgments, p. 6). Nine named influences are listed: Mal Emery, Dean Jackson, Joe Polish, Pete Godfrey, Dan Kennedy, James Schramko, Jim Rohn, Frank Kern, and Seth Godin (Acknowledgments, p. 7). The canvas is called an implementation breakthrough rather than an innovation, and the book is declared interactive, with signposts throughout pointing to a reader resources section at 1pmp.com holding the canvas, examples, links and videos (Acknowledgments, pp. 7–8).

### Introduction (pp. 9–42)

The argument for focusing on money: nearly every business problem can be solved with more money, and a business that does not make money is a hobby; the airline oxygen-mask instruction is the image (Introduction, p. 10). Failure statistics are given as a range — some estimates put five-year failure as high as 90%, and the author says he has never seen it quoted below 50% — with the added point that the statistics count only businesses that stop trading and miss the ones that plateau (Introduction, p. 12). The plateau is explained through Michael Gerber's *The E-Myth Revisited*: a technician has an entrepreneurial seizure, starts a business, and is good at the craft rather than at the business of the craft (Introduction, pp. 13–14). Professionals in high-stakes fields follow plans — doctors, pilots, soldiers — and business owners do not (Introduction, p. 16).

Pareto and the 64/4 rule follow: 80% of effects from 20% of causes, applied to itself to give 64% of effects from 4% of causes, and the author notes the top 4% still own about 64% of wealth despite the democratization of information, which he reads as evidence that the constraint is behavior rather than information (Introduction, pp. 18–19). Leverage is named the best-kept secret of the rich, with marketing as the biggest leverage point in any business, and the Willie Sutton bank-robbery line as the reason why (Introduction, pp. 20–21).

Marketing is then defined by the circus parable — a sign is advertising, the elephant is promotion, the flower bed and the newspaper story are publicity, the mayor laughing is public relations, the booths are sales, and planning the whole thing is marketing (Introduction, p. 24). Strategy is the architect's blueprint and tactics are the bricklaying; strategy without tactics is paralysis by analysis, and tactics without strategy is the bright shiny object problem (Introduction, pp. 27–28). A great product is a customer-retention tool, and a prospect finds out how good it is only after buying, so acquisition comes first; Betamax, the Newton and LaserDisc are the examples of technically superior products that failed commercially (Introduction, p. 29).

Large-company marketing is separated from small-business marketing by two things: a different agenda, listed as seven priorities of which profit is last against a small business's single priority of profit, and a different budget, where a $10 million spend over three years is not the same strategy as a $10,000 spend that must pay back now (Introduction, pp. 30–31). Direct response marketing is offered as the alternative branch, characterized as trackable, measurable, headline-driven, targeted to a niche, carrying a specific offer, demanding a response with a capture mechanism, and running multi-step short-term follow-up plus maintenance follow-up of unconverted leads (Introduction, pp. 34–36). Its name for the economics is "money at a discount" — spend $2 in advertising, take $10 in profit (Introduction, p. 34). The canvas is then shown blank (Introduction, p. 38), the three phases are walked through with a single worked example of Tom, a business owner who downloads a free IT report, takes a free audit, buys a discounted fix, and ends on a maintenance package having referred three friends (Introduction, pp. 40–41), and the phases are tabulated with their goals (Introduction, p. 42).

### Chapter 1: Selecting Your Target Market (pp. 48–62)

Targeting everyone means targeting no one; the mass marketer is an archer in fog with a limited supply of arrows (Chapter 1, pp. 49–50). A niche is defined as a tightly defined portion of a subcategory, worked through a beauty salon narrowing to cellulite treatment and then to cellulite treatment for women who have just had a baby, with two reasons given: limited money dilutes a broad message, and relevance is what makes a prospect recognize the ad as theirs (Chapter 1, p. 51). A hundred-watt bulb lights a room and a hundred-watt laser cuts steel, which is the focus argument (Chapter 1, p. 51). The photographer with a laundry list of services is the running counter-example: a bride and a purchasing manager photographing a truck want different ads, so a list speaks to neither (Chapter 1, pp. 51–52). The niche to seek is "an inch wide and a mile deep", and a business expands by dominating one niche and then another, never several at once (Chapter 1, pp. 52, 54–55). Niching is claimed to make price irrelevant, on the analogy of the heart specialist against the general practitioner: a specialist is sought out rather than shopped, and a generalist becomes a commodity bought on price (Chapter 1, p. 54).

The selection tool is the PVP index, credited to Frank Kern: rate each market segment out of ten on Personal fulfillment, Value to the marketplace, and Profitability, and total the scores (Chapter 1, p. 56). The worked table for the photographer scores Weddings 21, Photojournalism 18, Corporate photography 18, and Family portraits 26, so family portraits is the ideal segment (Chapter 1, p. 57). A list of about fifteen questions follows for getting into the prospect's head — what keeps them awake, what they fear, what they are angry about, their daily frustrations, what they secretly desire, their jargon, the magazines they read, the one thing they crave (Chapter 1, pp. 57–58). The chapter closes on the avatar, a detailed composite of the target customer built like a police sketch, with two fully written examples running about a page each: Max Cash, 51, owner of a $4.5 million financial planning firm with eighteen staff and no IT person, and Angela Assistant, 29, his personal assistant who handles the technology and carries about $10,000 of credit-card debt (Chapter 1, pp. 59–61). Avatars are recommended for every decision maker and influencer in the target market, and the author calls them the marketing equivalent of method acting (Chapter 1, pp. 59, 62).

### Chapter 2: Crafting Your Message (pp. 65–107)

Most small-business ads are name, rank and serial number — company name, logo, a list of services, claims of best quality, an offer of a free quote, contact details — which the author calls "marketing by accident" and compares to a slot machine (Chapter 2, pp. 66–67). The rule offered is "one ad, one objective", and anything not serving that objective, including the company name and logo, is told to go (Chapter 2, pp. 67–68). Rather than selling from the ad, invite the prospect to raise a hand, which lowers resistance and builds the marketing database (Chapter 2, p. 68). A call to action must be specific, and multiple means of response should be offered because people differ in preferred modality (Chapter 2, p. 68). Inwardly focused advertising is the party guest who talks about himself all night (Chapter 2, pp. 68–69).

The unique selling proposition is the chapter's spine. A business with no reason to exist is a "me too" business whose only sales are random walk-ins (Chapter 2, p. 70). Uniqueness need not be in the product: the hipster coffee at four or five dollars against a dollar at a convenience store, and bottled water at two thousand times the tap price, are the examples that the packaging and circumstances carry the premium (Chapter 2, p. 71). The USP answers one question, why buy from you rather than your nearest competitor, and the test is whether removing the name and logo would leave the business recognizable (Chapter 2, p. 72). Quality and service are rejected as USPs on two grounds: they are expectations, and a buyer learns them only after purchase (Chapter 2, p. 72). If a prospect can make an apples-to-apples comparison it comes down to price, so the USP's job is to force an apples-to-oranges one (Chapter 2, p. 72). The two questions to answer are why should they buy and why should they buy from me (Chapter 2, p. 73). What a prospect wants is rarely the thing sold but the result of it: a $50,000 watch buys status, and a printer should stop selling business cards and start selling a paid printing audit that repositions him as a trusted advisor (Chapter 2, pp. 75–76). The prospect's three options are to buy from you, buy from a competitor, or do nothing, and the author says inertia is the larger enemy; confusion leads to lost sales (Chapter 2, p. 76).

Being remarkable is presented as achievable with a commodity, with three examples: latte art at close to zero extra cost and a possible fifty cents a cup of pure profit; CD Baby's order confirmation email, reproduced in full, which Derek Sivers credits with thousands of new customers; and the iPod sold as a thousand songs in a pocket rather than as five gigabytes (Chapter 2, pp. 77–80). Lowest price is rejected as a USP because a small business cannot guarantee to beat Costco or Walmart on everything, and because higher prices attract a better class of client (Chapter 2, p. 81). The elevator pitch is given a formula — you know [problem], well what we do is [solution], in fact [proof] — with three worked examples in insurance, electrical engineering and website development (Chapter 2, pp. 82–84).

Crafting the offer gets two questions (which product do you have most confidence in delivering, and which do you most enjoy delivering) plus eight supplemental ones, and a warning that a lazy offer of ten or twenty percent off is where campaigns fail (Chapter 2, pp. 85–86). Asking the market what it wants must be supplemented by observing it, because people buy with emotion and justify with logic, and the Ford line about faster horses is quoted; Amazon and eBay trends, the Google AdWords Keyword Planner, and social and industry trending topics are the named research tools (Chapter 2, pp. 87–88). The irresistible offer has eight named elements: value, language, reason why, value stacking with bonuses worth more than the main offer, upsells, payment plans (a $5,000 item as twelve payments of $497, which totals almost $6,000 and covers finance costs while incentivizing lump-sum payers with a discount), an outrageous guarantee, and scarcity with a stated reason for it (Chapter 2, pp. 89–91). Targeting pain is preferred to features and benefits, on the pharmacy analogy: a person with a headache does not shop around, and people pay more for a cure than for prevention (Chapter 2, pp. 92–93).

Copywriting is called the master skill, described as "salesmanship in print", and contrasted with professional-sounding copy; the news talking head is the evidence that people respond to personality (Chapter 2, pp. 94–96). A short list of compelling words is given (free, you, save, results, health, love, proven, money, new, easy, safety, guaranteed, discovery), and the five major motivators are named as fear, love, greed, guilt and pride (Chapter 2, pp. 98–99). A dozen historic headlines are reprinted as examples, from the piano ad to the one-legged golfer (Chapter 2, pp. 99–100), and the author answers the manipulation objection with the surgeon's knife analogy (Chapter 2, p. 100). Entering the conversation in the prospect's mind is developed through the amygdala: risk evaluation happens whether or not the marketer participates, objections are rarely voiced aloud, and copy should say who the product is *not* for, for three reasons — filtering, credibility, and making the right prospects feel targeted (Chapter 2, pp. 101–103). The enemy in common is the second device: a list of things people blame is given, a survey on cost-of-living pressure is shown as a figure, and the Journal of Safety Research is cited for 74% of Americans believing themselves above-average drivers against 1% below (Chapter 2, pp. 103–104, and the footnote on p. 107 gives the citation as volume 34, 2003). Never blame the prospect; side with them against the blamed thing, with a sample tax headline (Chapter 2, p. 105). The chapter ends on naming: if the name needs explaining it fails, title should equal content, "Fast Plumbing Repairs" beats "Aqua Solutions", big brands with abstract names bought that meaning with hundreds of millions in advertising, clarity beats cleverness, and friends and family are the wrong people to test a name on (Chapter 2, pp. 106–107).

### Chapter 3: Reaching Prospects with Advertising Media (pp. 110–132)

Media is called the most expensive component of the marketing spend and the bridge between offer and market (Chapter 3, p. 111). The Wanamaker line about half of advertising being wasted is quoted and then declared inexcusable now, given toll-free numbers, website analytics and coupon codes; not tracking is the mark of an amateur (Chapter 3, p. 111). The advice on media technique is to hire specialists rather than learn each medium (Chapter 3, p. 112). Questions about a good response rate or open rate are refused: the answer is that it depends, sometimes 50% is a disaster and sometimes 0.01% is a success, and the real question is return on investment (Chapter 3, p. 112). A worked example follows: 100 letters at $300, a 10% response, a 20% close, two customers, so a customer acquisition cost of $150 — a loss at $100 profit per sale and a win at $600 (Chapter 3, pp. 113–114). The conclusion drawn is that response and conversion rates are in themselves useless and exist only to produce acquisition cost and lifetime value (Chapter 3, pp. 114–115). Niching lowers acquisition cost because a targeted ad wastes less and costs less (Chapter 3, p. 114). Front end is the money made on the first transaction and back end is the money made afterwards; the front end should generally cover acquisition cost, and going negative on the front end is for businesses that know their lifetime value, often subscription ones (Chapter 3, pp. 115–116).

Social media is put in its place as a medium, not a strategy, inside a three-part frame of market, message and media, all three of which must be right (Chapter 3, pp. 117–118). It is not an ideal selling environment — the analogy is the relative at a party pitching supplements — but it is good for relationships, for gauging customer sentiment, and for social proof (Chapter 3, p. 118). Two traps are named: it is a time sink, and it is free only if your time is worth nothing; and the profile is the network's property, with Facebook's change to organic reach on business pages as the example of the rug being pulled (Chapter 3, p. 119). The author's stated preference is to own the assets — websites, blogs, email lists — and use social media only to drive traffic to them, and he says he would rather have a thousand people on his own list than ten thousand page likes (Chapter 3, p. 119).

Email marketing is given a section: an opt-in form should be prominent, the list is a marketing asset that grows in value, and a responsive list produces cash on demand and cheaply tests offers before more expensive media (Chapter 3, pp. 121–122). MySpace is the cautionary example for building on someone else's platform (Chapter 3, p. 122). Six rules follow: do not spam and never buy lists, which the author says is both poor positioning and illegal; be human and write as though to one person; use a commercial email system rather than Outlook or Gmail, with ConvertKit, MailChimp, Infusionsoft, Ontraport and ActiveCampaign named; email at least monthly and ideally closer to weekly, with no hard rule on frequency; give value, at a suggested ratio of three value-building emails to one offer email; and automate sequences (Chapter 3, pp. 122–124). The three email challenges are delivery, opens (a compelling subject line) and reads, where the author says length is secondary to relevance (Chapter 3, pp. 124–125).

Postal mail gets its own section, argued against the assumption that it is dead. The author calls himself tech savvy and a co-founder of two tech startups and still regards physical mail as underused; email complements it rather than replacing it (Chapter 3, p. 126). The arguments are that physical objects move people emotionally, that Google itself mails AdWords coupon postcards, that postal mail has a long lifespan and takes effort to discard, and that postal mail has become less cluttered while email has become far more so (Chapter 3, pp. 126–127).

The budget section argues there should be no marketing budget for marketing that works: setting one implies the marketing either fails or is unmeasured, and effective marketing is a legal money printing press (Chapter 3, pp. 128–129). The objection about handling demand is answered by raising prices (Chapter 3, p. 129). A budget belongs only in the testing phase, where the instruction is to fail often and fail cheap, testing headline, offer and positioning, then cut losers and scale winners (Chapter 3, p. 129). The chapter closes on "the most dangerous number", which is one: one lead source, one supplier, one major customer, one medium, one product is a single point of failure (Chapter 3, p. 130). Examples given are businesses wiped out by a Google algorithm change, the "Google Slap" of paid-ad cost rising four to ten times, and fax broadcasting being outlawed in the United States (Chapter 3, p. 130). Seven what-if questions are listed, a Jim Rohn passage on thinking winter in the summer is quoted, and the recommendation is at least five sources of new leads, most of them paid — paid because it is reliable and because paying forces a return-on-investment discipline that free channels such as word of mouth escape (Chapter 3, pp. 130–132).

### Chapter 4: Capturing Leads (pp. 138–146)

Hunting against farming is the chapter's frame: most businesses hunt, cold-calling and discounting for an immediate kill, where a farmer plants, waters and harvests (Chapter 4, pp. 139–140). In direct response the purpose of an ad is to find people who are interested, not to sell; the responders go into a follow-up database where value is built and authority established, and the sale follows as a consequence (Chapter 4, p. 140). Selling from a one-shot ad loses everyone who was not ready that day (Chapter 4, p. 140).

The "ethical bribe" is the capture mechanism: offer something of value in exchange for identification, such as the photographer's free DVD on the seven costly mistakes in choosing a wedding photographer (Chapter 4, pp. 141–142). The arithmetic offered: $1,000 reaching 1,000 people is $1 each, of whom perhaps 100 are real prospects, so $900 is wasted; sifting first lets the same $1,000 be spent at $10 on each of the hundred (Chapter 4, p. 141). The market-temperature model follows — about 3% highly motivated and ready to buy now, a further 7% very open, another 30% interested but not now, 30% not interested, and 30% who would not take it free — so selling from the ad addresses 3% while a lead-generating ad addresses 40%, which the author states as increasing advertising effectiveness by 1,233% (Chapter 4, pp. 142–143). A side effect claimed is that immediate buyers see a business that is not desperate (Chapter 4, pp. 143–144). Educating positions the marketer as an expert rather than a salesperson (Chapter 4, p. 144). The chapter closes on infrastructure, using personal flight as the analogy — the technology exists, the infrastructure does not — against "random acts of marketing", and names the database as the center of the marketing infrastructure with a CRM as the system that manages it (Chapter 4, pp. 145–146).

### Chapter 5: Nurturing Leads (pp. 149–171)

Joe Girard opens the chapter: the Guinness World Records "world's greatest salesman", 13,001 cars sold one at a time at a Chevrolet dealership between 1963 and 1978, an average of six a day, a best day of 18, a best month of 174, a best year of 1,425, outselling 95% of North American dealerships by himself (Chapter 5, p. 150). The mechanism singled out is a personalized greeting card every month to his whole customer list, carrying the same three-word message, with varied envelope sizes and colors, hand-addressed and stamped to get past the physical equivalent of a spam filter; by the end he was mailing 13,000 cards a month and had hired an assistant, and within a decade almost two-thirds of his sales were repeat customers (Chapter 5, pp. 150–151).

The follow-up statistics follow: 50% of salespeople give up after one contact, 65% after two, and 79.8% after three, with a footnote saying the author could not trace the original source beyond "major national research firms" and that the figures should be taken with a grain of salt (Chapter 5, pp. 152, 171). The stated principle is that the money is in the follow-up (Chapter 5, p. 152). The irresistible lead nurturing model is the figure on p. 153: a lead rises through twelve contacts to a raving fan customer, annotated with the same give-up percentages at contacts one to four, with the lead saying the seller has become "a factor" in their mind at five, top-of-mind awareness at seven, the seller as probably the only person to reach eight contacts at eight, and a stated 90% chance of being called at nine (Chapter 5, p. 153). Contact means building a relationship and giving value in advance, by email or physical mail and preferably both (Chapter 5, p. 154). The farmer's three steps are: advertise to find interested people by offering a free report, video or audio; add them to a database; and nurture continually with value and an occasional offer (Chapter 5, pp. 154–155).

Marketing infrastructure is then built out. Follow-up grunt work can be automated with a CRM, triggered by prospect action, by tracked inquiries and purchases, or by preset timers (Chapter 5, p. 156). The instruction is to market to the database until people "buy or die", which the author immediately distinguishes from pestering: the posture is welcome guest rather than pest, sending a continuous stream of value (Chapter 5, p. 156). Fifteen infrastructure assets are listed, from lead capture websites and free recorded message lines through newsletters, blogs, free reports, direct mail and email sequences, social media, videos, podcasts, print ads, handwritten notes and autoresponders to shock and awe packages (Chapter 5, pp. 157–158). The build cost is compared to building a road: most of it is up front, then maintenance (Chapter 5, p. 158).

Lumpy mail is postal mail with a three-dimensional object in the envelope; the trinkets are called grabbers and are matched to the letter's theme, such as a small plastic trash can for a letter about wasting money (Chapter 5, p. 159). The shock and awe package escalates it: a physical box sent to a prospect containing books (with bonus points for one you wrote, which the author notes he is doing with this book), DVDs or CDs, testimonials, media clippings, brochures, independent reports, samples, coupons or gift cards, trinkets, and handwritten notes (Chapter 5, pp. 159–161). The three things it must do are give unexpected value, position the sender as an expert, and move the prospect further down the buying cycle (Chapter 5, p. 162). The cost objection is answered with lifetime value arithmetic, and the author warns that good marketing cannot substitute for bad math (Chapter 5, p. 162). The competitive claim is that most competitors will not copy it because they do not know their numbers (Chapter 5, p. 163). The name and concept are credited to Dan Kennedy (Chapter 5, p. 171).

Being a prolific marketer is next: high-growth businesses make many offers, test small first, and are not timid; more compelling and more frequent offers is stated as the formula for rapid growth (Chapter 5, p. 164). The chapter closes on the three types a business needs — the entrepreneur who makes it up, the specialist who makes it real, and the manager who makes it recur — with the observation that small business owners are usually the first two and rarely the third, which is why marketing infrastructure never gets running and newsletters never go out (Chapter 5, pp. 165–166). The forcing mechanism offered is a marketing calendar with daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly and annual activities, plus event-triggered activities such as a handwritten note and a shock and awe package on an inbound inquiry, or a five-part video series on a new subscriber (Chapter 5, pp. 167–168). Delegation gets a rule of thumb: if someone else can do it 80% as well, delegate it (Chapter 5, p. 168). A Jim Rohn passage on majors and minors is quoted (Chapter 5, p. 169), and the cost objection is answered with geoarbitrage — Southeast Asia, India and Eastern Europe, hired through Upwork, Freelancer and 99Designs — with the production of this book as the worked example: written in Australia, copy-edited in the United States and Canada, cover designed in India, research assistant in the Philippines (Chapter 5, pp. 169–170).

### Chapter 6: Sales Conversion (pp. 174–199)

The opening claim is that a small business starts in negative territory rather than neutral, because prospects have been bitten before; the Pink Panther dog joke is the image, and trust is named the major barrier to a sale (Chapter 6, p. 175). By the time a nurtured prospect reaches conversion they should already be asking to buy, and needing a hard sell means the nurturing is weak (Chapter 6, p. 176). Closing techniques such as always-be-closing, the trial close and the assumptive close are dismissed (Chapter 6, p. 176).

Positioning is the chapter's centerpiece, argued through Joshua Bell: one of the finest violinists in the world, playing a 1713 Stradivarius valued at $3.5 million, played a set in a Washington subway on the morning of January 12, 2007 for the Washington Post and took $32, where the same musician on the same violin in a Boston concert hall days earlier earned over $60,000 an hour from tickets at $100 or more (Chapter 6, pp. 178–179). The conclusion drawn is that people take you at your own appraisal unless proven otherwise, with the caveat that you must be able to perform at the level you claim (Chapter 6, p. 179). The product itself is subject to diminishing returns once it is good enough, so the real profit comes from how you market yourself (Chapter 6, p. 178).

Transitioning from pest to welcome guest repeats the nurturing argument in sales terms: selling before trust is like proposing on a first date, and the conversion ratio shows it (Chapter 6, p. 180). "Hopeium" is the author's coined term for the rush of a prospect who says send me a quote and then goes silent (Chapter 6, p. 181). The replacement model is to educate, which builds trust, positions the seller as an expert, and makes selling easier for both sides; delaying the sale shows willingness to give before taking (Chapter 6, p. 182). The author's definition of an entrepreneur is given here as someone who solves problems at a profit, and the recommended self-image is a doctor who diagnoses and prescribes (Chapter 6, p. 182).

Manufacturing trust addresses the small-business trust bias: people stay with large companies because the experience is unlikely to be horrible, and a small business is judged on a cursory glance (Chapter 6, p. 184). Technology is the leveler, and the New Yorker cartoon about nobody knowing you are a dog is the image (Chapter 6, p. 184). The specific signals listed as warnings on a website are no phone number, a PO box or no address instead of a physical one, no privacy policy or terms of use, and cheap-looking design; the other items are an own-domain email address rather than a free one, a toll-free or vanity phone number, a CRM, and a ticketing system for support (Chapter 6, pp. 185–186).

Outrageous guarantees follow, opening on ice-cream tasting spoons as evidence of how risk averse buyers are (Chapter 6, p. 187). Risk reversal means the seller has more to lose than the buyer, and must be specific rather than the vague satisfaction-guaranteed formula; two fully worked examples are given, an IT company guaranteeing non-recurring fixes, fifteen-minute callbacks and plain English against a double credit, and a pest controller guaranteeing ants gone forever without toxic chemicals and a clean house against double the money back (Chapter 6, pp. 187–188). The risk is said to be near zero for a competent operator, the law may already require warranties, and most people do not abuse guarantees (Chapter 6, p. 188).

Pricing strategy is treated as positioning: price is a critical positioning indicator, Rolls-Royce and Ferrari do not price from the bill of materials, and setting price from a competitor or from cost plus a markup are acceptable starting points that leave money on the table if the psychology is ignored (Chapter 6, p. 190). Four devices follow. **Number of options**: the Iyengar jam study in a California gourmet market, where a booth alternated twenty-four flavors with six, drew 60% of passers-by to the large assortment against 40% to the small, and converted 30% of the small-assortment samplers against 3% of the large; Apple's two or three variants per product is offered as the happy medium, and a standard-and-premium pair priced about 50% apart with twice or more the value works well, especially where the premium costs little more to deliver (Chapter 6, pp. 190–192). **Reverse risk with unlimited**: an unlimited variant at a fixed price removes fear of overage, protected by fair-use terms, and people overestimate how much they will use something at the point of purchase (Chapter 6, p. 192). **The ultra-high-ticket item**: price is the indicator consumers use for what is best, such items can be a large share of net profit on few units, they attract affluent buyers, and they make the rest of the range look reasonable; the stated rule of thumb is that 10% of customers would pay ten times more and 1% would pay a hundred times more (Chapter 6, pp. 192–193). **Resist the urge to discount**: avoid it except under a deliberate loss-leader strategy, and increase value with bonuses, quantities or peripheral services instead (Chapter 6, p. 193).

The chapter closes on two sales-floor points. Everyone is in sales: the BMW service clerk who would not lend the next model up to a customer whose warranty had just expired is the failure, and the fix is making it clear to all staff that responding to sales opportunities is their job, with incentives regardless of role (Chapter 6, pp. 194–195). Try before you buy, also called the puppy dog close, breaks resistance, puts the onus on the buyer to reverse the sale, and relies on a genuine customer rarely returning something that meets their needs (Chapter 6, pp. 195–197). Finally, close the sales prevention department: cash-only signs, card minimums, refusing a card brand, and surcharges for a preferred payment method all cost more in lost sales than they save in fees; offer payment plans on high-ticket items, because people think monthly and are less attached to future money; and look for any hoop, form or process that is a roadblock (Chapter 6, pp. 198–199).

### Chapter 7: Delivering a World-Class Experience (pp. 205–234)

A tribe is defined, with the definition credited to Seth Godin's *Tribes*, as people connected to one another, to a leader and to an idea (Chapter 7, pp. 206, 234). Extraordinary businesses lead tribes of raving fans, and five qualities are listed: they wow customers, create lifetime relationships, are easy and fun to deal with, create theater, and have systems (Chapter 7, p. 206). Ordinary businesses stop marketing at the first sale; small businesses have an advantage here over large ones because they can be agile and micromanage the relationship (Chapter 7, p. 207).

Sell them what they want but give them what they need: the fitness instructor sells the appearance outcome and delivers health; the treadmill that was bought and never used is the image for a customer who fails to implement and then calls the product a scam, now with forums and social media to say so in (Chapter 7, pp. 208–209). The conclusion is that the winning businesses will be turn-key ones that carry customers through implementation, and doing so is leadership (Chapter 7, pp. 209–210).

Creating theater opens on Drucker's two basic functions, marketing and innovation, and argues innovation need not be in the product: it can be in pricing, financing, packaging, support, delivery, management or marketing (Chapter 7, p. 211). Blendtec's *Will It Blend?* YouTube series is the example, with hundreds of millions of views against a small production cost; a local restaurant's pick-up and drop-off service, which sells more of its highest-margin product, is the second (Chapter 7, pp. 211–213).

Technology's purpose is stated flatly as eliminating friction (Chapter 7, p. 215). The beachside restaurant with a year-old handwritten sign apologizing that its card machine cannot take PINs is the counter-example, and the author notes it is the payment step of all things (Chapter 7, p. 215). The iPod before and after the iTunes Store is the friction-removal example; Amazon, Google and contactless payments are named alongside (Chapter 7, pp. 216–217). The method offered is to treat each piece of technology as an employee with a job and key performance indicators, and to fire the ones that are not working (Chapter 7, p. 217).

Becoming a voice of value argues for content creation: successful entrepreneurs are predominantly content creators and "wantrepreneurs" are consumers; a few voices with firsthand experience beat many with theory; the most valuable commodity in a connected age is reputation; and education-based marketing does two jobs, positioning as an authority and building relationships (Chapter 7, pp. 218–219). A blog, a mailing list, a monthly newsletter or regular videos are the suggested starting points (Chapter 7, p. 219).

Telling customers the trouble you go to is argued from a shower-gel bottle whose backstory fills the whole label with no logo, and from Guinness turning the slow pour into a feature in the mid-1990s with a stated 119.5 seconds to pour the perfect pint (Chapter 7, pp. 220–222). The lesson is to put the backstory — how the product is made, how skills were acquired, how staff are trained — into the copy and the packaging, especially for a premium offering (Chapter 7, p. 222).

Systems close the chapter. The author credits Gerber's *The E-Myth Revisited* again, and argues the most valuable systems are replicable ones, citing Warren Buffett's preference for boring businesses that do not depend on a superstar (Chapter 7, p. 223). Four systems are named: marketing, sales, fulfillment and administration; small businesses over-invest in the last two because those carry the urgent deadlines (Chapter 7, pp. 224). A business system is documented procedures, usually checklists, collected into an operations manual, and McDonald's is the poster child, where the author worked as a teenager (Chapter 7, p. 225). Two reasons systems get neglected are that they are back-office and boring, and that they feel unurgent; the cost is a business that cannot be sold because the owner *is* the business (Chapter 7, pp. 225–226). Four benefits are listed: a valuable asset, leverage and scalability, consistency, and lower labor costs (Chapter 7, pp. 226–227). The method is three steps — identify every role as if the business were ten times its size, define the tasks of each role, and write checklists for those tasks — with a worked example of chasing unpaid invoices at 7–13, 14–27 and 27-plus days (Chapter 7, pp. 229–230). The ultimate customer is the eventual buyer of the business: the Neil Armstrong line about solving how to get back before you leave is quoted, and the questions are who will buy, why, and how they get a return; the author says you rarely make as much running a business as selling one (Chapter 7, pp. 232–233). The owner-operator who does not intend to sell is answered with the observation that circumstances change (Chapter 7, p. 233).

### Chapter 8: Increasing Customer Lifetime Value (pp. 237–263)

The chapter opens on Russell Conwell's "Acres of Diamonds" — Ali Hafed sells his farm to hunt diamonds and dies in the search while the new owner finds the mine on the farm — and the moral of digging first on your own property (Chapter 8, p. 238). A widely quoted statistic is given, that a person is 21 times more likely to buy from a business they have bought from before, with a footnote saying the author will not attempt to trace it and that the exact number is irrelevant (Chapter 8, pp. 239, 263). Five ways to increase lifetime value follow.

**Raising prices** (pp. 240–241): customers are less price sensitive than owners imagine, and some will not notice; holding prices constant is a real-terms cut because of inflation. The key to palatability is giving a reason why — quality increases, input costs, benefits already received, future innovations. Customers who leave over a price rise tend to be the lowest-value ones, and a customer won on price is lost on price. Grandfathering existing customers at the old price is offered as the cautious option, with the instruction to tell them you are doing it so it reinforces the deal they are getting.

**Upselling** (pp. 242–243): Cialdini's contrast principle from *Influence* is the mechanism — a second item presented after an expensive first feels cheaper — worked through buying a suit and then shirts, ties, socks and belts. Two factors favor the upsell: contrast, and the buyer's lower price sensitivity toward an item they were not shopping for. Consumer electronics are named as the model of thin margins on the core product and profit in accessories and warranties. A framing device is given, that most customers who bought X also bought Y, which taps the desire to fit social norms. The claim that a buyer needs a break before being sold to again is rejected.

**Ascension** (pp. 244–245): moving existing customers up to higher-priced products, as an ISP or a car dealer does. Ascension campaigns fight inertia and pre-empt a customer who outgrows the product blaming you and shopping competitors. A single pricing option is named as the error; the minimum is a standard and a premium in each category, with the ultra-high-ticket item from Chapter 6 above them, which also gives less affluent clients a contrast anchor and an aspirational upgrade path.

**Frequency** (pp. 246–248): reminders by post, email or SMS, fully automatable, for products whose usefulness expires — car servicing, massages, ink cartridges, pet vaccinations — and, for long-lifespan products such as real estate or financial planning, the Chapter 5 nurturing system instead. Giving a reason to come back is worked through a shoe store's $30-per-$100 voucher, valid only from the day after issue and expiring in six months, which the author says nearly doubled the transaction and created psychological pain at not returning; he distinguishes it from discounting. Subscriptions are the third device, with Dollar Shave Club turning disposable razors into a subscription and cosmetics, underwear, fruit, socks and pet food following; the author's own dog food arrives every six weeks. The claimed side effect is that a subscriber's price-shopping radar switches off, because no purchasing decision is made each time.

**Reactivation** (pp. 249–250): past customers have already crossed from prospect to customer, so a reactivation campaign is fast cash. Three steps are given — pull the lapsed names and filter out the ones you do not want back, create a strong offer such as a gift card or coupon, and contact them and ask why they stopped, apologizing and naming corrective action where you were at fault — with "We Miss You" and "Have We Done Something Wrong?" as sample themes.

The numbers section argues that stories obfuscate and numbers do not, using *Shark Tank* and company prospectuses as illustrations (Chapter 8, p. 251). Four key numbers are named: leads, conversion rate, average transaction value, and break-even point (Chapter 8, p. 252). The worked example is an online electronics store at 8,000 visitors, 5% conversion, $500 average transaction, 50% margin and a $90,000 break-even, producing $10,000 of monthly net profit; improving the first three by 10% each — to 8,800, 5.5% and $550 — raises net profit to $43,100, a 431% improvement, which the author labels cowboy math done for demonstration (Chapter 8, pp. 252–254). Further metrics named are customer acquisition cost from Chapter 3, and, for subscription or recurring models, monthly recurring revenue, churn rate, and customer lifetime value; the author states that a business should have a recurring element and that this needs implementing urgently if it does not (Chapter 8, pp. 254–255). A company dashboard is recommended, from a whiteboard to a real-time screen, with Geckoboard named, plus customer satisfaction rate and complaint count as candidates, and incentives tied to metrics (Chapter 8, pp. 255–256).

Polluted revenue and the unequal dollar close the chapter: not all revenue is good, and a dollar from a toxic customer is not a dollar from a raving fan (Chapter 8, p. 257). Four customer categories are given, credited in a footnote to Richard Tripp and his POV Method: the **Tribe**, raving fans who promote the business; the **Churners**, who cannot afford you in time or money, were often won with overly aggressive tactics or heavy discounts, and leave calling you dishonest; the **Vampires**, who can afford you but whom you cannot afford, consuming disproportionate resources and always needing the CEO; and the **Snow Leopard**, the rare, large, delightful customer who is a bad investment because they cannot be replicated (Chapter 8, pp. 257–258, 263). The Net Promoter Score is given as the formal alternative, with its single recommend question, its 0-to-10 scale, promoters at 9–10, passives at 7–8 and detractors at 0–6, a range from −100 to +100, positive counted as good and +50 as excellent (Chapter 8, pp. 258–259). Firing problem customers follows: the customer is not always right, but the right customer is (Chapter 8, p. 260). Legitimate complainers are explicitly excluded and called valuable intelligence assets who surface weaknesses other silent leavers never report (Chapter 8, p. 260). The author states that across every business he has been in, low-value price-sensitive customers complain most, waste the most time and must be chased for payment (Chapter 8, p. 261). The benefits claimed are freed resources for tribe members, and scarcity created without being disingenuous; the closing suggestion is to send problem customers to competitors (Chapter 8, pp. 261–262).

### Chapter 9: Orchestrating and Stimulating Referrals (pp. 266–282)

Passive word of mouth is called the business equivalent of a free lunch: nice when it arrives, and a dangerous thing to rely on, since it is both a single source and one you do not control (Chapter 9, pp. 267–268). The chapter's title is used as the argument — orchestrating and stimulating, not sitting and waiting (Chapter 9, p. 267). The psychology offered is that people refer to look and feel good to their friend, not as a favor to the business (Chapter 9, p. 268).

Joe Girard returns with the Law of 250: counting signatures in funeral visitor books, then asking a funeral home owner and a wedding caterer, he concluded most people have about 250 people important enough to invite to a wedding or a funeral, so every customer represents 250 potential referrals or 250 enemies (Chapter 9, p. 269). The first strategy is simply to ask, with a scripted request offering $100 gift cards to pass on, broken down into three moves — acknowledge the customer, offer something valuable to give rather than ask a favor, and give a reason tied to a benefit they already receive (Chapter 9, pp. 269–270). The second is to set the expectation during sales or onboarding, with a second script that names about three referrals, broken down into four moves including leaving the power with the customer by making the referral conditional on good work (Chapter 9, pp. 270–271).

The bystander effect is the chapter's mechanism for why vague requests fail: diffusion of responsibility means everyone assumes somebody else will act, and the networking-meeting request to refer anybody who needs a plumber is the example (Chapter 9, pp. 272). First-aid training is the model — point at a specific person and give a specific instruction (Chapter 9, p. 273). Three things must happen in a conversation for a referral to occur: the referrer notices the conversation is about what you do, thinks of you, and introduces you (Chapter 9, p. 273). The worked example runs a financial planner from a vague request to a specific email sent to six real-estate-agent clients, hooking a redundancy-and-retirement trigger and offering a named special report for the agent to pass on, so the referral is warm, the agent looks good, and the prospect is not cold-called (Chapter 9, pp. 273–275). The instruction is to build a referral profile for each major customer group: who they know, what trigger will remind them, how you make them look good (Chapter 9, p. 275).

Joint ventures come next, from the observation that somebody had your customer before you did and paid to acquire them: a lawyer and an accountant, a car detailer and a mechanic, a pet food retailer and a vet (Chapter 9, p. 276). A finder's fee or commission is the direct route, but may be uncomfortable or illegal in some industries, so the recommended device is a gift card or voucher the partner gives away, worked through a vet handing a customer a $50 voucher for a named pet store; the vet gains goodwill, the customer gains a discount, and the store gains a customer whose lifetime value the author conservatively puts at $5,000 (Chapter 9, pp. 276–277). Four ways to monetize customers after you are done with them are listed — sell the leads, exchange the leads, resell complementary products on a wholesale or white-label basis, or become an affiliate referral partner on trailing commission — with an explicit caveat on both lead methods that customers' details need their permission (Chapter 9, p. 278).

Branding closes the book's ninth square. Four dictionary-style definitions found online are quoted and all called partial; the author's own is that a brand is the personality of a business, and he says "personality" can be substituted for "brand" throughout (Chapter 9, p. 280). Six personality attributes are mapped to marketing terms: the name, what it wears (design), how it communicates (positioning), core values (brand promise), who it associates with (target market), and whether it is well known (brand awareness) (Chapter 9, p. 280). Brand awareness is then argued to follow sales rather than precede them: Apple and Coca-Cola hustled and sold before they advertised, so the best form of brand building for a small business is selling, and branding is something that happens after someone has bought (Chapter 9, pp. 281). Brand equity is defined as the goodwill that makes customers cross the road to buy from you, showing up as loyalty, repeat business and a price premium, and as the key to the referral cycle; the Apple launch queue is the illustration (Chapter 9, pp. 281–282).

### Conclusion and About the Author (pp. 283–295)

The conclusion points at a Direct Response Marketing Lifecycle diagram downloadable from 1pmp.com (Conclusion, p. 284) and repeats that almost nothing in the book is the author's invention (Conclusion, p. 284). Three reasons entrepreneurs fail to implement are given: paralysis by analysis, answered with "80% out the door" against 100% in the drawer; inability to delegate, answered with the twenty-four-hour limit and other people's expertise; and "my business is different", answered with the claim that people are predictable across time and industry (Conclusion, pp. 284–285). Time is not money, value is: entrepreneurs are paid for value delivered and work in a results economy, while most people work in a time-and-effort economy (Conclusion, p. 286). "Playing business" — checking email, holding pointless meetings — is named as the substitute for doing business (Conclusion, pp. 286–287). Lipstick on a pig is the warning that the vehicle matters: the horse economy of 1900 New York and London is walked through its collapse by 1917, and Kodak, which invented digital photography, and Borders, which got into ebooks too late, are the modern cases (Conclusion, pp. 288–289). Taleb's turkey, fed for a thousand days and confident on day 1,000, is the model of the mindset (Conclusion, pp. 289–290). Four asset-light examples are listed — Uber owning no vehicles, Facebook creating no content, Alibaba owning no inventory, Airbnb owning no real estate — and three questions are posed about coming disruption (Conclusion, p. 290). Skunkworks projects are recommended, with the first Macintosh and Google's 20% time producing Gmail, AdSense and Google News as the examples (Conclusion, p. 291). The final pages urge the transition from business owner to marketer, on the claim that before a person buys, they know only how good the marketing is (Conclusion, pp. 292–293). The author's biography names businesses in IT, telecommunications and marketing, and a telecommunications business he grew from startup to a place on the BRW Fast 100 list of Australia's fastest-growing companies in four years (About the Author, p. 295).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** The book is for small and medium businesses, and the author says repeatedly that large-company practice is a different game with different priorities and budgets (Introduction, pp. 30–33; Chapter 9, p. 281). Mass marketing is not called ineffective — it is called effective, expensive and slow, and unavailable below millions of dollars of budget (Introduction, p. 32). Sales training is out of scope and sent to *SPIN Selling* (Chapter 6, p. 199); media technique is out of scope and sent to hired specialists (Chapter 3, p. 112); direct response is called a deep topic the canvas only helps implement (Introduction, p. 36).
- **The failure statistic is given as a range, not a number.** The author says estimates run as high as 90% and that he has never seen one below 50%, and works with the optimistic half (Introduction, p. 12).
- **Not everyone should stay in their industry.** Some industries are in decline, and a reader in one is told it may be time to cut losses rather than market harder; book stores and video rental stores are named (Introduction, p. 13). The same exception returns at the end as lipstick on a pig (Conclusion, p. 288).
- **Modeling competitors is not forbidden.** Copying what already works is called smart; the failure is copying a strategy whose priorities and budget you do not share, or copying a competitor who is himself struggling (Introduction, p. 33; Chapter 2, p. 70).
- **Niching does not mean a narrow business.** A business may offer a broad range of services, but each category is a separate campaign, and niches are dominated one at a time rather than all at once (Chapter 1, pp. 52, 54–55). Work outside the ideal target market may still be taken; only the marketing is focused (Chapter 1, p. 57).
- **Lowest price can be a USP, with a condition the author says is unlikely to be met.** It requires guaranteeing lower prices on everything against the largest discounters, and a promise of lowest prices on some things some of the time is called not compelling (Chapter 2, p. 81). Discounting is likewise allowed under a deliberate loss-leader strategy (Chapter 6, p. 193).
- **Uniqueness is not required, and is called dangerous.** The author says being unique is difficult and expensive, and that very few products are truly unique; what is required is being different (Chapter 2, pp. 73, 77).
- **Asking the market is not enough on its own.** People do not know what they want until shown it, and answer surveys with logic while buying with emotion, so asking must be supplemented with observing (Chapter 2, p. 87).
- **Emotional copy must be tailored, and has an ethical boundary.** The author concedes business and professional markets need a different approach, while arguing the technique still applies (Chapter 2, p. 94), and he answers the manipulation objection by conceding the tool can be used for harm and saying it should never be used unethically (Chapter 2, p. 100).
- **Copy is nothing without research.** Research is called the most neglected part of copywriting and the reason powerful copy fails; emotional direct response copy is no substitute for knowing the audience (Chapter 2, p. 101).
- **Response rates are refused as a benchmark.** The author declines to answer what a good response or open rate is: sometimes 50% is a disaster and sometimes 0.01% is a success, and the question is called a nonsense question (Chapter 3, p. 112). Response and conversion rates are said to be useless in themselves (Chapter 3, p. 114).
- **The author is not against social media.** He states he has used it in multiple businesses and continues to, and the limitation offered is that it is a medium and not a strategy, and that the profile is not an asset you own (Chapter 3, pp. 117–119).
- **Email frequency has no rule.** At least monthly, best practice closer to weekly, some marketers daily or more, and it depends on the target market; the author says there are no hard and fast rules (Chapter 3, p. 123). Email length is likewise secondary to relevance, and both long and short approaches are described as working (Chapter 3, pp. 124–125).
- **The no-budget rule has a named exception.** A budget belongs in the testing phase, where the instruction is to fail often and fail cheap (Chapter 3, p. 129). And the whole argument applies only to marketing that is measured and working; unmeasured or failing marketing is told to stop (Chapter 3, p. 128).
- **The follow-up statistics are disowned by their own author.** The footnote says he spent hours trying to trace them, got no further than "major national research firms", and that they should be taken with a grain of salt, while asserting the direction is right from experience (Chapter 5, p. 171). The 21-times statistic gets a similar footnote saying he will not attempt to trace it and the exact number is irrelevant (Chapter 8, p. 263).
- **Nurturing is not pestering.** Marketing to the database until people buy or die is immediately qualified: no pressure tactics, a stream of value, welcome guest rather than pest, and an occasional offer rather than a constant pitch (Chapter 5, pp. 155, 156).
- **Shock and awe packages have a numbers condition.** They only work if the lifetime value math supports them, the author says good marketing cannot substitute for bad math, and he exempts extremely low-margin purely transactional businesses — while adding that he does not recommend being in one (Chapter 5, p. 162).
- **Delegation is not held to the owner's standard.** The rule of thumb is 80% as good as you, and the author concedes outright that quality will probably be lower (Chapter 5, p. 168).
- **Positioning cannot outrun ability.** You cannot position yourself at a level you are unable to perform at (Chapter 6, p. 179). Product quality still matters up to a good-enough level, after which diminishing returns set in (Chapter 6, p. 178).
- **Trust-building technology is not a substitute.** The tools that help a small business look larger are explicitly said not to replace great products and service (Chapter 6, p. 186).
- **Guarantees carry a real risk, narrowly stated.** The author says an outrageous guarantee is risky only if you consistently do a poor job, and concedes some people abuse guarantees while arguing the net is favorable (Chapter 6, pp. 188–189).
- **Choice is not maximized or minimized.** Two or three variants is offered as the happy medium between too few and brain overload, so the jam study is not read as an argument for one option (Chapter 6, p. 191). A single pricing option is separately named as an error (Chapter 8, p. 244).
- **Unlimited pricing is bounded.** Fair-use terms and conditions are named as the remedy for abuse, and the low-risk case is specifically where the thing must be consumed in a particular time frame (Chapter 6, p. 192).
- **Price rises are conditional and will cost some customers.** They must be handled strategically, they need a stated reason why, some customers will leave, and grandfathering is offered as the option for owners who fear the reaction (Chapter 8, pp. 240–241).
- **Thin margins on a core product are not recommended.** The author describes the consumer-electronics model of profit-in-accessories and says explicitly that he does not recommend it as a strategy (Chapter 8, p. 243).
- **Reactivation is a repair, not a plan.** The author says that in an ideal world reactivation campaigns would be unnecessary, and that they exist because you will sometimes mess up or get complacent (Chapter 8, p. 250).
- **Firing customers excludes legitimate complainers.** The exception is stated before the rule: a customer with a genuine complaint is a valuable intelligence asset, and resolving it strengthens the relationship (Chapter 8, p. 260).
- **The metrics example is labeled as arithmetic for illustration.** The 431% figure is called a very simplistic example using cowboy math for demonstration (Chapter 8, p. 254).
- **Passive word of mouth is not dismissed.** It is called great, and the objection is that it is slow, unreliable, uncontrolled and a single source (Chapter 9, p. 267).
- **Selling or exchanging leads is fenced.** Both carry an explicit caveat that a customer's details need that customer's explicit permission, and the author adds that paying for leads may not be legal in some industries (Chapter 9, pp. 277–278).
- **Branding is not rejected, only sequenced.** Brand equity is treated as real and valuable, and the limit placed on it is that it follows sales rather than being bought with awareness campaigns before them (Chapter 9, pp. 281–282).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, company or study the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure; the session's web-search budget was exhausted partway through this pass, so several items below could only be checked by fetching a known URL, and the ones that needed a search are marked unchecked for that reason.

- **The reader resources at 1pmp.com (Acknowledgments, p. 8, and signposts on pp. 39, 42, 100, 179, 212, 238, 284).** The domain still resolves but no longer serves a site of its own: `1pmp.com` returns a 302 to `leanmarketing.com/1pmp-book-resources`, a page branded "Lean Marketing Hub" and copyrighted to Lean Marketing Group LLC. The 1PMP canvas is still offered there, now behind a registration form, alongside guides, a 20-minute call with an advisor, live trainings and a community invitation. Every signpost in the book still leads somewhere; it leads to the author's later brand.
- **Joe Girard, 13,001 cars and the Guinness title (Chapter 5, p. 150; Chapter 9, p. 269).** The lifetime total holds and is still the recognized record. The single-year figure the book prints, 1,425 in his best year, has been surpassed: Ali Reda of Les Stanford Chevrolet sold 1,582 cars in 2017, the year before this edition was published. Girard died on February 28, 2019, at 90 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Girard).
- **The Iyengar jam study, 24 flavors against 6, 60% against 40% attention and 3% against 30% purchase (Chapter 6, p. 191).** The study is real and correctly summarized, but the effect it stands for is disputed. Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd's 2010 meta-analytic review, "Can There Ever be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload", is the standard counter-source and is cited in the literature on the effect (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overchoice). The specific 24-versus-6 numbers could not be confirmed against a source that reprints them, so those figures are unchecked; the existence of the meta-analytic challenge is not.
- **Joshua Bell's subway experiment, January 12, 2007, $32 (Chapter 6, pp. 178–179).** Holds, with a correction to the number and a detail the book leaves out. Bell played at L'Enfant Plaza for about 45 minutes and collected $32.17 from 27 people, out of roughly 1,097 who passed and seven who stopped; a further $20 came from the one person who recognized him. Gene Weingarten, who set it up for the Washington Post, won the 2008 Pulitzer Prize for Feature Writing for the piece (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Bell). The book says the concert-hall comparison was "a few nights earlier"; the source says three days before.
- **CD Baby's order confirmation email (Chapter 2, pp. 78–79).** The email is real and Derek Sivers founded CD Baby in 1998, but the service it describes no longer exists: CD Baby discontinued physical media fulfillment in June 2023 and continues as digital distribution only, now serving more than 650,000 artists. The company was sold to Downtown for $200 million in March 2019 as part of AVL Digital Group (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD_Baby). The email is about a CD being taken off a shelf and mailed, and nothing is taken off a shelf any more.
- **Blendtec's *Will It Blend?* series and its hundreds of millions of views (Chapter 7, pp. 211–212).** Over. The series ran from October 30, 2006 to November 10, 2020, and the final video was made shortly before Blendtec was sold to the Wasatch Group (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_It_Blend%3F). The view-count figure the book prints could not be confirmed and is unchecked.
- **Dollar Shave Club as the subscription example (Chapter 8, p. 247).** The company holds, the ownership does not. Unilever bought it for $1 billion in cash on July 19, 2016, and on October 26, 2023 sold a 65% majority to Nexus Capital Management, keeping 35%; the company moved its headquarters from Los Angeles to Durham, North Carolina in September 2025 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar_Shave_Club).
- **Infusionsoft, named as a commercial email platform (Chapter 3, p. 123).** The name is gone twice over: Infusionsoft became Keap in January 2019, and Thryv Holdings announced its acquisition of Keap for $80 million on October 29, 2024 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keap).
- **MailChimp, named as a commercial email platform (Chapter 3, p. 123).** Still operating, under new ownership and a new name: Intuit announced the acquisition on September 13, 2021 and closed it on November 1, 2021 for about $12 billion, and the product now sells as Intuit Mailchimp (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mailchimp).
- **ConvertKit, named as a commercial email platform (Chapter 3, p. 123).** The company rebranded as Kit; the rename date and current status could not be confirmed from a reachable source, so this one is unchecked.
- **99Designs, named as a hiring platform (Chapter 5, p. 170).** Acquired by Cimpress in October 2020 and operating as a brand under that owner (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/99designs). Upwork and Freelancer, named alongside it, are unchecked.
- **Geckoboard as the dashboard tool (Chapter 8, p. 255).** Live and current, with 90-plus integrations, dashboards on TVs, in Slack and Microsoft Teams, by email, on mobile and in the browser, and a 14-day trial with no card (geckoboard.com). Pricing is not published on the page fetched.
- **The asset-light quartet: Uber owning no vehicles, Facebook creating no content, Alibaba owning no inventory, Airbnb owning no real estate (Conclusion, p. 290).** The wording is Tom Goodwin's, from "In The Age Of Disintermediation The Battle Is All For The Customer Interface", published in TechCrunch on March 3, 2015; the book prints it without attribution. Alibaba's standing as "the most valuable retailer" was already a 2015 claim when the book reprinted it in 2018, and no current market-capitalization comparison against Amazon or Walmart could be reached, so that half is unchecked.
- **Google's 20% time producing Gmail, AdSense and Google News (Conclusion, p. 291).** Two of the three hold and one does not. AdSense (released June 18, 2003) and Google News (beta September 2002) are both documented as 20%-time products; Gmail is not on that list. The program's status is contested: Quartz reported it effectively dead in 2013, Google said in 2020 that it still exists, Laszlo Bock described it as having waxed and waned to nearer 10% with an impact requirement, and Marissa Mayer described it as 120% time in practice (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20%25_Project).
- **Fax broadcasting "outlawed" in the United States (Chapter 3, p. 130).** Overstated, and older than the book implies. The Telephone Consumer Protection Act of 1991 made unsolicited fax advertising illegal, and the Junk Fax Prevention Act of 2005 amended it to create an established-business-relationship exception with opt-out requirements, so unsolicited fax advertising is heavily restricted rather than banned outright, and has been since long before 2018 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junk_Fax_Prevention_Act_of_2005).
- **The Net Promoter Score, +50 as excellent (Chapter 8, pp. 258–259).** The mechanics the book prints are correct — the 0-to-10 scale, promoters at 9–10, passives at 7–8, detractors at 0–6, a −100 to +100 range. What has changed is the standing of the claim behind it. Fred Reichheld introduced NPS in a 2003 Harvard Business Review article, and scholars now question whether it predicts growth at all or measures anything a conventional loyalty question does not; Reichheld himself has warned against overuse and against tying it to compensation, which incentivizes gaming (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_promoter_score). The book prints the bands without any of that.
- **74% of Americans believing they are above-average drivers, cited to the Journal of Safety Research vol. 34 (2003) (Chapter 2, pp. 104, 107).** The effect is real and well documented, but the specific figure could not be matched to a source. The classic studies give different numbers: Svenson (1981) found 93% of a US sample placing themselves in the top half for skill and 88% for safety, and McCormick, Walkey and Green (1986) found almost 80% rating themselves above average (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusory_superiority). Neither is 74%, and neither is the companion claim that only 1% believe they are below average. The figure as printed is unchecked.
- **Five-year business failure "as high as 90%" and never below 50% (Introduction, p. 12).** The US Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes establishment survival rates from its Business Employment Dynamics program, and its entrepreneurship page states that survival rates follow a similar path regardless of birth year and vary by industry. The specific five-year figure was not readable from the page fetched, so the comparison against the book's range is unchecked.
- **Facebook's change to organic reach on business pages (Chapter 3, p. 119).** Unchecked. The change the book describes is well known, but no post-2018 source could be reached for the current state of organic reach.
- **The Google AdWords Keyword Planner as the research tool (Chapter 2, p. 88).** Unchecked. AdWords was renamed Google Ads, but the rename date and the tool's current form could not be confirmed from a reachable source.
- **The follow-up statistics, 50%, 65% and 79.8% (Chapter 5, pp. 152–153, 171), and the 21-times repeat-purchase statistic (Chapter 8, pp. 239, 263).** Unchecked by design: the author states in both footnotes that he could not trace them either, so there is no source to check them against. The same applies to the 1,233% effectiveness claim (Chapter 4, p. 143), which is arithmetic on the book's own unsourced 3% and 40% figures, and to the 10%-would-pay-ten-times rule of thumb (Chapter 6, p. 193).
- **The author's later book.** Allan Dib published *Lean Marketing* in 2024, and the resources domain for this book now redirects into its brand. Its read-back is at [`lean-marketing.md`](lean-marketing.md); [#991](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/991) lists it under "Consult, never structure".

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Figures without a source, and the author says so twice.** The follow-up percentages carry a footnote admitting he could not trace them past "major national research firms" (Chapter 5, p. 171), and the 21-times statistic carries a footnote saying he will not try (Chapter 8, p. 263). Both are then used as load-bearing arguments, the first as the foundation of the twelve-contact nurturing model and the second as the reason the back end is where the money is. The five-year failure range (Introduction, p. 12), the market-temperature split of 3/7/30/30/30 (Chapter 4, p. 142), the 10%-and-1% high-ticket rule (Chapter 6, p. 193), and the 80%-as-good delegation threshold (Chapter 5, p. 168) carry no citation at all. The book has exactly four footnoted sources across nine chapters, and two of them are disclaimers.
- **Arithmetic presented as a finding.** The 1,233% increase in advertising effectiveness (Chapter 4, p. 143) is the ratio of 40% to 3%, both of which are the book's own unsourced numbers, so the precision of the result is borrowed from figures that carry none. The 431% improvement in the metrics chapter (Chapter 8, p. 254) is labeled cowboy math by the author himself and is still given a heading in the chapter summary (Chapter 8, p. 237) as though it were a finding.
- **Correlation presented as cause.** Joe Girard's 13,001 cars are attributed to the monthly greeting card, with working hard and being likeable named and then set aside on the ground that thousands of other salesmen had those qualities (Chapter 5, p. 150) — which is an argument about a control group the book does not have. The shoe voucher is credited with almost doubling the transaction on one anecdote about the author's own household (Chapter 8, pp. 246–247). Guinness's mid-1990s turnaround is credited to the slow-pour campaign with no other factor considered (Chapter 7, pp. 221–222). Blendtec's viral success is offered as evidence that theater works, with no account of the businesses that made videos and got nothing (Chapter 7, pp. 211–212).
- **A single positioning anecdote carrying a chapter.** Joshua Bell's $32 is the whole evidentiary basis for the pricing and positioning argument (Chapter 6, pp. 178–179), and the experiment was designed by a newspaper to test whether people notice beauty in a hurry, not to test pricing. The confound the book does not address is context and timing — a commuter at rush hour in a transit corridor is not a person who has decided to buy a concert ticket — which is a different claim from the one drawn, that people take you at your own appraisal.
- **Examples that have aged out.** The section 7 pass found the CD Baby email describing a fulfillment service that ended in 2023, *Will It Blend?* ending in 2020, Infusionsoft renamed and then sold, MailChimp absorbed by Intuit, ConvertKit renamed, 99Designs acquired, Dollar Shave Club sold twice, and the book's own resources domain redirecting into the author's later brand. The book was published in 2018 and the media chapter is the part that dates fastest, which the author partly anticipates by refusing to print response-rate benchmarks (Chapter 3, p. 112) while still naming five specific email vendors eleven pages later.
- **Borrowed material without attribution in one place and with it in others.** The asset-light quartet on Conclusion p. 290 is Tom Goodwin's sentence from 2015, reprinted without a name, in a book whose acknowledgments make a point of crediting sources and whose footnotes credit Frank Kern for the PVP index (Chapter 1, p. 62), Dean Jackson for before/during/after (Introduction, p. 42), Dan Kennedy for the shock and awe package (Chapter 5, p. 171), Seth Godin for the tribe definition (Chapter 7, p. 234), James Schramko for the 64/4 rule (Introduction, p. 42) and Richard Tripp for the customer categories (Chapter 8, p. 263). The inconsistency is the weakness, not the borrowing.
- **The 64/4 rule is asserted, not derived.** Applying the 80/20 rule to itself to get 64/4 assumes the distribution is self-similar, which is a property of some power laws and not a consequence of the Pareto observation about Italian land (Introduction, pp. 18–19). The supporting evidence offered is that the top 4% still hold about 64% of wealth, which is the same distribution restated rather than an independent confirmation, and from it the book concludes that the constraint on the bottom 80% of business owners is behavior rather than information — a claim about causes drawn from a static distribution.
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** Niching is said to make price irrelevant (Chapter 1, p. 54) on the strength of an analogy to a cardiologist, whose premium rests on credentialing and scarcity that a beauty salon narrowing to one treatment does not have. The claim that higher prices attract a better class of client, and that the author has seen it across multiple businesses and industries (Chapter 2, p. 81; Chapter 8, p. 261), is asserted from experience with no data, and is then reused as the justification for firing customers. Subscription pricing is said to switch off the buyer's price-shopping radar (Chapter 8, p. 248) on the evidence of the author's own dog food.
- **A universality claim used to pre-empt disagreement.** The conclusion answers the objection "my business is different" by asserting the strategies are time-tested across almost every category and that people are predictable across time and industry (Conclusion, p. 285). That is not evidence, and it is placed where a reader's counter-example would otherwise go; the book gives no account of a market in which its methods failed.
- **Two internal tensions.** The choice chapter argues that fewer options convert better and that two or three variants is the happy medium (Chapter 6, p. 191), then the ascension section names a single option as an error and requires at least a standard, a premium and an ultra-high-ticket item in each category (Chapter 8, p. 244) — three or four, once the ultra-high-ticket item is added, with no reconciliation. And the media chapter tells the reader to hire specialists for whichever media a campaign uses (Chapter 3, p. 112) while the budget section a few pages later tells the same reader to test cheaply and iterate personally (Chapter 3, p. 129).
- **Deferred content.** The canvas, the headline list, the lifecycle diagram, the Acres of Diamonds recording, the Blendtec videos and the Joshua Bell footage are all moved to the website (Acknowledgments, p. 8; Chapter 2, p. 100; Chapter 6, p. 179; Chapter 7, p. 212; Chapter 8, p. 238; Conclusion, p. 284). A reader of the book alone has the nine squares described in prose and one figure, and the destination is now a registration form on a different brand's site.
- **An unlabeled figure and a missing one.** The national survey on cost-of-living pressure is shown as a chart (Chapter 2, p. 103) with no publication named, no date and no sample size, introduced only as one of the major newspapers. The café's latte art (Chapter 2, pp. 77–78), the restaurant poster (Chapter 7, pp. 212–213), the shower gel bottle (Chapter 7, pp. 220–221) and the McDonald's item (Chapter 7, p. 225) are all photographs the surrounding prose depends on, and the twelve-contact nurturing model (Chapter 5, p. 153) carries its give-up percentages and its 90% figure inside the image rather than in the text.
