## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that positioning is context setting for a product, that any product can be positioned in several different markets, and that choosing the market deliberately is a business decision that can be made by a repeatable ten-step process rather than left to default, instinct, or an agency (Positioning as Context, pp. 25–26, 34; Conclusion, p. 137). Context is the argument's engine: the Joshua Bell subway experiment and the "cake on a stick" that becomes a cake pop are both offered to show that the same product, framed differently, is worth a different amount to the same person (Positioning as Context, pp. 26–27, 35–37). The author's own one-line definition is that positioning is deliberately defining how a product is "the best at something" a defined market cares a lot about (Introduction, p. 15); elsewhere she describes it as "context setting" for products and calls it a "secret superpower" (Introduction, pp. 16–17). Positioning breaks into five components plus one optional bonus — competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value and proof, target market characteristics, market category, and relevant trends — and the order matters, because each is only definable against the one before it (Components, pp. 44–45, 55). Above the components sit three positioning styles: Head to Head, Big Fish, Small Pond, and Create a New Game (Step 8, pp. 97–98). The claimed evidence is the author's own record of repositioning sixteen products as a marketing executive and dozens of consulting engagements, which she calls "thoroughly field tested" (Introduction, pp. 18–19; About the Author, p. 139).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a positioning statement exercise; she describes her "intense hatred" for that template and calls her own IBM attempt an act of "malicious compliance" (Positioning as Context, p. 40; Components, pp. 42–43). It is not theory: the book gives "just enough" theory and is otherwise about getting the job done (Introduction, p. 22). It is not messaging, and it is not branding; positioning is the input that messaging is written from, and messaging is sent to a separate stage and to other books (Introduction, p. 16; After Positioning, pp. 129, 131). It is not for positioning to investors, which the author says is a different story on a different horizon (Step 1, pp. 63–64). It is not a way to skip having customers: the process assumes enough happy ones to see a pattern (Step 1, pp. 61–62).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction (pp. 14–24)

Conference organizers ask the author for a cooler topic than positioning, and her answer is that every marketing and sales tactic in use today — she names account-based marketing, growth hacking, content, video, visual search, voice search, and chatbots — takes positioning as an input, so none of the newer tactics works without it (Introduction, p. 14). The definition follows: positioning is deliberately defining how a product is "the best at something" a defined market cares a lot about, and each tactic is recast as a question that definition answers — better account-based marketing wants a better read on the best target accounts, better content wants a better grasp of value and differentiators, faster revenue growth wants an understanding of what makes a best-fit customer (Introduction, p. 15). Weak positioning is described as a wind in the face that diminishes everything marketing and sales do, and spending more on marketing behind unclear positioning is compared to speaking a foreign language louder (Introduction, pp. 15–16). The author defines positioning as "context setting" for products and reports seeing innovative solutions dismissed as irrelevant because the maker was too close to the product to see how the market framed it (Introduction, pp. 16–17). The history: Ries and Trout's 1981 *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* argued that crowded markets and marketing noise force a company to contrast its strengths against competitors, but the book gave case studies rather than a method, and its television campaigns left the impression that positioning was an agency's work done through big-budget advertising (Introduction, pp. 17–18). The author says she repositioned every product she worked on as an executive, "all sixteen of them", learning by making mistakes, and that other practitioners she met were doing the same (Introduction, p. 18). The reader is named as a startup founder or executive, a marketer, a salesperson, an executive at a mature business whose growth has flattened, or someone selling products online (Introduction, p. 19). Four signs of weak positioning are listed: current customers love the product but prospects cannot figure out what is being sold; long sales cycles and low close rates; high churn shortly after purchase, which the author calls particularly bad for subscription businesses that depend on renewals; and price pressure, because a product that looks like everything else cannot charge a premium (Introduction, pp. 20–22).

### Positioning as Context (pp. 25–40)

Context setting is compared with the opening scene of a movie, worked through the first four minutes of *Apocalypse Now*: by the time the scene ends the viewer knows the where, the who, and the tone, and can stop asking (Positioning as Context, pp. 25–26). The Joshua Bell experiment is the chapter's centerpiece: the *Washington Post* had a violinist who sells out halls at $300 a ticket play outside a Washington subway station during the morning commute, and of 1,070 people who passed in forty-five minutes, 27 gave money, 7 paused, and he earned $32.17 (Positioning as Context, p. 26). The author anticipates the objection that commuters were rushed and answers it with the Post's own interviews of people who were not rushed — a man having his shoes shined, people in a lottery-ticket line twenty feet away — who did not notice either; the shoe-shine owner considered calling the police about the noise (Positioning as Context, pp. 26–27). Her conclusion is that a world-class product, poorly positioned, can fail, and that context is what lets people decide what to ignore (Positioning as Context, pp. 27–28).

Two traps follow. Trap 1 is being stuck on what you intended to build while the product became something else: the baker who sets out to make chocolate cake has already chosen target buyers, competitive alternatives, price band, and roadmap before the flour hits the bowl, and the same batter sold as a muffin changes all four (Positioning as Context, pp. 30–32). Trap 2 is a product designed for a market that has since changed: the "diet muffin" that loses its customers to an identical muffin across the street positioned as a "gluten-free paleo snack" (Positioning as Context, pp. 33–34). The common failure in both is not positioning deliberately (Positioning as Context, p. 34). The deliberate-versus-default example is Cake 2.0, a cake on a stick, which the author calls "Frankencake" as long as it is pitched as cake and which becomes obvious the moment it is positioned as a lollipop for grownups — a cake pop (Positioning as Context, pp. 35–37). The chapter's long story is the author's own: a startup founded by database PhDs whose product found things fast in very large collections of data, which prospects refused before the pitch finished because they did not want another database; a customer told her they were a data warehouse, and after repositioning, prospects stopped comparing them with the database they already had and started buying, and the roadmap changed to match (Positioning as Context, pp. 37–39). Great positioning, the chapter closes, takes into account the customer's view of the problem and its alternatives, how the product differs from those, which customers value that difference, and the market context that makes the difference obvious (Positioning as Context, p. 40).

### The Five (Plus One) Components of Effective Positioning (pp. 41–58)

The chapter opens against the positioning statement. The author first met it at IBM as a fill-in-the-blanks template in the product release process, filled it in for a non-mainframe database, produced a sentence she calls mumbo jumbo, filed it away and never looked at it again, and offers the episode as her first encounter with "malicious compliance" (Components, pp. 42–43). Four named failures of the template: it assumes you already know how to fill in the blanks, so it captures current thinking without telling you whether that thinking is good; it reinforces the status quo, which favors the incumbent; it gives no hint what to do next, and of dozens of companies she has asked, none used the finished statement for messaging, roadmap, or targeting; and it is hard to remember (Components, pp. 43–44). What she keeps is the blanks themselves, which name the components (Components, p. 44).

The six components, in order: **competitive alternatives**, what customers would do if the product did not exist, which may be "hire an intern", "use a spreadsheet", or nothing at all, and which in business software is most often general-purpose software plus manual process; the alternative is the yardstick by which the customer defines better, so a product easier to use than a rival startup's is not easier to use unless it beats Excel (Components, pp. 45–46). **Unique attributes**, what the product has that the alternatives do not, which can be a delivery model, a business model, or specific expertise as well as a feature, with Rent the Runway's leasing of special-occasion wear as the business-model example (Components, pp. 46–47). **Value and proof**, the benefit those attributes enable, which must be as fact-based as possible; qualitative claims are too subjective to be believed, and an opinion of one's own value "does not count as proof", where data and third-party opinion do (Components, pp. 47–48). **Target market characteristics**, the customers who care most, defined as the ones who buy quickly, rarely ask for discounts, and tell their friends; industry, location, and company size are rarely enough on their own, and the worked example is an invoicing product whose best customers are the small businesses that send many invoices (Components, pp. 48–49). **Market category**, the frame of reference, which triggers a set of assumptions: describe a product as CRM and the listener assumes Salesforce is the competitor, that contacts, activities, and opportunities are present, and that the price is a monthly price per user at a similar level — assumptions that either work for the product or are fought for the life of the marketing effort (Components, pp. 49–50). The illustration is a startup whose founders, ex-lawyers, built "better email for lawyers" with no calendar; their happy customers had chosen it for secure, context-aware file sharing, so it was repositioned as team collaboration software (Components, p. 51). **Relevant trends**, the optional sixth, which tell the buyer why a product matters right now rather than what it is; the author distinguishes the two by saying a category is a group of similar products and a trend is a new and noteworthy characteristic, with Pantone's 2019 color of the year as the consumer version and "AI-empowered CRM" as the technology version, where AI alone would say nothing about what the product is (Components, pp. 51–53). The Sampler story closes the chapter: a product-sampling company whose founder says North American brands spend nearly $10 billion a year on samples with no way to target, follow up, or track, which repositioned from "online product sampling" to "direct-to-consumer sampling" to attach to the direct-to-consumer initiatives its buyers were already funding, after which deals closed faster (Components, pp. 54–55). The components are related, so the order is fixed: attributes are only unique against alternatives, value follows from attributes, the value decides who cares, and who cares decides the best frame (Components, p. 55).

### Step 1. Understand the Customers Who Love Your Product (pp. 59–67)

The circularity problem is stated first — category depends on customers, customers depend on value, value depends on differentiators, differentiators depend on alternatives, and the category tells customers who the competitors are — and the answer is to start from the happiest customers, because a survey of all customers produced muddy results where a survey of only the ecstatic ones produced a clear pattern (Step 1, pp. 59–60). The first step of the exercise is a short list of best customers, the ones who understood the product quickly, bought quickly, referred others, and acted as references (Step 1, p. 61).

Four questions are then answered in the author's own voice. What if there are no super-happy customers yet: the process assumes enough of them to see a pattern, and until then positioning is kept loose while the product is tested on a deliberately wide audience — the fishing-net analogy, where a net believed good for grouper is fished broadly until the catch shows what it is really for (Step 1, pp. 61–62). What about starting from the product: starting with features means unconsciously comparing against a set of competitors that customers have often never heard of, so customer-facing positioning must be centered on a customer frame of reference (Step 1, pp. 62–63). Should investor positioning be the same: "absolutely not", because investors buy the five- or ten-year company and customers buy relief from a problem they have now, and the word "disruption" reads as opportunity to one and as replacing an existing investment to the other (Step 1, pp. 63–64). Product or company: single-product startups are advised to make the two the same so customers have one thing to remember, with Slack, Basecamp, Twilio, Box, Zippo, and WD-40 given as large single-product companies and Crocs and Salesforce as companies that sold one product for a long time first; multi-product companies treat the two as separate but linked, and which comes first depends on the revenue mix and the sales process (Step 1, pp. 64–66). The chapter's story is Wave, which launched in 2010 as free cloud accounting for small business owners working from spreadsheets and shoeboxes, reached over 500,000 businesses by 2013, found that advertising alone would not meet revenue goals, dropped "Accounting" from the name, and repositioned as financial software with payroll and payments on transaction fees while the accounting stayed free, reporting over 4 million customers in 2019 (Step 1, pp. 66–67).

### Step 2. Form a Positioning Team (pp. 68–71)

Positioning works as a cross-functional team exercise because sales, marketing, and customer success each hold a different view of how customers perceive the product, and assembling them exposes disagreements about attributes, value, and target market (Step 2, p. 68). The person who owns the business must be in the room and be seen to drive it — in a startup the CEO or the founders — because positioning is a business strategy exercise, and an exercise that is not a team effort driven by the business leader will fail (Step 2, pp. 68–69). Marketing cannot own positioning for the same reason it cannot own business strategy: it is too broad for one silo, and the outputs flow into messaging and targeting, sales segmentation and account strategy, customer success onboarding and expansion, and product roadmap and prioritization (Step 2, p. 69). One or at most two senior people per function is enough; the author has run sessions with as few as three people and as many as twelve (Step 2, p. 70). She recommends an outside facilitator, because strategic discussions are hard to balance without one and because a facilitator can challenge assumptions that insiders treat as untouchable (Step 2, p. 71).

### Step 3. Align Your Positioning Vocabulary and Let Go of Your Positioning Baggage (pp. 72–76)

The first hour of the author's workshops is spent on definitions, so the team is not arguing about what positioning means while trying to do it; at a minimum the team agrees on what positioning is and why it matters, what the components are, and how market maturity and the competitive landscape affect the style chosen (Step 3, p. 72). "Positioning baggage" is the history of the product baked into its current position, which founders and long-time employees carry more of than newer staff, and the step is to name where that history shows — is the current market the one imagined at the start, is the terminology the original terminology — so the team can suspend its opinions for the duration of the exercise (Step 3, pp. 73, 75). The example is Arm & Hammer baking soda, invented in 1846 and sold for baking, whose sales flattened in the 1970s as packaged food rose and baking fell, and which was repositioned as a refrigerator deodorizer with packaging to match, taking product sales from $16 million in 1969 to over $318 million by 1987 and extending the brand into cat litter and underarms (Step 3, p. 74). The closing story is Clearpath Robotics, whose industrial material-moving machines were positioned as robots and so compared against stationary manufacturing robots that do none of what they do; repositioned as self-driving vehicles for industry under a new division called OTTO Motors, with white headlights and red taillights added to the industrial design and a website that looks like a car maker's, after which sales accelerated and the company raised $30 million (Step 3, pp. 75–76).

### Step 4. List Your True Competitive Alternatives (pp. 77–79)

Customers do not see competitors the way the company does, and for positioning their opinion is the only one that counts (Step 4, p. 77). Asking customers what problem they are solving is not enough: the author's database users described their problem in specific technical terms, but asked what they would use if the product did not exist, none named another database and all named business intelligence tools or data warehouses (Step 4, pp. 77–78). So the question is, what would our best customers do if we did not exist, and the answers include "use a pen and paper", "hire an intern", and, for the most innovative products, "do nothing" because the customer does not know there is a problem (Step 4, pp. 78–79). In workshops, teams are held to two rules: name only what the best-fit customers would see as an alternative, and rank the list by how common it is (Step 4, p. 79). A competitor with few customers has no mindshare with prospects and is not a real alternative (Step 4, p. 79). Alternatives cluster — "do it manually" holding the intern and the spreadsheet, "use a small-business accounting solution" holding QuickBooks and FreshBooks — and teams usually end with between two and five groups (Step 4, p. 79).

### Step 5. Isolate Your Unique Attributes or Features (pp. 80–83)

The step stays on features and capabilities rather than the value they create, which is Step 6; a feature is something the product or company has or does, such as a 15-megapixel camera, an integration, one-click installation, or metal construction (Step 5, p. 80). List everything the alternatives lack, including attributes some of the team consider negative — the worked case is a company that alone required a professional services team onsite to install, which the head of sales read as a delay and the head of customer service as a uniquely positive high-touch attribute (Step 5, pp. 80–81). Claims that are really benefits are pushed back: "outstanding customer service" and "very easy to use" are the two most common, and the author notes she has never met a company that believed it had terrible customer service, so the test is how you would prove it — more support people, certifications, a measurable time to proficiency (Step 5, p. 81). Third-party validation counts as proof, whether an independent reviewer's judgment or an approved customer quote (Step 5, p. 82). Attributes can be a proprietary process, expertise, distribution channels, partnerships, or skills, not only product features (Step 5, p. 82). The step's last instruction is to concentrate on consideration attributes, the ones a buyer weighs before purchase, rather than retention attributes such as ease of doing business and support quality, because a prospect who sees too little value to buy never gets far enough to experience the latter (Step 5, pp. 82–83).

### Step 6. Map the Attributes to Value "Themes" (pp. 84–87)

Features enable benefits, and benefits become value when put in the context of a goal the customer is trying to reach: a 15-megapixel camera enables sharp images, whose value is that photos stay sharp printed or zoomed; all-metal construction enables a stronger frame, whose value in the book's table is a frame lasting five times longer and $50,000 a year saved on replacements (Step 6, pp. 84–85, Table 1). The author, an engineer by training, says she treated features and benefits as interchangeable early in her career, and notes that consumer technology often presents a feature as valuable in its own right only because buyers have been trained to do the translation themselves (Step 6, p. 85). Where the translation is not automatic, the answer to a stated feature is "So what?", and the step is to keep asking what it means for the customer until the answer reaches a goal — a patented algorithm becomes faster response times becomes answering a customer while they are still on the phone (Step 6, pp. 85–86). The value points are then clustered from the customer's point of view — "works on any mobile device" and "works without an internet connection" grouping as support for remote environments — with the goal of reducing the list to between one and four clusters, and a single value point being not uncommon (Step 6, p. 86). The chapter closes by saying the exercise is not about every little feature customers love, but about the few that make the product worth considering (Step 6, p. 87).

### Step 7. Determine Who Cares a Lot (pp. 88–92)

Not every customer cares about the same value in the same way: an accounting integration is worthless to a customer who does not use that accounting package, and among those who do, the customers who send many invoices feel the data-entry pain most (Step 7, p. 88). Marketers call this segmentation, which the author says is even more widely misunderstood than positioning, because most teaching of it is consumer demographics and the business version becomes firmographics — company size bands and industries that do not identify a good fit (Step 7, pp. 88–89). An actionable segmentation is a list of easily identifiable characteristics that make a person or company care a lot: for consumers, other brands they own, stores they buy from, their job, their tastes; for businesses, the way they sell, what they have already invested in, and which skills they do or do not have in house (Step 7, p. 89). Best-fit customers are contrasted with the rest: the others think the product is fine for the price and will leave for cheaper or better, take their time, and ask for discounts, while best-fit customers bought quickly, sometimes said the price should be higher, tell their friends, do not churn, and are "buying" as much as the company is selling (Step 7, pp. 89–90). The instruction is to target as narrowly as the near-term sales objective allows, and to broaden only if the narrow definition cannot hit the number (Step 7, p. 90). Positioning is expected to keep evolving and does not have to fit the company of five or ten years hence (Step 7, p. 91). The method for the step is to take each value point and repeat the question, who cares and why, worked through an appointment-scheduling product whose multi-location, multi-service capability matters to financial services and tax preparation but not to drugstores (Step 7, p. 91). A segment must meet two criteria to be worth focusing on: big enough to meet the business's goals, and holding important, specific, unmet needs common to the segment — the tennis racquet for seniors needs both enough tennis-playing seniors and an unmet need in their racquets (Step 7, p. 92).

### Step 8. Find a Market Frame of Reference That Puts Your Strengths at the Center and Determine How to Position in It (pp. 93–118)

A market, for this exercise, has to already exist in customers' minds, except in the rare case of a deliberate decision to create one; positioning inside it triggers the assumptions about competitors, features, and price that the company wants triggered (Step 8, pp. 93–94). Three ways to find candidates: abductive reasoning, asking what kinds of product typically have these features and deliver this value; examining adjacent markets, with particular attention to fast-growing ones, but only choosing one that makes the strengths obvious rather than one the company wishes to belong to; and asking customers, which the author answers with "sometimes" and a caution, because customers will place a product in the market nearest their own industry or job and that is often not the market that shows its strengths (Step 8, pp. 94–96). The Wattpad story runs here: a 2006 social storytelling platform that grew with larger phone screens and the App Store, deliberately did not monetize while it grew its community, tested adapting its stories for television with a station in the Philippines whose viewership rose 30%, and in 2016 announced Wattpad Studios and repositioned as a global multi-platform entertainment company with 70 million monthly users (Step 8, pp. 96–97).

The three styles are then defined and given a chapter each. **Head to Head** is positioning to win an existing market by beating the leader at the established criteria; the buyer already knows what the category is and how to judge it, so no education is needed, but the product has to fit the existing definition to win (Step 8, pp. 98–99, 102). It suits the current leader, for whom the status quo is an asset; it is "rarely a good choice" for a small business launching a new product, which the author likens to trying to out-cola Coke; a large company may attempt it where the market is shifting in a challenger's favor; and the one case for a new product is a category that buyers understand but where no leader has yet emerged, illustrated by consumer smart glasses, where Epson and North are named as vendors and no leader comes to mind (Step 8, pp. 99–100). Entering such a category means committing to grow fast enough to take the lead before someone else does, which for technology companies often requires outside funding, and the author adds that a founder looking to bootstrap may not want to enter a market this way (Step 8, p. 101). Her own database-to-data-warehouse story is re-read through this lens: calling themselves a database announced an intention to beat Oracle, the first question from every prospect was how they were better than Oracle, and they could not credibly compete; data warehousing was an established market with demand but no clear leader, and repositioning there also let them raise prices, because a warehouse commanded premium pricing where a database was a commodity (Step 8, pp. 103–104).

**Big Fish, Small Pond** is positioning to win a subsegment of an existing market, described as selling Coke for dogs rather than out-colaing Coke: the product still has to be good enough on the category's criteria, but the argument is that a subsegment has specific needs the leader does not meet, important enough that buyers will relax on the general criteria (Step 8, pp. 104–105). Smaller subsegments are easier to reach, list-build against, and target, and an advantage compounds, because success with one law office or bank applies directly to the next and tight communities share information — the author says word-of-mouth marketing happens most naturally in tight market subsegments (Step 8, pp. 105–106). The caution is size: the test is how many deals are needed next year against how many businesses the subsegment holds, and thirty deals against a hundred businesses means looking elsewhere (Step 8, p. 106). Narrow now does not mean narrow forever — credit unions widening to retail banks and then to insurance is the example (Step 8, pp. 106–107). Four conditions: the category is well defined with a clear leader that is not you; there are definable groups with needs the leader does not address, by industry, geography, preference, or ecosystem; the subsegment is identifiable enough to build a prospect list from, which the author contrasts with trying to list "companies that value design"; and the unmet need is specific and important, where "Bic for Her" is the counter-case, a pen identical to the others but pink, which women answered with satirical Amazon reviews and then ignored until it left the market (Step 8, pp. 107–108). The work is to educate the subsegment that a general-purpose solution is not meeting their needs, with proof points and ideally a quantified gap, while still showing the category's basic criteria are met (Step 8, pp. 108–109). The standing risk is the leader turning its attention to the subsegment with a good-enough answer, or acquiring a fast-growing competitor there; the best defense is an advantage the leader would damage its broader business by copying, since Coke would struggle to make a product for dogs without harming how humans see the brand (Step 8, pp. 109–110). The author's Janna Systems story is the long illustration: an enterprise CRM startup facing Siebel, which she describes as having over $2 billion in annual revenue, summarized by one prospect as a "cheaper, crappier Siebel"; its one differentiator, modeling relationships between people, meant nothing to most buyers until an investment bank got excited about it; repositioned as CRM for investment banks over internal and investor objections that the market was too small, it used banking terms and banking demo data, raised prices, grew revenue from $2 million to over $70 million in eighteen months, and was acquired by Siebel for $1.7 billion (Step 8, pp. 110–112).

**Create a New Game** is positioning to win a market you create, and the author says it should be used only after every existing category has been evaluated and rejected, or where the company is large enough to make the case that a new category deserves to exist (Step 8, p. 112). It is possible mainly after a large change — new technology, economic change, political force — and a category often emerges when an enabling technology, a shift in customer preference, and a supporting ecosystem arrive together (Step 8, p. 113). It is the hardest style because it involves the most teaching: the customer has to be shown why the category deserves to exist, how to evaluate solutions in it, and why this vendor is the best of them, all at once, and category leaders will argue the new product is merely a feature of theirs (Step 8, pp. 113–114). A strong answer to "why now" is required, and the selling is of the problem before the solution, because customers are not yet aware they have one (Step 8, pp. 114–115). The reward is a market whose boundaries and purchase criteria are drawn around the company's own strengths, and the cost is the money and time to shift how customers think, which the author says generally suits established companies with resources or, for smaller ones, deep-pocketed and patient investors (Step 8, p. 115). The named failure mode is building the market and then losing the leadership of it to a better-funded fast follower at the moment prospects begin to understand it (Step 8, p. 116). The Eloqua story closes the chapter: a 2000 startup whose buyers called themselves demand generators rather than marketers, which named its category "demand generation automation" to buyers' satisfaction and investors' confusion, grew as that job title proliferated after 2005, raised a round only after fourteen quarters of profitability, and shifted to the broader "marketing automation" to separate itself from simple email tools, growing from around $12 million in 2006 to $96 million in 2012 when it went public and was then acquired by Oracle for $870 million (Step 8, pp. 116–117). Its founder's summary is that category creation does not come from executives inventing an acronym at an offsite but from deeply understanding a narrow set of customers whose attitudes later become mainstream (Step 8, p. 117).

### Step 9. Layer On a Trend (but Be Careful) (pp. 119–124)

The target is the center of three overlapping circles: the product's strengths, the market context, and a trend the customer base cares about (Step 9, p. 119). The author's own example is a retail sales-associate product that used retailers' interest in mobility, since access to information across devices was an obvious way for a retailer to act on that interest (Step 9, pp. 119–120). The step is explicitly optional: a well-positioned product can succeed without a trend, and the author says she has worked on fundamentally boring products that sold well, with a trend giving such products a gloss of interest at best and muddying the position at worst (Step 9, pp. 120–121). The Redgate story is the positive case: a database tools company in Cambridge, founded 1999, leader in its category with over 800,000 users, whose category shift made no sense but which wove "database DevOps" into its positioning with content and sales training at a time new data privacy regulations had customers worried, and reports a doubling of inbound leads and more multi-product purchases (Step 9, pp. 121–122). Two failure modes follow. Describing a trend without declaring a market makes a product cool but baffling: a company described its app to the author as the sharing economy for pets, then as "Uber for cats", and only on probing turned out to be a marketplace for pet services — sharing economy being a trend rather than a market, and Uber a company rather than a market (Step 9, pp. 122–123). Focusing on the trend and the market without linking either to the product is the second, and the illustration is Long Island Iced Tea renaming itself Long Blockchain, whose stock soared on headlines, then fell when no strategy, partnership, or explanation followed, and which was delisted from the Nasdaq within months (Step 9, p. 124). The chapter's rule of thumb is that it is better to be a little boring than completely baffling (Step 9, p. 121).

### Step 10. Capture Your Positioning so It Can Be Shared (pp. 125–128)

Positioning is of no use until it is shared across the organization, because it has to inform branding, campaigns, sales strategy, product decisions, and customer success (Step 10, p. 125). The author's complaint about the positioning statement returns: too brief to carry the subtleties and too awkward to memorize or repeat (Step 10, p. 125). Her recommendation is a document with enough detail for marketing, sales, and product to use, breaking down each component and showing how they interact, plus a shorter one-page version that can be circulated (Step 10, pp. 125–126). Above that sits a positioning canvas, reproduced in the book: the product name and a one-line description, the market category and subcategory, and four columns — competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, and who cares a lot — laid out to show the relationships between them (Step 10, p. 126, Table 2).

### After Positioning: What Happens Next? (pp. 129–136)

The question the author gets at the end of a workshop is what to do next, and her answer is that although new messaging is the obvious next step, it is more effective to craft a "sales story" first (After Positioning, p. 129). The sales story follows a common arc for business selling: define the problem the solution was built for; describe how customers try to solve it today and where those attempts fall short; describe "the perfect world", the features a perfect solution would have given the problem and the limits of current answers; introduce the product and place it in its market category; then walk the value themes with detail on how the solution delivers them, before saying what the prospect should do next (After Positioning, pp. 130–131). The point of doing it as a team is that everyone agrees how the positioning turns into a pitch, which requires agreeing on the problem, the current solutions, the gap, and the purchase criteria (After Positioning, p. 131). Messaging is not a group exercise in real time; the marketing team translates the story arc, and the author's one tip is to write a master messaging document, because without a fixed baseline each piece of copy is built on the last and both the messaging and the positioning creep (After Positioning, pp. 131–132). Roadmap and pricing follow: the roadmap for "accounting software" looks different from the roadmap for "financial services", and price expectations exist in each category, which is why the author's CRM for investment banks was priced above a general-purpose CRM (After Positioning, p. 132). The chapter calls a positioning shift a shift in business strategy rather than a marketing change (After Positioning, p. 132). Positioning is then tracked over time, with a check-in recommended every six months or after a major event (After Positioning, p. 133). Four forces are named that can move a market: a credible new competitor, which does not necessarily require a change if target customers do not expect it to be relevant to them, and where the re-examination starts at Step 4 with the alternatives; government regulation, which can make customers care about features that did not matter; economic climate, which shifts buyers between growth and cost-cutting priorities; and new technology, with the 3G-to-4G shift making video a differentiator for products that already supported it (After Positioning, pp. 133–135). The chapter's last story is a data center tour where a bank's CIO walked past millions of dollars of anonymous server hardware to show off a small appliance with purple lights, after which vendors of bundled hardware started packaging in jazzier boxes and talking about design (After Positioning, pp. 135–136).

### Conclusion and back matter (pp. 137–142)

Five takeaways close the book: any product can be positioned in multiple markets; great positioning rarely comes by default and has to be deliberated, tried, failed at, tested, and tried again; understanding what best customers see as true alternatives leads to the differentiators; position in a market that makes the strengths obvious to the intended buyer; and use trends cautiously, since a product is better "successful and boring" than "fashionable and bewildering" (Conclusion, pp. 137–138). The author frames the book as an attempt to codify and teach something she learned to do but found hard to explain (Conclusion, p. 138). The back matter describes the author's consulting practice and four formats — a two-day positioning workshop for startups, a two-hour course for larger teams, a ninety-minute group workshop for accelerators, and customized workshops — and notes that the workshop also breaks the positioning down into a sales story (About the Author, pp. 139–142).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits her own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **The process needs customers first.** It assumes enough happy customers to see a pattern in who loves the product and why, and where that pattern is not visible the author says to hold off on tightening the positioning and to keep it loose while the product is tested on a deliberately wide audience (Step 1, pp. 61–62). This exempts exactly the pre-customer startup the introduction addresses (Introduction, p. 19).
- **Customers, not investors.** The whole process is built for customer-facing positioning, and the author says the investor pitch deck may look dramatically different from the sales deck without either being wrong (Step 1, pp. 63–64).
- **Product or company.** For a single-product company the two can be the same thing; for a multi-product company they are separate but linked, and which is positioned first depends on the revenue mix and the sales process rather than on a rule (Step 1, pp. 64–66).
- **The positioning statement is not entirely useless.** After four pages against it, the author says there are things she does like about it: its blanks name the components of positioning and are where her own framework comes from (Components, p. 44).
- **Negative-looking attributes stay on the list.** An attribute one function reads as a weakness may be another's differentiator, and the onsite-installation example is given precisely because the head of sales and the head of customer service disagreed about it (Step 5, pp. 80–81).
- **Retention attributes are excluded, but retention still matters.** The author says explicitly that she is not saying positioning does not matter for retention — poorly positioned products are bought by people who misunderstand them and leave — only that consideration attributes come first because a prospect who does not buy never meets the retention attributes (Step 5, pp. 82–83).
- **A single value point is allowed.** The clustering step aims for one to four value clusters, and the author notes it is not uncommon for the exercise to produce just one (Step 6, p. 86).
- **Narrow is bounded by the number.** Targeting is as narrow as the near-term sales objective allows; if the best-fit segment cannot hit the target, the definition of best-fit is widened instead (Step 7, p. 90). The subsegment test has the same shape: thirty deals needed against a hundred businesses means the segment is too small (Step 8, p. 106).
- **Positioning is not permanent.** It does not have to fit the company of two, five, or ten years hence, and it is expected to evolve with the best-fit customers (Step 7, p. 91). Narrow now is not narrow forever, and the widening path from credit unions to retail banks to insurance is given as the normal case (Step 8, pp. 106–107).
- **Asking customers what market they are in works only sometimes.** The author answers her own question with "sometimes" and cautions that customers place a product in the market nearest their industry or job, which is often not the one that shows its strengths (Step 8, pp. 95–96).
- **Head to Head has one opening for a newcomer.** The style is "rarely a good choice" for a small business launching a product, and the one case named is a category buyers already understand where no leader has emerged — with the added caveat that taking that lead often needs outside funding, which the author says may not be how a bootstrapper wants to enter (Step 8, pp. 99–101).
- **Create a New Game is a last resort.** It is for use only after every existing category has been evaluated and rejected, or by a company large enough to make the case itself, and for smaller companies it generally requires deep-pocketed, patient investors (Step 8, pp. 112, 115).
- **Trends are optional.** A product well positioned in a market can be very successful with no trend attached, many companies operate in markets with no sizzle, and a trend that does not reinforce the position muddies it (Step 9, pp. 120–121, 124).
- **A new competitor does not automatically change the positioning.** If target customers do not expect the newcomer to be relevant to them or to change the market's price and feature expectations, the author says the position should not need to change (After Positioning, pp. 133–134).

Oracle's own press release returned HTTP 403 and Eloqua's Wikipedia article and Crunchbase page returned 404 and 403, so the basis could not be settled and the $12 million 2006 revenue and the $96 million 2012 revenue are unchecked.
- **Siebel Systems "which had over $2 billion annual revenue" at the time Janna Systems was pitching against it, and Janna's $1.7 billion acquisition by Siebel (Step 8, pp. 110–112).** The revenue figure could not be confirmed for the period. Per [Wikipedia's Siebel Systems article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siebel_Systems), Siebel's key dates record "2000: Revenue surpasses the $1 billion mark" — the year the Janna deal closed — and the source says nothing further about the level it reached that year, so whether it had passed $2 billion by the time Janna was pitching against it is not settled either way by anything reached here. That article lists only the Scopus Technology (1998) and OnLink Technologies (2000) acquisitions and does not mention Janna at all, so the $1.7 billion price and the $2 million-to-$70 million growth are unchecked. Siebel itself is gone: "On September 12, 2005, Oracle Corporation announced it had agreed to buy Siebel Systems for $5.8 billion."
- **Positioning is said to have become "a popular marketing construct" only in 1981, with the publication of Ries and Trout's book (Introduction, p. 17).** The book date is right and the popularity date is a decade late. Per [Wikipedia's positioning article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positioning_(marketing)), "Ries and Trout, both former advertising executives, published articles about positioning in *Industrial Marketing* in 1969 and *Advertising Age* in 1972. By the early 1970s, positioning became a popular word with marketers… In 1981, Ries and Trout published their now-classic book."
- **The consumer smart glasses example, where "Epson or North might come to mind" and no leader exists (Step 8, p. 100).** Unchecked as to North specifically: three candidate Wikipedia titles for the company returned 404 and the web-search budget was spent, so no first-hand source for its status could be reached. The market itself has moved — [Wikipedia's Google Glass article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Glass) records that "Google stopped production and sale of the Google Glass Enterprise Edition on March 15, 2023" and names Ray-Ban Meta among current products — so a 2019 example chosen for having no recognizable leader is no longer an example of that.
- **Sampler, its repositioning to "direct-to-consumer sampling", and the "nearly $10 billion a year" North American sampling spend (Components, pp. 54–55).** Unchecked. The company's own site returned a TLS error and its Crunchbase page returned HTTP 403; the $10 billion figure is quoted in the book from the company's own founder and no independent publication of it was reached. Its named brand customers — L'Oréal, Kimberly-Clark, Unilever — are likewise unchecked.
- **Arm & Hammer, product sales given as "$16 million in 1969" rising to "over $318 million by 1987" after the deodorizer repositioning (Step 3, p. 74).** Unchecked. No source giving either figure could be reached without a search, and the book cites none.
- **"Over 2 million choices" in the App Store and "just under 580 million products" on Amazon (Introduction, p. 17).** Unchecked. Apple's newsroom page for its App Store ecosystem figures returned HTTP 404 and no equivalent first-party page was reached. Both figures are stated in the book without a source and both are the kind of count that moves every year.
- **"Bic for Her", removed from market for not meeting a specific need (Step 8, p. 108).** Unchecked. [Wikipedia's Bic article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bic_(company)) does not mention the product, the Amazon reviews, or any withdrawal, and no first-party Bic page confirming or refuting the withdrawal was reached.
- **Pantone's 2019 color of the year, "living coral" (Components, p. 53).** Unchecked. No Pantone page was fetched, and the claim is by construction a 2019 fact that a 2026 reader would expect to have been superseded seven times over; the point it illustrates, that a trend is a characteristic rather than a category, does not depend on which year's color is named.
- **Crocs selling "over a billion dollars of colorful foam shoes" before moving into other footwear, and Salesforce as a single-product company "for over ten years" (Step 1, p. 64).** Unchecked. No source for either was reached. Both are used only as examples of single-product companies, and the surrounding list — Slack, Basecamp, Twilio, Box, Zippo, WD-40 — is not checked either.
- **The author's own record, "sixteen products into market" over a twenty-five-year career (Introduction, p. 18; About the Author, p. 139).** Unchecked, and uncheckable from an outside source: it is a self-report about work at unnamed companies, and the book names only two of them.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Every case is told by its winner, after the fact, with the repositioning as the cause.** Janna Systems goes from $2 million to over $70 million in eighteen months (Step 8, p. 112), Arm & Hammer from $16 million to over $318 million (Step 3, p. 74), Redgate reports a doubling of inbound leads (Step 9, p. 122), Wattpad Presents raises a television station's viewership 30% (Step 8, p. 97), Eloqua grows from $12 million to $96 million (Step 8, p. 117), Clearpath raises $30 million (Step 3, p. 76), and Sampler's deals close faster (Components, p. 55). None has a control, none separates the repositioning from the other things the company changed at the same time — Clearpath also built new industrial design and a new website (Step 3, p. 76), Wave also changed its business model and its name (Step 1, pp. 66–67) — and no story in the book is of a company that ran the process and got the wrong answer.
- **The author is a participant in several of the cases she cites.** The database-to-data-warehouse story is her own company, told twice (Positioning as Context, pp. 37–39; Step 8, pp. 103–104); Janna Systems is her own company (Step 8, pp. 110–112); the retail-mobility startup (Step 9, pp. 119–120) and the purple-lights data center (After Positioning, pp. 135–136) are her own employers; and the remaining cases are consulting clients or interviewees. The sample is chosen from memory and from a client list, and the clients who did not get a result do not appear.
- **Figures without a source.** "Over 2 million choices" in the App Store and "just under 580 million products" on Amazon (Introduction, p. 17), Redgate's "over 800,000 users" (Step 9, p. 121), Siebel's "over $2 billion annual revenue" (Step 8, p. 110), the Arm & Hammer sales figures (Step 3, p. 74), and the "nearly $10 billion a year" North American sampling spend, which is quoted from the founder of the company selling into that spend (Components, p. 54), all carry no citation. The book has no notes, no bibliography, and no source list.
- **A number that does not match its own source.** The Joshua Bell experiment is the book's most-cited piece of outside evidence, and its figures are not the *Washington Post*'s: the passer-by count is 1,070 against the source's 1,097, and the $32.17 is given as the total where the source records it as the take from 27 passers-by excluding a further $20 from the one person who recognized him (Positioning as Context, p. 26). Section 7 also found the Eloqua price stated as $870 million where the later record carries $810 million, though that is likely one deal on two bases rather than an error, and could not confirm Siebel's "over $2 billion annual revenue" (Step 8, p. 110) for the year in question either way.
- **A dated claim about the field's own history.** Positioning is said to have become "a popular marketing construct" only in 1981, with Ries and Trout's book (Introduction, p. 17), where their own articles ran in 1969 and 1972 and the word was current among marketers by the early 1970s. The book's case for a new positioning book rests partly on how long the concept has been around without a method, so the date is not incidental to the argument.
- **The method's evidence for itself is an assertion.** The process is called "thoroughly field tested" and a "bulletproof way" to connect customer and product (Introduction, p. 19), with no count of engagements, no success rate, no failure, and no comparison against companies that did not run it. The claim that a positioning exercise is valuable on its own (After Positioning, p. 129) is stated rather than shown.
- **A circularity the book names and then does not resolve.** Step 1 opens by naming the interdependency between the components and answers it by starting from the happiest customers (Step 1, pp. 59–60), then concedes that a company without a visible pattern of happy customers should hold off (Step 1, pp. 61–62). That exempts the first reader the introduction names, the startup founder whose prospects cannot figure out what is being sold (Introduction, pp. 19–20), and the fishing-net advice offered instead — position broadly and see what you catch — is the "default" positioning the book spends its first chapter arguing against (Positioning as Context, p. 34).
- **No way to tell a right answer from a wrong one.** The ten steps end at a document and a canvas (Step 10, pp. 125–126), and the only check offered afterward is a review every six months or after a major event, made by judgment (After Positioning, p. 133). The book gives no test a chosen position can fail, no measure to run before and after, and no worked example of the canvas filled in for a real product.
- **The market-category advice is close to circular.** A market must already exist in customers' minds (Step 8, pp. 93–94), the way to find one is abductive reasoning from your own features (Step 8, p. 94), adjacent markets should only be chosen if they make your strengths obvious (Step 8, p. 95), and customers cannot be trusted to name it (Step 8, pp. 95–96). What is left is the team's own judgment about what customers already believe, which is the positioning baggage Step 3 exists to remove (Step 3, pp. 73, 75).
- **Retention is conceded and then excluded.** The author agrees positioning matters for retention and that poorly positioned products are bought by people who misunderstand them (Step 5, pp. 82–83), and the introduction lists high churn shortly after purchase as a sign of weak positioning, singling out subscription businesses (Introduction, p. 21) — but the exercise then deliberately drops retention attributes, so nothing in the ten steps addresses the churn the introduction raised.
- **Segmentation is criticized and then done by hand.** Demographic and firmographic segmentation is dismissed as what marketing courses teach for consumer companies (Step 7, pp. 88–89), and the replacement is a list of identifiable characteristics arrived at by repeating "who cares and why" in a room (Step 7, p. 91). No data source, sample size, or validation is named for that list, so the actionable segmentation rests on the same team judgment the demographic version was faulted for.
- **The pricing claims rest on the author's own two cases.** That clear positioning makes it easier to charge a premium (Introduction, p. 22), that a data warehouse "commanded premium pricing" where a database was a commodity (Step 8, p. 104), and that the CRM for investment banks could be priced above a general-purpose CRM (After Positioning, p. 132) are the same argument told three times from two companies, both the author's own.
- **A quiet assumption about the buyer.** The worked examples are almost entirely business-to-business technology with a sales team, a demo, and a purchase committee — the sales story is introduced for companies with "a complex sales cycle" in which a salesperson speaks to a prospect before a sale can close (After Positioning, p. 129) — while the reader list in the introduction includes someone who sells products online (Introduction, p. 19). The book gives that reader the cake-pop and Bic examples and nothing else.
