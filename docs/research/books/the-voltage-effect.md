## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that whether an idea survives being taken from a small group to a much larger one is predictable rather than lucky, and that the prediction rests on five named checks plus four named amplifiers (Introduction, PDF pp. 16–17, 19–20). The author defines "to scale" broadly, as achieving a desired outcome when an idea moves from a small group of customers, students, or citizens to a much larger one, not merely as growing a company (Introduction, PDF pp. 9–10). A "voltage drop" is his term, borrowed from implementation science and credited to Amy Kilbourne and co-authors, for an enterprise falling apart at scale; he reports, citing Straight Talk on Evidence, that between 50 and 90 percent of programs lose voltage at scale (Introduction, PDF p. 16). The Five Vital Signs are false positives, misjudging how representative the initial population is, unscalable ingredients, spillovers, and unsustainable costs; the four voltage gains are behavioral-economic incentives, marginal thinking, optimal quitting, and a scalable culture (Introduction, PDF pp. 19–20; Conclusion, PDF pp. 216–217). The evidence base is the author's own field experiments across three decades, his time as chief economist at Uber and then at Lyft, the Chicago Heights Early Childhood Center, and his White House work on benefit-cost analysis (Introduction, PDF pp. 7–12, 19).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a business book alone: he says he toggles between business, policy, and everything in between, and writes for a founder, an executive, a civil servant, a researcher, a citizen, or a parent alike (Introduction, PDF pp. 13, 21). It is not a search for a single quality: he calls the idea that scalable ideas share some "silver bullet" feature fundamentally wrong (Introduction, PDF pp. 16–17). It is not a theory of the founder: he argues that in most cases it is not the who that matters but the what, and that if incentives are right character becomes largely irrelevant (Chapter 6, PDF p. 128; Conclusion, PDF p. 217). It is not an instruction to scale: he says there are legitimate reasons for staying small or medium-sized and points at his family's trucking business as one (Chapter 3, PDF p. 74; Conclusion, PDF pp. 216–217). And it is not a license for public shaming, which he explicitly declines to recommend as part of a business incentive structure (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 135–136).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction: Built to Fail or Built to Scale? (PDF pp. 7–21)

The author frames two projects as one problem: the Chicago Heights Early Childhood Center, a preschool he ran from 2010 to 2014 as a live research laboratory on a $10 million gift from the Griffin Foundation, serving nearly 1,500 students a year; and Uber, which he joined as chief economist in 2016 (Introduction, PDF pp. 7–9, 12). Both were about scale, and he defines the term broadly (PDF pp. 9–10). The invented-but-true story of Greta and Mason, a superintendent and a school board member who adopt a peer-reviewed program that then fails a benefit-cost test, is the book's opening case: the problem was not the science, it was that the program did not scale (PDF pp. 15–16). He introduces the "voltage drop", credits the term to Kilbourne and co-authors, and cites Straight Talk on Evidence for 50 to 90 percent of programs losing voltage (PDF p. 16). The book is split in two: five causes of voltage drops, then four sources of voltage gains (PDF pp. 19–20). He calls on policymakers to move from evidence-based policy to "policy-based evidence" (PDF p. 19).

### Chapter 1: Dupers and False Positives (PDF pp. 24–45)

D.A.R.E. is the case. Launched in Los Angeles in 1983, encouraged by early research, and graduating 43 million children from more than forty countries over twenty-four years, it did not reduce drug use, and one study found it raised curiosity about drugs (PDF pp. 24–25). The 1985 National Institute of Justice assessment of 1,777 children in Honolulu calculated a 2 percent chance of a false positive; the researchers either underestimated that probability or fell inside it (PDF p. 26). A 2005 study is cited for burglar alarms: 94 to 99 percent of calls are false alarms, and false alarms are 10 to 20 percent of all calls to police (PDF p. 26).

The business case is Chrysler in 2006. A seven-month wellness pilot at one of thirty-one plants, run with Staywell, produced healthy behaviors, lower medical spending, and less absence; the author's team insisted on one more pilot with a new sample from the same plant before the rollout, and the effect vanished, then vanished twice more at two further plants (PDF pp. 27–29). Absenteeism at roughly 10 percent cost millions a year in bullpen wages, and a 5 percent reduction at the Sterling Heights plant had cut defects by about five hundred a month (PDF pp. 27–28).

Three biases follow. Confirmation bias, from Kahneman and Tversky's 1974 paper, illustrated with Peter Wason's rule-discovery test, in which almost all subjects tested only sequences that confirmed their hypothesis (PDF pp. 30–32). Bandwagon bias, from Asch's 1951 line experiment, where more than a third of participants on average followed the wrong answer and 75 percent did so at least once over twelve trials (PDF p. 32); the author's own version is that the leader talks first and loudest, and he distinguishes implicitly forcing a consensus from building one (PDF pp. 33–34). And the winner's curse, demonstrated by a classroom auction for a jar of coins that a student wins at $25 against a $10 jar, which the author says has happened every time in over a decade (PDF p. 35); the escape is a "comparative advantage" — a patent, a key resource, or personal expertise (PDF pp. 35–36).

The remedy is replication. Ronald Fisher's lady-tasting-tea experiment at Rothamsted, eight cups rather than two, produced the design principle in *Statistical Methods for Research Workers* (1925) and *The Design of Experiments* (PDF pp. 36–37). Replication alone is not enough: the author calls for an *independent* replication by a party with no stake, and says the same study should be run three or four times with the same type of population (PDF pp. 37–38). He cites Johns Hopkins researchers in 2016 for more than 250,000 American deaths a year from medical error, the third-leading cause (PDF pp. 38–39), and Collaborative Strategic Reading, a Florida reading pilot that scaled to five districts in Oklahoma and Texas with no discernible effect (PDF p. 39). On the replication crisis he reports that of a hundred high-profile experiments only thirty-nine produced the published results, and describes his own work on the file-drawer problem (PDF pp. 39–40).

The dark half is duping. Brian Wansink of Cornell's Food and Brand Lab falsified results for years; nineteen studies had been retracted as of writing, six by JAMA in one day in 2018, and Cornell's press release named "data falsification" among the misconduct (PDF pp. 40–41). The author's own survey of a thousand academic economists found nearly 5 percent admitting to having falsified research data (PDF p. 41). "Duper" is Antonio Gracias's term for someone who deliberately misrepresents to produce a false positive (PDF p. 41). Theranos raised over $700 million at a $9 billion valuation on technology that did not exist, and Holmes's options were once valued above $4 billion (PDF pp. 42–43). At Uber the manager with an idea was the person who tested it, and shipping ideas was how people were promoted (PDF p. 44). The remedies offered are independent replication, profit-sharing that links pay to later performance, and a standing devil's advocate function (PDF pp. 44–45).

### Chapter 2: Know Your Audience (PDF pp. 46–67)

Lyft Pink is the case. Logan Green wanted a paid membership modeled on Costco, whose 2018 net earnings topped $3 billion and whose members number 100 million (PDF pp. 47–48). The author argued against it in four town-hall-style debates: a subscription scales only where it gives access to goods not readily available without it, and riders dual-app, so switching is nearly effortless (PDF pp. 48–49). He sets this against the literature on two-part tariffs, from W. Arthur Lewis's 1941 paper and Walter Oi's 1971 "Disneyland Dilemma", and notes Disney chose a single tariff (PDF pp. 49–50). The Rose Bowl Flea Market's tiered admission — $25, $18, $14, then $9 general at 9:00 a.m. — is the worked example (PDF pp. 49–50).

Two customer types decide it. "JoGoods" buy more because of the discount; "NoGoods" buy the same amount at a lower price, and the membership fee does not cover what they cost (PDF pp. 50–51). In early March 2019 Lyft offered roughly 1.2 million riders one of six randomly assigned deals, from $5 up front for 5 percent off to $25 for 15 percent off (PDF p. 52). NoGoods outnumbered JoGoods nearly three to one, and none of the six would scale (PDF pp. 52–53). The analysis found the optimal structure was 7.5 percent off for $19.99, which none of the six had been; Lyft Pink launched at the end of 2019 at $19.99 a month with 15 percent off and a suite of perks, recommended for riders taking two to three rides a week (PDF pp. 53–54).

Representativeness is the general lesson. Pinterest is used primarily by women, which caps its user base; dating apps never scale to people in committed monogamous relationships; Ashley Madison claims a 70-million-strong market (PDF p. 55). Kmart's Blue Light Special scaled because individual store managers chose the discounted goods and knew their customers, and broke when the corporate office in Hoffman Estates began dictating the same goods months in advance for every store (PDF pp. 56–57). Selection bias is defined as people opting in non-randomly, illustrated by insomniacs in a sleeping-pill trial and by McDonald's Arch Deluxe, which focus groups liked and customers did not (PDF pp. 57–58). Deliberate selection can do the same: an iron-fortified-salt program tested only in adolescent women did not reduce anemia in the general population (PDF pp. 58–59). The Nurse-Family Partnership is the counter-case, piloted by David Olds in Memphis, Denver, and Elmira for demographic variation, with results that replicated (PDF pp. 59–60). Opower's energy-comparison letters ran in 111 randomized controlled trials across 8.6 million households and still failed to generalize, because utilities in environmentalist areas were likelier to take part — "site selection bias" (PDF pp. 60–61). In Chicago Heights, Parent Academy's average gain turned out to hold only for Hispanic families, whose multigenerational households could absorb a missed meeting (PDF p. 61). Joseph Henrich's fairness experiment in Peru and the "WEIRD" critique close the argument, with the author's own finding that women in a matrilineal Indian society are as competitive as men (PDF pp. 62–63).

Broadening the audience is the remedy. Gopuff, founded by two Drexel sophomores in 2013, reached some 7,000 employees and more than 650 cities, over $250 million of 2019 revenue, and a reported $750 million SoftBank investment; growth past the college market required an instant-delivery pharmacy and diapers, not more of the same (PDF pp. 64–65). Taco Bell's Nacho Fries, introduced in 2018, is the fast-food version (PDF p. 66). Where product tweaks are too expensive, the author names cheaper production, a better market, or quitting (PDF p. 66). His Sierra Club fundraising experiment found matching gifts moved men and not women, and the Chicago Heights answer to the Hispanic-only result was an all-day preschool that did work for Black and white families (PDF p. 66).

### Chapter 3: Is It the Chef or the Ingredients? (PDF pp. 68–86)

Jamie Oliver's chain is the case. Jamie's Italian opened in Oxford in 2008, drew a favorable Guardian review, and reached seventy locations in twenty-seven markets (PDF pp. 68–69). A chef's talent does not scale because the person cannot be cloned, which the author illustrates with Ferran Adrià's El Bulli, closed by choice in 2010, where a reported one million people a year sought 8,000 seats (PDF pp. 69–70). Oliver escaped that trap because his secret ingredient was his ingredients rather than himself, and his fame got people through the door (PDF pp. 70–71). It fell apart when the less visible ingredients changed: managing director Simon Blagden and two other executives left in 2017 and were replaced by Oliver's brother-in-law Paul Hunt, a former stockbroker fined for insider trading in 1999, who could not pick sites or retain staff; a future CEO's postmortem said the company was opening too many restaurants too quickly in the wrong places (PDF pp. 71–72). By the start of 2019 the chain was $100 million in the red, twenty-five UK restaurants closed and a thousand employees were laid off (PDF p. 72).

The framework is negotiables and non-negotiables, which the author ties to hedonics, the economics of valuing component parts (PDF pp. 73–75). Non-negotiables have infinite value because the enterprise will not function at scale without them; negotiables have finite value (PDF p. 75). His own family's trucking business is the "chef" case that is profitable and deliberately unscalable (PDF p. 74). Publishing is his worked example of mixed scalability: the distribution network scales, great content does not (PDF p. 74). Airbnb's platform and host network, and Netflix's shift from shipping infrastructure to streaming library, are the fidelity cases (PDF p. 75).

Compliance is named as a non-negotiable for nearly every enterprise, and defined simply as use (PDF pp. 75–77). Medication adherence is the long-running example; a park nobody uses and a training program nobody enrolls in are the policy versions (PDF p. 76). Present bias explains why an immediate cost and a delayed benefit is the hard case (PDF p. 77). The Chicago Heights curriculum had two non-negotiable pillars, explicit instruction in cognitive and noncognitive skills and guided parent involvement, the second bought with cash incentives; a London school district that copied the curriculum was barred by policy from paying parents, sign-ups collapsed, and attendance was worse than that of the most truant students in the lowest-performing US districts (PDF pp. 77–79).

"Program drift" is the subtler failure, and it comes from the implementors rather than the users (PDF p. 79). Early Head Start, launched in 1995 with sixty-eight programs and grown past 1,200 and three million families by 2019, lost voltage on its twice-monthly home visits: at-risk parents had less time, and the visits drifted from the method (PDF pp. 79–80). Multiple funders with different priorities are named as a common cause, and public evaluation is slow enough to let failure run for years (PDF pp. 80–81). Paul Midler's "quality fade" is the business term, and AARP's billion-dollar insurance arm is the two-masters case (PDF p. 81).

Technology looks scalable and is not automatically so. Opower's Honeywell smart thermostat passed every engineering test and produced no savings across nearly two hundred thousand households, because customers gradually undid the default settings; the engineers had modeled a perfect customer (PDF pp. 82–83). Apple's public beta program is the counter-practice (PDF pp. 83–84). Instagram and the Grassroots Law Project's automated call drive after George Floyd's murder are the easy-to-use cases (PDF p. 84). The chapter closes on backward induction: imagine success at scale first, and if fifty thousand teachers are needed, do not pilot with the ten best (PDF p. 85).

### Chapter 4: Spillovers (PDF pp. 87–103)

Ralph Nader's *Unsafe at Any Speed* (1965) produced the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration and the 1968 seatbelt law; Sam Peltzman's 1975 paper concluded that auto safety regulation had not affected the highway death rate, because drivers who felt safer took more risk (PDF pp. 87–88). Later work is offered in support: bicycle helmets and closer overtaking, and a 2009 NASCAR study finding fewer serious injuries and more accidents after a new head-and-neck restraint (PDF p. 88). Scott Sagan's argument that more guards can make nuclear facilities less secure, and moral hazard in insurance, extend it (PDF p. 88). The author defines a spillover for this book as the unintended impact of one group's actions on another, and offers a Murphy's law of scaling (PDF p. 89).

General equilibrium effects are the first category. Fifty percent of sophomores switching to economics would depress economists' wages where a hundred-student experiment would not (PDF pp. 89–90); large job-training programs face the same problem (PDF p. 90). At Uber, Hall, Horton, and Knoepfle examined base-fare increases across thirty-six cities over 105 weeks and found driver earnings above the old level for several weeks, close to it by week six, and the gain gone entirely by week fifteen, because higher fares drew more drivers (PDF pp. 90–91). A $5-off coupon in Seattle raised rides in the small test and, scaled citywide, spiked demand, thinned driver supply, raised fares and wait times, and cut trips (PDF pp. 91–92). Positive spillovers exist too: the 2014 Kenya cash-transfer experiment by Egger, Haushofer, Miguel, Niehaus, and Walker gave $1,000 each to some ten thousand households across 653 villages, $10 million in total, amounting to over 15 percent of local GDP in the peak twelve months, with no inflation spike and higher consumption among non-recipients (PDF pp. 92–93).

Social spillovers are the second category. The author hired more than two hundred canvassers for the Flossmoor Firebirds in 2010 at $15 and $10 an hour, mixed in the same vans, and found the higher-paid worked harder; a later all-$10 van worked just as hard as the $15 group; a three-arm design showed that mixed vans produced the gap and segregated vans did not (PDF pp. 94–96). The mechanism is "resentful demoralization", the flip side of the John Henry effect, and the underpaid canvassers also pocketed donations at a much higher rate (PDF p. 96). Cullen and Perez-Truglia's 2017 experiment with 2,060 employees of an Asian commercial bank found that employees told their manager earned 10 percent more than they thought worked 1.5 percent more hours over ninety days, while employees who learned peers earned 10 percent more worked 9.4 percent less and were likelier to leave (PDF p. 97). Because the manager-to-employee ratio shrinks as a company grows, the author predicts a voltage gain from publishing managers' salaries and a drop from publishing lower-level ones (PDF pp. 97–98).

Network effects are the third. Peer effects in education, citing Bruce Sacerdote, set up the Chicago Heights finding: treatment children passed cognitive and noncognitive skills to control children by proximity, more strongly the closer they lived, and treatment children accelerated each other; parents in the control group, hearing about Parent Academy, raised their own involvement, and counting spillovers expanded the program's measured impact at least tenfold (PDF pp. 99–101). One negative spillover is named: the extra parental time came out of time with siblings (PDF p. 101). Facebook, LinkedIn, lock-in, Lyft's rider-driver loop, and herd immunity close the category (PDF pp. 101–102). The recap names all three categories and tells the reader to address negative spillovers and exploit positive ones (PDF p. 103).

### Chapter 5: The Cost Trap (PDF pp. 104–123)

Arivale is the case. Founded in 2014 by Leroy Hood and Clayton Lewis, it sold genetic workups, blood tests, microbiome analysis, and coaching, raised $50 million, and was GeekWire's 2016 Start-up of the Year (PDF pp. 104–105). The author says it cleared the first four Vital Signs: a 2019 peer-reviewed observational study of over 2,500 clients, a broad potential audience, known non-negotiables, and no spillovers (PDF p. 105). It charged about $3,500 a year, could not lower the price without losing money, and had no economies of scale to grow into, because coaches cannot be mass-produced; Lewis called the costs "wickedly expensive" (PDF pp. 108–109). A 2018 cut to $1,200 a year, or $99 a month, still left only 2,500 enrollees the following year, and the company shut in April 2019, releasing a public statement that the cost of providing the program exceeded what customers could pay, and letting go 120 employees (PDF pp. 110–111). Lewis's postmortem regret was staying focused on the flagship instead of launching lower-cost, simpler programs (PDF p. 111).

The economics come from Adam Smith. Economies of scale spread a fixed cost across units; diseconomies arise where a key input gets scarcer, as with the last barrels from a well or the last credentialed teachers in a district (PDF pp. 106–107). Wise Acre Frozen Treats is the second case: Jim Picariello's organic ice pops reached a dozen employees and East Coast supermarket contracts, ran about $30,000 a month in costs, and went bankrupt in 2008 (PDF pp. 112). Space tourism is the third: Virgin Galactic planning $250,000 a seat, parallel cargo revenue, NASA subsidies, and SpaceX's reusable components cutting cost-to-orbit by a factor of eighteen and building more than one rocket a week (PDF pp. 112–114). Tesla's batteries and cells, and the automated plant Musk calls his Alien Dreadnought, are the author's example of an obsession with scale economies (PDF p. 113).

The practical advice is a list: take stock of fixed costs at the outset; reduce them where possible, including by trading salary for equity; look for reusable materials or a saleable byproduct, as natural gas is to crude oil; build in the potential for scale economies, accepting a higher fixed cost for a lower marginal one; assume operating costs will run at least 10 percent above expectation; and where scale economies exist, price low to get customers in the door, which he says is why many software products run a freemium strategy, and do due diligence on the "choke price" at which demand drops to zero (PDF pp. 115–116).

Cost is just as binding outside business. The polio vaccine scaled because it was cheap to produce and easy to deliver, and the United States eliminated polio by 1979 (PDF p. 117). A public-policy urban legend about hovercrafts bought to deliver the vaccine across fifty thousand people in northern Zambia is the counter-case, and the author flags it as a story (PDF pp. 117–118). A drug rehabilitation program with a 100 percent success rate at $50,000 per person will not scale (PDF p. 118).

Human capital is the last cost trap. The best teachers are expensive to retain and get more expensive as you hire more, the opposite of Costco's lettuce (PDF p. 119). California's 1990s statewide class-size reduction and a later Tennessee initiative both failed at scale because the teachers were not there to hire (PDF pp. 119–120). The answer is backward induction, built on Ernst Zermelo's 1913 theorem and the 1950s work that joined it to reasoning backward from the desired outcome (PDF p. 120). CHECC hired its thirty teachers and administrators from the same candidate pool and under the same salary caps as the Chicago Heights public schools, accepting a less dazzling pilot in exchange for a program that could be staffed at thirty thousand (PDF p. 121). The author rephrases Voltaire so that perfection is "the enemy of scale" (PDF p. 121).

### Chapter 6: Incentives That Scale (PDF pp. 126–150)

A dropped-wallet field experiment scored the character of small-business loan applicants, and the author's answer to whether character predicts scaling is that it largely does not: correspondence bias makes people overweight the individual, and if incentives are right character becomes largely irrelevant (PDF pp. 126–128). Incentives, unlike people, are almost infinitely scalable (PDF p. 128).

Uber's tipping story is the first case. Drivers put out tip cups; Lyft had tipping from the outset; the #DeleteUber campaign of January 2017 made driver trust an existential issue, and Travis Kalanick was persuaded in the summer of 2017 (PDF pp. 129–130). Drivers' per-ride take rose and their overall wages did not, because tipping drew so many new drivers that each got fewer rides — the spillover of Chapter 4 (PDF p. 130). Customer ratings showed no better service (PDF p. 131). The headline finding: 1 percent of passengers tipped on every trip, 60 percent never tipped, and 39 percent sometimes did, because nobody was watching (PDF p. 131).

Loss aversion explains it. Kahneman and Tversky's central finding, the disposition effect in stocks, and sellers overpricing after a housing boom are the illustrations (PDF p. 132). Self-monitoring extends loss aversion to social standing (PDF pp. 132–133). Uber's method removed the immediate reputational pressure, and the incentive went with it (PDF pp. 133–134).

The Dominican Republic is the second case. In 2017 nearly 62 percent of companies and roughly 57 percent of people failed to pay income tax; in 2018 the author's team sent one of two messages to 28,000 self-employed people and over 56,000 companies, one about jail time and one about a new law putting punishments on the public record (PDF pp. 134–135). Revenue rose by more than $100 million, more than 0.12 percent of that year's GDP; the jail message worked better, but incarcerating a large share of the population is not scalable, and the public-disclosure message also worked (PDF p. 135).

Virgin Atlantic is the third. From February to September 2014, three treatment groups of captains received monthly fuel-efficiency reports, reports plus personal targets, or reports plus targets plus a charity donation in their name, against a control group told only that fuel use would be measured (PDF pp. 136–137). All three treatment groups improved, and so did the control group, which the author attributes to the Hawthorne effect (PDF p. 138). Targets plus encouragement produced the biggest gain, up to 28 percent above the report-only group, and the donation added nothing (PDF pp. 138–139). The stated result is 7,700 tons of fuel saved, $5.37 million in fuel costs, and more than 21,500 metric tons of carbon dioxide avoided across 335 pilots, some 40,000 flights, and over 100,000 pilot decisions; 79 percent of captains wanted more such initiatives and 6 percent did not (PDF pp. 138–139). Opower's Home Energy Report is the scaled version: across thirty-eight field experiments sent to some 250,000 homes, consumers cut electricity use by 2.4 percent on average, and 35 to 55 percent of the reduction persisted after the reports stopped (PDF pp. 139–140). One-time incentives are named as easier to scale than continuous messaging, which goes stale (PDF p. 140). The Toxics Release Inventory, published workplace-diversity data by division, and voting studies extend the pattern (PDF p. 141). Social incentives are cheaper at scale than pay or perks, and human psychology varies less across groups than the pay level needed to move one person (PDF pp. 141–142).

The clawback is the money version. The endowment effect, from Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler's mug experiment, is that owners valued the mug at double what they would have paid; Ariely and Carmon found the same for Final Four tickets (PDF p. 143). At the Wanlida Group's Nanjing factory in 2008, the author and Tanjim Hossain provisionally allocated a bonus to one group, to be lost if the weekly production goal was missed, and framed the same money conventionally for another; team productivity in the loss group rose by over 1 percent, undiminished across six months (PDF pp. 143–144). In Kampala, 1,200 bean sorters produced a 20 percent increase (PDF p. 145). In Chicago Heights, 150 teachers — nearly 95 percent — opted in; the reward group could earn $80 per percentile to a maximum of $8,000, while the loss group received $4,000 up front and signed a contract to return the difference if performance was below average, with the same maximum (PDF pp. 146–147). The loss group's students gained, and the teachers' improvement persisted for five years after the incentive ended (PDF p. 148). A further experiment with over six thousand students found the clawback beat the traditional reward, that a trophy worked as well as cash, and that a reward delayed by one month had no effect at all (PDF p. 148).

### Chapter 7: Revolution on the Margins (PDF pp. 151–171)

The author joined the Council of Economic Advisers in 2002 to do benefit-cost analysis; more than a hundred federal agencies issue about 4,500 rulemaking notices a year, of which fifty to a hundred are "economically significant" at more than $100 million a year (PDF pp. 151–152). Stephen Breyer's *Breaking the Vicious Circle* (1993) and Reagan's Executive Order 12291 are the background, and the author's colleague John D. Graham pushed benefit-cost measured in years of life saved rather than lives (PDF pp. 153–155). His insight was that the data were averages: inside the EPA, removing tailings dams cost tens of millions per life saved while compliance standards with the same effect cost tens of thousands (PDF p. 155). The rule he draws is to look at the positive impact of the last dollar spent, not the average (PDF pp. 155–156).

The Marginal Revolution — Jevons, Menger, and Walras, building on Bentham — introduced utility and marginal utility, and the law of diminishing marginal utility, which the author illustrates with a third donut (PDF pp. 156–157). The War on Drugs is his policy case: more money went to enforcement while the marginal benefit was greater on the last dollar spent on prevention and recovery (PDF p. 158). Tiered electricity prices are the consumer case: people respond to average prices, which Zeckhauser and Liebman call "schmeduling" (PDF p. 159). His marginal analysis of federal programs went nowhere, because reallocating money needs political consensus and agency funding depends on prior-year spending, which disincentivizes saving (PDF p. 160).

Lyft is where it worked. Looking at a spreadsheet of advertising returns, he found the last bucket of dollars on Facebook ads yielded a fiftieth of the business of the last bucket on Google ads, and the fix was to move spending (PDF p. 161). The same pattern ran through driver recruiting and refer-a-friend offers; the company had been reading average returns (PDF p. 162). The memo, "Adam Smith Visits Lyft", became one of the most-read in company history and was renamed after the Mandalorians during the 2020 cost-cutting; it states that a firm is efficient only when the marginal benefit of the last dollar on every input is equalized (PDF pp. 162–163). Logan Green asked every group to look at the last dollar, and nobody was punished for earlier allocations (PDF p. 163).

Experimenting on the margins is the practice. The Wisconsin Cheeseman, where the author drove a forklift in the mid-1980s, doubled its assembly lines and never needed twice the cheese, because the plant manager budgeted on averages and the line moved at the speed of its weakest link; the plant closed in 2011 (PDF pp. 164–165). The advice is to look where there are many levers, collect data granularly over time and across strategies, run multisite trials, and look per region rather than at a national daily average (PDF pp. 166–167). Some marginal benefits resist measurement: the Make-A-Wish Foundation asked him to value its gifts to terminally ill children, and his answer was that some things transcend monetization (PDF p. 167).

Sunk costs are the obstacle. The University of Chicago's fundraising department had mothballed a call center after computing an average cost per call that included the one-time phone-banking system; recalculated on the margin, calls beat mailers on cost and on donations, and the call center was rebooted (PDF pp. 168–169). The bygones principle is that money spent in the past should have no influence on present decisions (PDF p. 168). The outdoor-concert thought experiment — would we buy the tickets now? — is the offered test (PDF pp. 169–170). Marginal analysis surfaces past mistakes, which is where the temptation to double down arrives (PDF p. 170). Culture decides how well an organization handles that, and some money-management firms rotate portfolios every six months so a fresh reader can sell (PDF pp. 170–171).

### Chapter 8: Quitting Is for Winners (PDF pp. 172–188)

The author's own case opens it: a college golfer who compared his scores against Steve Stricker's and Jerry Kelly's over two days in a library and concluded his PGA ambition was a false positive, against a childhood culture of Vince Lombardi (PDF pp. 172–174). He quit golf, took an economics degree in 1992 and a doctorate in 1996, was turned down by 149 of 150 tenure-track jobs, and persisted there instead (PDF p. 175). His claim is that people and organizations do not quit enough and do not quit soon enough (PDF pp. 175–176).

Opportunity cost is the mechanism. His son Mason's choice between a $200 bat plus a glove and a $325 bat is the illustration, as is his own sports-card practice of reframing two cards as one card plus five packs (PDF pp. 176–177). Opportunity cost neglect is the named bias, supported by affective-forecasting research and by a study of policymakers (PDF pp. 177–178). Time is the scarce resource, and Gary Becker's work on valuing time is cited (PDF p. 178). "Optimal quitting" is quitting at the point where the opportunity cost is still low (PDF p. 179). A 1990s experiment in which participants asked questions almost exclusively about the focal event rather than the alternatives is the evidence for tunnel vision, and the prescription is to actively generate the alternatives (PDF pp. 179–180). Google's X Development is the model: teleportation and space elevators were abandoned, and Astro Teller's TED talk describes balancing unchecked optimism with enthusiastic skepticism (PDF p. 180). Netflix killing Qwikster in 2011 is bold quitting, but the author says it is debatable whether it was optimal, because the company had already driven to the edge of the cliff (PDF pp. 180–181). Angela Duckworth's grit is reframed as the resilience to quit early and start again (PDF p. 181).

Comparative advantage is the second half. David Ricardo's 1817 wine and cloth is the source; the modern versions are Japanese cars, Saudi oil, and American computers (PDF pp. 181–182). Twitter, spun out of the podcasting platform Odeo, is a pivot into a new sport; Confinity's PalmPilot payments became PayPal on eBay because the technology had an edge in a market that existed (PDF pp. 183–184). The caveat the author adds is that being best at something is not enough, and that it has to be something many people need and want (PDF p. 184). Knowing when to quit is then two questions — is there a comparative advantage, and is there a substantial market for it (PDF p. 184).

Why people still do not quit: sunk costs magnify the pain of failure (PDF p. 185); ambiguity aversion favors the known (PDF p. 186); and the counterfactual is never observed, so the cost of not quitting is never learned (PDF pp. 186–187). The 2013 Freakonomics coin-flip experiment, in which participants flipped more than twenty thousand virtual coins, found that those who made a major change reported being happier at two months and happier still at six (PDF p. 186). The offered exercises are to watch the competitor who took the opportunity you declined, and to list what else you could have done with the past six months (PDF p. 187). Reid Hoffman's line about killing a product that lacks the potential to scale closes the chapter (PDF p. 187).

### Chapter 9: Scaling Culture (PDF pp. 189–213)

Two Bahian fishing villages open it. Cabuçu fishes the sea in teams of three to eight; Santo Estêvão fishes a lake alone (PDF pp. 189–190). In field experiments run by Andreas Leibbrandt with the author and Uri Gneezy — a trust game, donations, lotteries, ultimatum, coordination, competition, and public goods — the Cabuçu fishermen trusted more, were more trustworthy, made more equal offers, contributed more to public goods, and donated more to an outside charity (PDF pp. 190–191). The author's reading is that daily teamwork instilled the prosocial behavior, not that they were better people (PDF pp. 191–192).

Uber is the counter-case. The author's first day included an openly distraught employee nobody acknowledged (PDF p. 192). Early 2017 brought Susan Fowler's blog post, the Waymo suit, the leaked dash-cam video, and the New York Times Greyball story (PDF p. 193). Meetings were jousting matches in which the loudest or fastest speaker's idea usually won; Travis Kalanick believed in meritocracy in its purest form, and that culture had worked while Uber was a scrappy disruptor (PDF pp. 194–195). The author, who found the University of Chicago cutthroat enough to be used to it — a first visit interrupted throughout by Gary Becker in a hospital gown — still found Uber different (PDF p. 195). The failure he names is that a stated meritocracy drifted from an actual one, because who talks loudest and who plays politics distorted it; Kalanick's own later self-critique is quoted on toe-stepping being weaponized and on favoring logic over empathy (PDF pp. 196–197). Deep trust is named as a factor in scaling, and Uber's disrespect extended to riders through the "God view" tool, the handling of a rape case in India, and surge pricing during a New York taxi strike (PDF p. 197). Kalanick resigned in June 2017 and kept a board seat until December 2019; an unsent letter he wrote admits the company forgot to put people first (PDF pp. 198–199).

The alternative is trust and teamwork. The author argues that the opportunity cost of not collaborating rises with size, because a five-thousand-person firm has good partners around every corner where a five-person firm may not (PDF pp. 200–201). He does not argue for cooperation alone: research on "coopetition" supports rewarding individual, team, and organization-wide performance together, and Netflix is his example — untracked vacation and expenses, seven-figure acquisitions without approval, equity in place of individual bonuses, and Reed Hastings's refusal to tolerate "brilliant jerks" (PDF pp. 201–202). One structural suggestion: put every employee on at least two teams, preferably in different departments (PDF p. 202).

Recruiting is where his own research lands. Through the Science of Diversity Initiative he argues diversity in every sense produces cognitive diversity, better decisions, and higher profits (PDF p. 202). With Andreas Leibbrandt he posted an administrative-assistant opening in ten US cities, with and without an equal employment opportunity statement; nearly 2,500 people applied, and the statement discouraged racial minorities from applying by as much as 30 percent, most strongly in less diverse cities and among more educated applicants, because they feared tokenism, and prior research found employers using such statements no less likely to discriminate (PDF pp. 203–204). The remedy offered is showing rather than telling, plus a "flywheel effect" once published workforce data tell the story (PDF p. 204). With Daniel Hedblom and Brent Hickman he created a real data-entry firm, HHL LLC, advertised on Craigslist in twelve cities, and found a corporate-social-responsibility paragraph raised applications by 25 percent, produced a more diverse pool, and produced more productive employees (PDF pp. 205–206). Directed at existing employees, the same messaging produced moral licensing: among three thousand transcription workers, 20 percent more shirked and cheating rose 11 percent (PDF p. 207). A third experiment with about 2,500 job-seekers found that stating salary is negotiable draws more women, who then negotiate as hard as men or harder, while men are drawn to ambiguous compensation rules, and that low-performing men bargain most when salary is vague while the strongest women hold back (PDF p. 208). A 2021 study on informal short lists favoring men supplies the last fix: a longer short list, three to five (PDF p. 209).

Apologies close the chapter. After a driver drove the author in a circle back to his own house during #DeleteUber, he and Kalanick agreed on a field experiment (PDF pp. 210–211). Statistical twins across nearly 15 million rides a day showed a rider who had a bad trip spent 5 to 10 percent less over the next ninety days (PDF p. 211). Different apologies went to the 1.5 million riders identified as having had a bad trip, some with a $5 coupon and some with nothing (PDF pp. 211–212). Three findings: contrite apologies beat basic ones; any apology with a coupon did best, because a gesture with a material cost showed the customer was worth fighting for; and apologizing for three or more transgressions in a short period is worse than not apologizing at all, because an apology is read as a promise (PDF p. 212).

### Conclusion: To Scale or Not to Scale? (PDF pp. 214–218)

The Covid-19 response is the closing case, split into what scaled — testing infrastructure, hospital capacity, masking messaging, stimulus, vaccines inside a year — and what did not: US contact tracing, unreliable tests, $1.4 billion of stimulus sent to dead people, and a slow vaccine rollout whose small-scale efficiency was not representative (PDF pp. 214–215). Two restated lessons: weaknesses reveal themselves at scale, and scalable ideas remain the most valuable resource for urgent problems (PDF p. 216). Jared Diamond's "Anna Karenina principle" is applied: scaling is a weakest-link problem, and successfully scaled ideas are alike while every failure fails in its own way (PDF p. 216). The five checks and the four gains are restated (PDF pp. 216–217). The author repeats that it is not the who but the what, that the reader need not be a founder, and that staying small is legitimate (PDF pp. 216–217). The last call is to policymakers, for policy-based evidence and for abandoning implemented programs the data condemn (PDF pp. 217–218).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scale is not obligatory.** A "chef" business has a limit on how big it can get and can still be profitable, which the author illustrates with his family's trucking firm, and he says that accepting a small firm for life can be a happy and successful outcome (Chapter 3, PDF p. 74). He repeats it at the end: there are legitimate reasons to stay small or medium-sized even with the potential to go bigger, and he says national or international success sounds exhausting to him too (Conclusion, PDF pp. 216–217).
- **A ceiling is not a failure.** Many businesses have built-in limitations, and an idea can still serve a purpose and make money with a ceiling; Pinterest's female-majority user base and dating apps' irrelevance to people in committed monogamous relationships are his examples (Chapter 2, PDF p. 55).
- **The winner's curse has an escape.** Being the highest bidder is acceptable where the bidder holds a comparative advantage — a patent, a key resource, or expertise that lets them scale faster (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 35–36).
- **Three or four replications may not be feasible.** Where they are not, the author's stated fallback is caution rather than speed (Chapter 1, PDF p. 38). He also notes his own Chrysler team caught its false positive without an independent replication, because their reputations gave them a motive to doubt themselves (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 37–38).
- **Character is not nothing.** He says the character of leaders matters, and the culture they foster with it, immediately before arguing that just as much does not (Chapter 6, PDF p. 127).
- **Medication adherence has named exclusions.** The compliance argument excludes experimental medicines without established efficacy and drugs with a high risk of interactions or intolerable side effects (Chapter 3, PDF p. 76).
- **Do not hire mediocre engineers.** The advice to design for average staff is bounded: where hardware or a digital interface must be high quality, that is a non-negotiable, and only the forty thousand technicians maintaining it at scale are the negotiable part (Chapter 5, PDF p. 122).
- **The hovercraft story is a story.** He introduces the Zambian polio-delivery case as a public policy urban legend of sorts and marks the purchase as supposed (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 117–118).
- **Public shaming is declined.** Having reported that the public-disclosure tax message worked, he says he is not suggesting public shaming and threats to reputation belong in a business incentive structure, calls it a recipe for a toxic workplace culture, and says it would likely disincentivize (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 135–136).
- **Loss aversion has a boundary condition.** People with vast experience in trading assets show very little sign of it, and repeated exposure to loss desensitizes; the author says this bounds how far the incentive can be pushed, while adding that he has not yet reached the boundary inside an organization (Chapter 6, PDF p. 145).
- **Clawbacks carry an obligation.** He says a company scaling them must not set impossible or unrealistic benchmarks that produce toxic stress, must pay out however many employees earn it, and that upholding organizational ethics matters more than any voltage gain (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 145–146). He also says the goal is not to make people constantly feel what they are at risk of losing (Chapter 6, PDF p. 150).
- **The critique of educational incentives is granted.** He calls the extrinsic-versus-intrinsic worry valid, and limits his own finding to situations where intrinsic motivation is already very low (Chapter 6, PDF p. 149).
- **Tipping is not the lesson.** He states that the significance of the Uber story is not that the company should have made tipping public, and adds the conditional that a business whose model makes tipping a non-negotiable may weigh public against private differently (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 133–134).
- **Marginal thinking failed where he first tried it.** In the federal government the reallocation never happened, because it required political consensus and because agency funding depends on prior-year spending, which disincentivizes saving (Chapter 7, PDF p. 160).
- **Some marginal benefits resist measurement.** Asked by the Make-A-Wish Foundation to value gifts to terminally ill children, his answer was that some things worth doing and scaling transcend monetization and conventional measurement (Chapter 7, PDF p. 167).
- **Quitting late still beats not quitting.** Odeo and Confinity could in theory have quit earlier, and he says arriving late is preferable to never (Chapter 8, PDF p. 185). Netflix's Qwikster reversal is called bold and wise quitting and, in the same passage, debatable as optimal quitting (Chapter 8, PDF pp. 180–181). Grit is redefined rather than rejected: he says it is not about persisting with something hopeless (Chapter 8, PDF p. 181).
- **Cooperation is not the whole answer.** He explicitly declines to ignore competition, pointing to research on "coopetition" and to rewarding individual, team, and organization-wide performance together; Netflix's trust policies, he notes, lead to small problems once in a blue moon (Chapter 9, PDF pp. 201–202).
- **CSR is not to be avoided, only watched.** Having found moral licensing among existing employees, he says his advice is simply to be aware of the possibility and monitor it closely (Chapter 9, PDF pp. 207–208).
- **The apology finding is bounded and partly untested.** Apologies should be used strategically and ideally only after unexpectedly bad outcomes unlikely to repeat; three or more in a short period is worse than none (Chapter 9, PDF p. 212). He states he has not run the workplace version and that the recommendation for apologizing to employees stands to reason rather than resting on his data (Chapter 9, PDF p. 213).
- **Lyft Pink is unresolved in the book.** Writing as the pandemic eased, he says only time and data will tell whether it is scalable, and that the JoGoods-to-NoGoods ratio will decide (Chapter 2, PDF p. 54).
- **Peltzman is flagged as contested.** The paper is described as controversial at the time and politicized by advocates on both sides (Chapter 4, PDF p. 88).
- **His own view of Uber is qualified.** He says his arrangement kept him away from headquarters most of the time, that as someone who is not a woman, a queer person, or a person of color he was not acutely aware of the power imbalances, and that he missed a lot; he includes himself among the leaders who did not question the culture (Chapter 9, PDF pp. 193, 198). He also states he does not believe Kalanick is a bad person (Chapter 9, PDF p. 199).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, or company the book leans on, checked in September 2026. Where the book names a source in its Notes (PDF pp. 223–252), the check is against that source first. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure, and the reason is given. This session's web-search budget was exhausted before this section was written, so every check below was made by fetching a named URL directly; that is the reason several items are unchecked.

- **Virgin Atlantic: 7,700 tons of fuel, $5.37 million in fuel costs, more than 21,500 metric tons of carbon dioxide, across 335 pilots, some 40,000 flights, and over 100,000 pilot decisions (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 138–139).** Checked against the source the book's endnote names, Gosnell, List and Metcalfe, NBER Working Paper 22316 (June 2016). Three of the figures match exactly: the paper reports "more than 110,000 observations of behavior across 335 captains" and "over 40,000 unique flights", and gives "$5.37 million in fuel savings". Two do not quite: the paper's introduction says "more than 6.8 million kilograms of fuel saved" and "more than 21 million kg of CO2 abated", where the book prints 7,700 tons and more than 21,500 metric tons. The published version, Journal of Political Economy (2020), returned HTTP 403 and could not be opened to see whether it carries the higher numbers, so the gap is unresolved. One difference of attribution is visible in the source and not in the book: the paper's abstract attributes only 266,000–704,000 kg of fuel and 838,000–2.22 million kg of carbon dioxide to the three treatments, and assigns the rest to a monitoring effect, noting that "Company policies precluded the designation of an uninformed control group".
- **The replication crisis: of a hundred experiments, only thirty-nine produced the results reported in the published study (Chapter 1, PDF p. 39).** The cited source is Open Science Collaboration, "Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science", *Science* 349, no. 6251 (2015), which returned HTTP 403. Two independent summaries of that paper give its headline figure as 36 percent of the 97 original studies that had significant effects, not 39 of 100 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproducibility_Project). The 39 percent that circulates alongside it is a subjective-judgment rate over effects rather than a count of experiments, and because the abstract itself could not be opened, that reading is unchecked at the source.
- **D.A.R.E.: 43 million children from over forty countries in twenty-four years, and the program did not reduce drug use (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 24–25).** The ineffectiveness finding holds for the curriculum the book describes, and the program changed: D.A.R.E. adopted the "keepin' it REAL" curriculum in 2012, federal funding was greatly reduced in the early 2000s on the ineffectiveness evidence, and a 2023 study is reported to find the new curriculum deters the start of alcohol use and vaping (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D.A.R.E.). The 43-million count comes from a 2007 annual report the book reaches through a Wayback capture; no current reach figure was found, so the count is unchecked against a present-day source.
- **Brian Wansink: nineteen retractions as of writing, six by JAMA in one day in 2018, and Cornell's misconduct finding (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 40–41).** The finding holds word for word: Cornell concluded on September 20, 2018 that the practices included "data falsification, a failure to assure data accuracy and integrity, inappropriate attribution of authorship of research publications, inappropriate research methods, failure to obtain necessary research approvals, and dual publication". He resigned effective June 30, 2019 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Wansink). That page records seventeen further retractions beyond the first batch without stating a single current total, so the exact count today is unchecked.

Holmes was convicted on January 3, 2022 of four counts of wire fraud and one of conspiracy, sentenced in November 2022 to 11 years and 3 months, and reported to Federal Prison Camp, Bryan, on May 30, 2023; Sunny Balwani was convicted in July 2022 on all twelve counts and sentenced to 12 years and 11 months (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theranos).

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Correlation presented as cause.** Kmart's Blue Light Special is said to have short-circuited its own proven scalability when the corporate office took the product choice from store managers (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 56–57); there is no control, no date, and no mention of anything else happening to Kmart over the same decades. Jamie's Italian is attributed to Simon Blagden's departure and Paul Hunt's appointment, with app-driven delivery named in one clause and no counterfactual offered (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 71–72). The two Bahian villages are a cross-sectional comparison of two places, and the direction of causation — that daily teamwork instilled prosocial behavior rather than a prior cultural difference shaping both the fishing and the games — is asserted rather than shown (Chapter 9, PDF pp. 191–192).
- **Headline figures with no endnote.** The Dominican Republic result, the book's single largest claimed intervention at more than $100 million, carries no note of any kind in the Chapter 6 notes, which cite almost every other experiment in the chapter (PDF pp. 134–135, 240–242). The Home Energy Report meta-analysis — thirty-eight field experiments, some 250,000 homes, 2.4 percent average reduction, 35 to 55 percent persistence — carries none either (PDF pp. 139–140). The instruction to assume operating costs will run at least 10 percent above expectation is offered with no source and no derivation, inside a chapter whose whole argument is for measuring (Chapter 5, PDF p. 116).
- **A range wide enough to carry little information, used as the opening fact.** The claim that between 50 and 90 percent of programs will lose voltage at scale, which sets up the entire book, is sourced to a 2018 post by Straight Talk on Evidence rather than to a study, and the interval spans nearly the whole possible answer (Introduction, PDF p. 16).
- **A figure that does not match its own source.** The book reports that of a hundred high-profile experiments only thirty-nine produced the published results (Chapter 1, PDF p. 39). The cited paper's headline figure, per the summaries reachable in section 7, is 36 percent of the 97 original studies that had significant effects; the 39 that the book converts into a count out of a hundred is a percentage of effects under a different, subjective criterion.
- **A dated fact and a mis-sized one in the same sentence.** El Bulli is said to have closed in 2010 with a reported one million reservation attempts a year against 8,000 spots (Chapter 3, PDF p. 70); the last service was 30 July 2011 and the commonly reported demand figure is more than two million.
- **Support asserted rather than shown.** Having called Peltzman's paper controversial, the book says much research in the intervening years has borne out similar conclusions in other domains and offers two studies, a bicycle-helmet paper and a NASCAR paper (Chapter 4, PDF p. 88). Neither the size of the effect nor the long dispute over it is discussed, and the chapter's whole opening case rests on the finding.
- **A story the book flags and then uses anyway.** The Zambian hovercraft case is introduced as a public policy urban legend of sorts, with the purchase marked as supposed, and then carries the contrast against the polio vaccine that the chapter's cost argument turns on (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 117–118).
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** That tipping did not improve service rests on customer ratings alone, and the author states the team was not sure why and that it contradicted years of hospitality management research (Chapter 6, PDF p. 131). The apology findings are extended from riders to employees on the stated basis that it stands to reason, with no experiment (Chapter 9, PDF p. 213). The prediction that publishing managers' salaries yields a voltage gain at scale is an extrapolation from one bank's ninety-day study through an argument about shrinking manager-to-employee ratios (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 97–98).
- **The sample of evidence is the author's own work.** Almost every case in Part Two is an experiment he ran or co-authored: Virgin Atlantic, the Dominican Republic, Wanlida, Kampala, the Chicago Heights teachers and students, the Flossmoor canvassers, the EEO and CSR job postings, the Uber apology trial, the Bahian villages, and the Freakonomics coin flip, with the co-authors listed in the acknowledgments (PDF pp. 220–222). Chapter 1 argues that the person with the idea should not be its only tester and that independent replication is crucial for businesses as well as scientists (PDF p. 44), and Part Two's findings are presented without independent replication. Counter-examples from the same body of work — his own field experiments that did not hold up — do not appear, in a book with a section on the file-drawer problem (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 39–40).
- **One framing chosen over the source's own.** The Virgin Atlantic result is presented as what the nudge design produced; the working paper the book cites attributes the bulk of the saving to a monitoring effect that reached every captain, including the control group, and records that company policy precluded an uninformed control group at all (section 7 above).
- **Five signs that are listed as four.** The Five Vital Signs are named as five throughout, but every recap lists four items, because representativeness of population and representativeness of situation occupy two chapters and one slot in the list. The recap at Chapter 5, PDF p. 122 names false positives, representativeness of population and situation, spillovers, and costs; Chapter 8, PDF p. 185 names the same four with overgeneralizing across people and situations in the middle slot; and the Conclusion, PDF pp. 216–217 names them a third time, again four. A reader counting from the recaps cannot reconstruct the five.
