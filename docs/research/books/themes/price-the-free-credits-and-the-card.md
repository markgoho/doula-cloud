# Theme synthesis: price, the free Credits, and the card

## What this file is for

This is the second stage of ADR-0041 for one theme on the values track. It lays out what each of the twenty-nine values books claims about how a product like this one is priced, whether a person tries it free, and whether a card is taken before value is delivered; where the books agree; where they conflict; and what the repo has already decided. It contains no ruling. Page citations follow each read-back's own page rule, stated in its Source line. The read-backs are in `docs/research/books/`. Where an agreement or conflict bullet names Walling without a title, it means the two 2023 books, *The SaaS Playbook* and *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*, which were the only Walling books on the track when the bullet was written; *Start Small, Stay Small* is named by title wherever it is meant. A bare Blank means *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* and Blank and Dorf means *The Startup Owner's Manual*; a bare Miller means *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* and Miller and Peterson means *Marketing Made Simple*; Dib is named by title or as both of his books wherever the two differ.

## The question

What does a Practice pay, per what unit, at what moment, and what does a stranger get before paying anything?

## What each book claims

**The SaaS Playbook** (Walling, 2023). Price at $50 a month or more for breathing room, and $10–15 only for a no-touch consumer product; if nobody complains about the price it is too low (Pricing, pp. 67–68). Choose a value metric, and use seats only if two users from one company see different things (pp. 70–71). Freemium suits a simple, low-support, viral product; 66% of surveyed SaaS offer a trial and 17% a forever-free plan (pp. 73–74). A card up front is the default and a qualifying event; trials without one multiply support tenfold (pp. 75–78). No trial at all, with a refund policy in the first thirty days, runs the most pricing experiments (p. 78). Revisit pricing every six to twelve months; aspirational pricing builds the product up to the price (pp. 79–80). Grandfather unless the increase grows MRR by at least 10%, never promise it for life, and announce two to four months ahead (pp. 83–85).

**Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding** (Walling, 2023 ed.). Free plans convert at about 1% and drain support; ask for money up front ("Why Free Plans Don't Work", pp. 37–42). $1 a month cannot work; $50–100 a month is better; when asked to lower the price, build up to it instead ("You Can't Make Money Charging $1", pp. 146–148).

**Make: The Bootstrapper's Handbook** (Levels). Do not be afraid to charge; do not lower prices or go free, because the complainers are not the customers; expect no more than 5% of active users to pay (Monetize, pp. 133–135). Build with monetization in mind from the start, tested with a real Buy button that stops short of charging (pp. 136–138). Subscription revenue is the seller's holy grail but forgotten subscriptions may be half of it; a one-time lifetime price at predicted lifetime value is the author's middle ground, and pay-per-feature has potential (pp. 138–139, 145–149).

**Landing Page Hot Tips.** Offer more tiers, suggest one, minimize the options, add an annual discount, and consider a lifetime price (Tips #14, #50, #58, #32, #88; PDF pp. 39–41, 95–97, 109, 67, 161). Offer a free teaser, remove the obstacles to a demo, and lower the risk to commit with a free trial, no card, and a money-back guarantee (Tips #40, #46, #47; PDF pp. 83, 91, 92). The first question a SaaS visitor asks is how much it costs, answered with full transparency (Tip #85, PDF p. 157).

**Jobs To Be Done** (Ulwick, 2016). A product wins by getting the job done better or more cheaply; a dominant strategy is at least 20% better and 20% cheaper (ch. 3, pp. 62–63, 73). The buyer weighs 40 to 80 financial outcomes, and the outcome survey carries willingness-to-pay questions (ch. 2, p. 61; ch. 6, pp. 170, 172).

**Design for Cognitive Bias** (Thomas, 2020). Whatever is incentivized gets gamed, and a measure made a target ceases to measure (ch. 3, pp. 41, 49). Three plans laid out so one is visually favored steer the buyer toward the business's choice; the test is whether the designer would disclose why (ch. 4, pp. 87–88).

**Copyhackers: Uplift.** No position on price, trial, or card. The book is about page copy; its nearest claim is that the reader's stage of awareness decides how much a page says (Section 1, p. 11; Section 5, p. 48), which the marketing theme carries.

**The Almanack of Naval Ravikant** (Jorgenson, 2020). No position on price or trial mechanics. Its nearest claim, that wealth comes from giving society something it wants and cannot yet get (Building Wealth, pp. 31, 39), sits in the moat theme.

**The Best Interface Is No Interface** (Krishna, 2015). No position on price, trial, or card.

**Obviously Awesome** (Dunford, 2019). Price is one of the assumptions a market category carries rather than a figure set on its own: declaring a category triggers assumptions about competitors, features, and price, and the CRM example assumes "a monthly price per user" (Components, pp. 49–50). Price expectations differ by category, and a positioning shift moves the price with it — the CRM for investment banks was priced above a general-purpose CRM (Step 8, p. 104; After Positioning, p. 132). The same product framed differently is worth a different amount to the same person, which is what the Joshua Bell subway performance and the cake pop are offered to show (Positioning as Context, pp. 26–27, 35–37). The book takes no position on a trial or a card.

**Building a StoryBrand 2.0** (Miller, 2025). A page carries a direct call to action and a transitional one beside it, and a limited-time free trial is a transitional call to action that works as a risk-removal policy and on-ramps the customer (ch. 8, PDF p. 123). The Buy Now button is a different and preferably brighter color from every other button on the page, all such buttons identical, and repeated as the page scrolls (ch. 8, PDF pp. 116–117; ch. 14, PDF pp. 178–180). An agreement plan is a list of agreements that alleviate a customer's fear of doing business and may be titled as a guarantee, though unlike a process plan it often works in the background and does not have to be on the home page (ch. 7, PDF pp. 110–111). A service business packages its services, names them, and puts the resulting menu of products on the website (ch. 14, PDF p. 181). A sales email's call to action carries scarcity, and the printed example's strength is that it is a onetime offer (ch. 15, PDF pp. 207–208).

**Lean Marketing** (Dib, 2024). Price is a signal of quality and of status, cost-plus pricing is "a common mistake" against value-based pricing, and customers "don't care about your cost structure" (Chapter 3, pp. 70, 72). Positioning as the cheapest is to be done "with extreme caution" and only on "some unique input cost advantage"; low-price customers "complain the most, pay late" and need the most support, and the mediocre middle "is the worst place to be" (Chapter 3, pp. 69–70). A flagship asset is "typically" free "but it doesn't have to be" (Chapter 8, p. 135); a taster, trial, or sample is "a better use of resources" early on than a flagship experience (Chapter 8, p. 139); and most users of a free tool "may never become paying customers", though many will (Chapter 8, p. 140). A trial that is never set up is churn, the case being a 30-day SaaS trial taken with a card, never used because the data import looked daunting, then canceled (Chapter 14, pp. 231–232). A business with no customers yet gives the product "away for free to a select group" in the target market in exchange for "an honest review" (Chapter 14, pp. 237–238). A products or services page carries pricing, or a price range where the pricing is custom (Chapter 9, pp. 150, 152–153). Monthly recurring revenue and churn are the two subscription metrics, with churn as the leak in the MRR bucket (Chapter 15, p. 257), and lifetime value calculated from revenue is normally a mistake, excepting "(typically venture-backed) businesses" focused wholly on growth (Chapter 15, p. 255).

**The 1-Page Marketing Plan** (Dib, 2018 edition). Price is a positioning indicator, not a markup on cost and not a step below the market leader (Chapter 6, p. 190). Lowest price is a losing unique selling proposition for a small business, because it would require guaranteeing lower prices on everything against the largest discounters, and the better answer is to raise the value of the offering (Chapter 2, p. 81; Chapter 6, p. 193); discounting is allowed under a deliberate loss-leader strategy (Chapter 6, p. 193), and thin margins on a core product are not recommended (Chapter 8, p. 243). A single pricing option is named an error: the book offers a standard and a premium about 50% apart, with two or three variants as the happy medium against brain overload (Chapter 6, pp. 191–192; Chapter 8, p. 244), and an ultra-high-ticket item beside them, because about 10% of customers would pay ten times more (Chapter 6, pp. 192–193). An unlimited variant at a fixed price removes the fear of overage, bounded by fair-use terms (Chapter 6, p. 192). Prices are raised periodically with a stated reason why, some customers leave, and grandfathering is the option for an owner who fears the reaction (Chapter 8, pp. 240–241). A specific guarantee reverses the risk better than a vague satisfaction promise, and is risky only for a business that consistently does a poor job (Chapter 6, pp. 187–189). A free trial is the puppy dog close, putting the onus on the buyer to reverse the sale (Chapter 6, pp. 195–197). Every business is told to carry a subscription or recurring element (Chapter 8, pp. 247, 255).

**The Mom Test** (Fitzpatrick, 2013). Asking how much someone would pay is a bad question, and adding a number to it does not help; the usable price signal is what a person pays today for the workaround, which is also the alternative and the anchor (ch. 1, pp. 16, 19–20). Commitment is giving up something valued, in the currencies of time, reputation, and money, and a meeting has succeeded when it ends in one (ch. 5, pp. 64–66). A trial counts as commitment only in proportion to what it costs the person: the author records trying countless contact-management apps at a cost of ten seconds each, and says a cheap trial has to be made more expensive before it means anything (ch. 5, p. 70). Enterprise sales carries the perk of accurate early signals while consumer products are much murkier, because the conversation does not mimic the purchase process (ch. 5, p. 71). The idea does eventually have to be mentioned and money taken for it; the claim is about order, not about silence (ch. 1, p. 13).

**Competing Against Luck** (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan, 2016). A clear view of the job means never overshooting what a customer will pay for, and a customer whose job is solved is often willing to pay more (ch. 3, PDF p. 66; ch. 6, PDF pp. 122–123). Not every premium is the same kind: one extracted through interdependence, as with printer ink, chargers, and phone cases, creates anxiety rather than resolving it, and the customer pays while resenting the company (ch. 6, PDF p. 123). A brand that fails to integrate around a job becomes a category placeholder competing on price (ch. 6, PDF pp. 132–134). Cost and efficiency are not core elements of a job, and where a decision rests almost entirely on mathematical analysis the theory is stated to be useless (ch. 2, PDF p. 48). Hiring anything new means firing something, including the habit of doing nothing, and loss aversion weighs about twice as heavily as gain (ch. 5, PDF pp. 93–95). The book takes no position on a trial or a card.

**Forget the Funnel** (Laudi and Suellentrop, 2023). A marketing funnel accounts only for the search and the purchase, and a recurring-revenue business needs the customer to keep paying, so treating marketing as something that stops at signup is a serious mistake (ch. 1, PDF pp. 21–22); the relationship is better pictured as a circle than a line, because the growth phase after purchase is where such a business defies normal growth (ch. 7, PDF p. 104; ch. 10, PDF p. 125). The best customers to learn from are the ones who struggled with the problem, "pay without hesitation", have an ongoing need, have reached value realization, and started paying three to six months ago (ch. 3, PDF pp. 40–41). Buyer journey maps and pirate metrics are said to reward transactional moments such as signup or paid over customer value moments (ch. 6, PDF pp. 73–75), and value realization is rarely immediate, which is why a lower First Value milestone exists (ch. 7, PDF pp. 101–102). A Try-it-free call to action replacing a Schedule-a-demo one is a named way to bridge the Interest milestone (ch. 8, PDF pp. 113–114). ARR, CAC, ARPU, and lifetime value are called critical to watch and not actionable (ch. 7, PDF p. 97).

**Marketing Made Simple** (Miller with Peterson, 2020). A site publishes three price options with bullet points of what each includes, or publishes no prices at all; the middle item is the one that sells most often, a finding the authors attribute to what StoryBrand's certified guides have discovered rather than to a study, and custom pricing or a long catalog is a reason to leave prices off the site entirely (ch. 5, p. 103). Price Choices is one of only two website sections the book marks optional (ch. 5, pp. 59–60). A lead generator trades something genuinely valuable for an email address, to create trust and reciprocity before any money is asked for (ch. 6, pp. 108–110, 118), and its hit rate is given as roughly six in ten (ch. 6, p. 125). A direct request for the sale is called honest about a relationship that is transactional by nature, with the funnel controlling the pace so the request is never premature (ch. 2, pp. 26–28), and a sales campaign converts a minority, rejection being the expected outcome of asking (ch. 9, p. 147). The last sales email carries a buying deadline with an expiring bonus in the postscript (ch. 9, pp. 149, 151), a limited-time offer being conditional on whether one can be made at all (ch. 9, p. 149).

**This Is Marketing** (Godin, 2018). Price is a marketing tool rather than a way to collect money, on the pair of claims that marketing changes pricing and pricing changes marketing, and the price aligns with the extremes claimed in positioning (Ch. 16, PDF p. 145). Margin is the engine: the baker at $1.95 of ingredients and overhead per loaf earns a nickel at two dollars, fifty-five cents at $2.50, and more than a dollar at three, so the two-dollar baker must sell twenty-one loaves for each loaf the three-dollar baker sells, and the margin is then spent visibly on what the buyer sees, the author's summary being that apologizing once for the price beats excusing a hundred small slights (Ch. 16, PDF pp. 145-146). Cheap is another way to say scared, because nothing is easier to sell than cheaper and being cheapest promises the same rather than a change, with an explicit exception for a seller who has found an extraordinary new way to deliver the thing (Ch. 16, PDF p. 147). Free draws two objections — a transaction differs fundamentally from encountering a freely shared object, since scarcity, tension, and enrollment exist only when a purchase is decided on, and without cash flow there is nothing to invest in product, team, or marketing — and is then called a different category rather than a lower price, because it scales to infinity; the structure offered is free ideas that spread paired with expensive expressions of them, the chef giving away recipes while charging twenty-four dollars for the pasta on china (Ch. 16, PDF pp. 147-149). Trust runs opposite to intuition, because people invent trust when they are spending a lot, so lowering a price does not make a seller more trusted but does the opposite (Ch. 16, PDF p. 149). Telling buyers that the price is the price is itself a story, the single price tag being credited to the Quakers, and the hard part for a small organization is the storytelling rather than the mechanics, tested by how it feels to receive a discount nobody else got or to miss one others received (Ch. 16, PDF pp. 146-147). A person who understands the offer and declines is not a failure of the marketing: it is not for them, not at that price or with that structure (Ch. 5, PDF p. 43). A subscription is named an overt act of permission, which is why subscribers are worth more than newsstand buyers (Ch. 17, PDF p. 154). The funnel is called unlikely to be a magical fountain, because lifetime value rarely exceeds the cost of the ads needed to win a customer (Ch. 19, PDF p. 167), and the upset customer over a small sum is worth the refund rather than the argument (Ch. 14, PDF p. 134; Ch. 19, PDF p. 165).

**Start Small, Stay Small** (Walling, 2010). The chapter gives explicit price ceilings for online sales with no salesperson, each qualified by "unless you solve a serious pain": about $29 or $14/month for consumer hobby products, about $49 or $19/month where a consumer saves money, about $400 or $99/month for small businesses, and about $1,000 or $199/month for larger ones (Ch. 3, p. 97). The twelve-step pricing process computes the value delivered as a ceiling, combines a range whose top is four times its bottom, leans high, uses three tiers doubling each time, ends every price in 7, 8 or 9, makes each tier more than double the previous benefit, adds an annual support fee of about 20% of a one-time price, and tests (Ch. 3, pp. 98–100); the reason for tiers is the author's own admission that there is no single best price for a product, and the ending-in-7-8-or-9 rule is introduced as "stupid" and kept because it works (Ch. 3, p. 99). Price testing depends on the sales cycle: under a week, change it silently for one or two weeks; longer, raise it with a new version while offering the old price to the mailing list for seven days, or cut it and refund the difference to anyone who writes in (Ch. 3, pp. 100–101). Business software starts as a hosted web application on a monthly or annual recurring fee, for the steady revenue and the single deployment (Ch. 3, pp. 101–103). Failing a free trial, the pricing page carries a 30-day, no-questions-asked money-back guarantee, because the buyers it wins outnumber the people who abuse it (Ch. 4, pp. 131–133), and that page also gets large type, few words, a highlighted plan, and an upsell (Ch. 4, pp. 131–132). The website's first goal, ahead of the sale, is an email address, won with trust, relevance, and a reward (Ch. 4, pp. 118–119). Conversion runs 1–4% for consumer prices and 0.5–2% above $50 or on recurring pricing, while a targeted launch mailing can convert 20%+ (Ch. 2, p. 67; Ch. 4, p. 144). A build under 200 hours is a warning as well as a good sign, because it may mean a tool rather than an application, and tools are hard to charge for (Ch. 3, p. 93).

**Software as a Science** (Martell, Verlaque, Page, and Petitpas, 2024). The authors' blunt position is that a reader of the book should probably raise prices, and that nobody saying no means the price is too low (ch. 9, PDF p. 192); not changing a price is effectively a price decrease, most bootstrapped companies update pricing once every three years, and companies that adjust quarterly are said to grow four times faster (ch. 10, PDF pp. 217–218, 227). The Pricing Triangle is a value metric, depth-of-usage drivers, and feature fencing (ch. 9, PDF pp. 195–196, 205). A good value metric grows with usage, is easy to track, is easy for the customer to understand, is predictable, is perceived as fair, and grows when the customer's own business grows (ch. 9, PDF p. 203), with a tenfold return as the target (ch. 9, PDF pp. 199–200). The chapter's central warning is not to charge on the value metric directly where doing so makes the customer use the product less: Netflix prices on account size rather than hours watched, and Slack gives unlimited messaging away while capping integrations and history at ninety days (ch. 9, PDF pp. 197–198, 201–202). Three plan levels are the shape, and a feature most customers do not need belongs outside the plans as an add-on rather than bundled into the top one (ch. 9, PDF pp. 205–206). Onboarding is charged at 10 to 20 percent of annual contract value, and priority support is a paid add-on about a fifth of customers take (ch. 11, PDF pp. 233–234, 242). A credit card is asked for on the sales call itself, because every drop-off point is a chance to lose the deal (ch. 5, PDF p. 114). On an increase, the email quantifies the value delivered and then says plainly that this is a price increase, never hidden at the bottom and never blamed on inflation; loyalty is rewarded with six months of the old price, because churn from an increase clusters immediately after it, and permanent grandfathering is named as what forces later corrections above 50 percent (ch. 10, PDF pp. 222–224). A 20 percent increase absorbs losing 17 percent of clients, a 30 percent increase 23 percent, and doubling the price 50 percent (ch. 10, PDF p. 218), while NPS takes a large hit above 50 percent and a salvage offer bridging three to six months is the prepared exception (ch. 10, PDF p. 224). An increase is also the moment to sell a year of the current price paid in advance, bypassing the customary annual discount and pulling cash forward (ch. 10, PDF pp. 225–226). Where usage data is missing, the first price is a SWAG, which the authors say is how most founders set one, tested on new customers only (ch. 9, PDF pp. 193, 209).

**The Lean Startup** (Ries, 2011). A business model buries an assumption in one spreadsheet cell — the share of people who see a free trial and sign up for it — and the instruction is to state it as a leap of faith and test it before building the features behind it (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 97). Charging from day one is named among the IMVU tactics readers most want to copy, and the author calls those tactics useful but not the moral, with "too many exceptions" (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF p. 62). For a viral product, charging is described as friction the viral loop cannot afford, so the value hypothesis has to be tested by a nonmonetary exchange, and the author says it would have been foolish for Facebook or Hotmail to charge early (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 207). IMVU's early revenue is reported as running only to about $8,000 a month, significant as a prediction rather than as revenue (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 95-96). Cohort analysis is called the gold standard of learning metrics, because it follows each group of people who met the product independently rather than reporting cumulative totals (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 121-122, 142).

**The Four Steps to the Epiphany** (Blank, second edition, 2013). The pricing hypothesis asks what customers pay today for a product somewhat like the one being built, or what the piece-part solutions would sum to, and it is framed on lifetime value rather than on the first sale; no objection at all to a price may mean it is too low (Chapter 3, pp. 161–163; Chapter 4, p. 327). The word customer is reserved for people who pay: giveaways do not prove customers will buy, and the goal is near list price from visionary customers (Chapter 4, pp. 299, 301, 310). The book is tough on discounts and flexible on terms — no payment until delivery, or until the product works as specified (Chapter 4, pp. 310–311). Market Type changes the cash needs and the shape of the sales growth curve, and resegmenting an existing market as a low-cost entrant is one of the four types (Chapter 2, pp. 87, 90). The minimum feature set is the smallest or least complicated problem the customer will pay to have solved (Chapter 3, pp. 157–158; Appendix B, p. 616).

**The Startup Owner's Manual** (Blank and Dorf, 2012). The pricing tactics named are value pricing and competitive pricing, so the price is asked to reflect the value delivered and what competitors charge (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 373). Free-to-paid conversion is called sexy but dangerous, and the authors say mapping profitable conversion of free to paid users makes or breaks the company (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 280). The first paying customers are earlyvangelists, a small subset of visionary buyers who have the problem, know they have it, and have cobbled together a makeshift solution, and failure to sell to them is treated as decisive (Strategy Guide, Ch. 4, PDF pp. 99-102, and Ch. 5, PDF p. 123). Customer validation is proved by orders or usage rather than by opinions, with per-category thresholds for how much response counts as enough (Strategy Guide, Ch. 5, PDF p. 124; Web/Mobile, Ch. 6, PDF p. 445). Velocity in acquisition has two named exceptions, niche vertical sites and high-value products such as enterprise software or expensive jewelry, where a few high-value customers may be enough (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420). A multi-sided market optimizes the side that generates the cash, and the audience side is verified separately from the paying side (Web/Mobile, Ch. 9, PDF p. 576). Market type is a late-binding decision that may be deferred to customer creation, though the cost of choosing wrong is called severe (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 287).

**Zero to One** (Thiel with Masters, 2014). A monopoly owns its market and sets its own price, while a firm in a competitive market must sell at whatever price the market determines (ch. 3, PDF pp. 20–21), and competition is the condition in which no company makes an economic profit (ch. 3, PDF p. 21). The sales model has to match the price, and the chapter names an admitted dead zone, the distribution doldrums around a $1,000 product sold to small businesses, where advertising is too broad or too inefficient and personal sales too expensive — offered as the reason small and medium-sized businesses do not use tools bigger firms take for granted (ch. 11, PDF pp. 99–101). Advertising works for a startup when acquisition cost and lifetime value make every other channel uneconomical, Warby Parker being the case (ch. 11, PDF pp. 102–103). Distribution follows a power law of its own: one channel dominates, the kitchen-sink approach fails, and poor sales rather than bad product is the most common cause of failure (ch. 11, PDF p. 104). The book takes no position on a trial or a card.

**Company of One** (Jarvis, 2019). Fixed, published prices build trust where negotiated ones do not, and Mazda's move to one price is the case (ch. 10, PDF p. 117); publishing what you charge where anyone can compare it is itself the trust move, with WealthSimple and Ellevest as the examples (ch. 10, PDF pp. 116–117). A free version feeds word of mouth and converts without much extra effort, and Trello is the case (ch. 7, PDF p. 89). Retention beats acquisition and renewals are the metric worth watching, on the Econsultancy figure of a new customer costing five times an existing one (ch. 4, PDF pp. 55–56; ch. 7, PDF p. 86). Simplicity sells, the worked example being Casper making three mattresses rather than 108 and explaining why theirs is better instead of what a mattress is (ch. 1, PDF pp. 25–27; ch. 11, PDF p. 130). The model is stated as neither anti-growth nor anti-revenue, only as questioning growth first (ch. 1, PDF p. 18).

**Buy Back Your Time** (Martell, 2023). No position on price, trial, or card. Its nearest claim is the Buyback Rate, what the business pays the founder in salary, profits, and discretionary expenses divided by 2,000 work hours and then divided by four (ch. 2, PDF pp. 55–56), which the founder-time-hiring-funding theme carries.

**Entrepreneur Revolution** (Priestley, third edition, 2024). The core offer is never sold at break-even or a loss, and absent funding it usually generates over $1,000 of revenue per customer per year, the figure carrying the explicit condition unless the business has funding (Chapter 5, PDF p. 120). Below the core offer sit two steps that may lose small money: a gift, which is free and asks nothing back, and a product for prospects, a low-cost first step that gives a quick win inside seven days without cannibalizing the core offer (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 116–118, 120). A lead capture process sits between the free gift and the first paid step, and the book asks a business for several ways to collect contact details (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 122–123). A service is structured repeatably and given a name so that it resembles a product, because the customer buys an outcome (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 107–108). Roughly 1 in 20 of an audience buys at once, so an audience has to exceed 200 people for every 10 sales wanted at launch, and the lead-to-sale rate the author sees across industries is under 5% (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 96, 104). Money taken for something not yet secured is set aside so that it can be returned (Chapter 4, PDF p. 100).

**The Voltage Effect** (List, 2022). To scale, find out not only how many people like the idea but what they are willing to pay for it and how much it will cost to provide (Chapter 5, PDF p. 111). Where scale economies are built in, price low to get customers in the door, which the author says is why many software products run a freemium strategy, and do due diligence on the choke price, the price at which demand drops to zero, with tests on how different prices influence demand (Chapter 5, PDF p. 116). The chapter's case is Arivale, which charged about $3,500 a year, could not lower the price without losing money because coaches cannot be mass-produced, cut to $1,200 a year or $99 a month in 2018, and shut in April 2019 saying the cost of providing the program exceeded what customers could pay (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 104–111); the accompanying advice is to take stock of fixed costs at the outset and to assume operating costs will run at least 10 percent above expectation (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 115–116). A membership scales only where it gives access to goods or services not readily available without it, and of six membership prices tested on 1.2 million riders not one would scale (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 48, 52–53). The clawback gives the money before it is earned so that it is the recipient's to lose, and the loss framing beat the identical bonus offered conventionally (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 143–144, 147–148). A ceiling is not a failure: many businesses have built-in limitations, and an idea can still serve a purpose and make money with one (Chapter 2, PDF p. 55).

**Thinking in Bets** (Duke, 2018). No position on price, trial, or card. Its nearest claim is that a belief carries a stated level of confidence and is recalibrated as information arrives rather than being reversed wholesale from right to wrong (ch. 2, PDF pp. 62–63), which its read-back attaches to the record holding the Credit price as provisional rather than to any claim about what a price is.

## Where the books agree

- Charge money, and do not go down when asked: Walling in *The SaaS Playbook* and *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*, Levels, and Hot Tips all say build up to the price rather than lower it, and Walling in *Start Small, Stay Small* leans the range high on the same reasoning. Dib says the same in both of his — cost-plus is a mistake and the mediocre middle is the worst place to be in *Lean Marketing*, lowest price is a losing unique selling proposition in *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* — and Godin calls cheap another way to say scared, while *Software as a Science* says a reader should probably raise prices.
- A forever-free plan is a support cost, not a channel: Walling in *The SaaS Playbook* and *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*; Levels expects at most 5% of active users to pay.
- Nothing given away proves a customer: *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* reserves the word customer for people who pay and says giveaways do not prove customers will buy, *The Mom Test* says a trial counts as commitment only in proportion to what it costs the person and a cheap one has to be made more expensive before it means anything, and Godin says scarcity, tension, and enrollment exist only when a purchase is decided on.
- The anchor is what the buyer already pays for the job, not what the seller spent: Dunford says a market category carries price assumptions with it, Godin aligns price with the extremes claimed in positioning, *Competing Against Luck* says never overshoot what a customer will pay for, Dib says customers do not care about your cost structure, Blank's pricing hypothesis asks what customers pay today for something like it, *The Mom Test* names that same figure as the only usable price signal, and *The Voltage Effect* asks what people are willing to pay before the idea is scaled.
- A price is revisited on a schedule rather than left alone: Walling's *SaaS Playbook* says every six to twelve months, *Software as a Science* says most bootstrapped companies wait three years and that not changing a price is effectively a decrease, *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* says raise it periodically with a stated reason, and Walling in *Start Small, Stay Small* tests it by changing it. Walling's if-nobody-complains-it-is-too-low, Blank's no-objection-at-all, and *Software as a Science*'s nobody-saying-no are the same test in three wordings.
- Fewer choices, one suggested: Hot Tips's suggested tier and Thomas's warning about a visually favored plan describe the same layout from opposite sides; *Marketing Made Simple* says three options with the middle one selling most often, *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* says a standard and a premium about 50% apart with two or three variants the happy medium, *Start Small, Stay Small* says three tiers doubling each time, and *Software as a Science* says three plan levels.
- A refund is a legitimate substitute for a trial: Walling (p. 78) and Hot Tips's money-back guarantee, joined by Walling in *Start Small, Stay Small*, where a 30-day no-questions guarantee is what the pricing page carries failing a free trial, by *The 1-Page Marketing Plan*, where a specific guarantee reverses the risk better than a vague satisfaction promise, and by *Building a StoryBrand 2.0*, whose agreement plan may be titled as a guarantee.

## Where the books conflict

- **The card.** Walling wants a card before value, as a qualifying event; *Software as a Science* goes further and asks for it on the sales call itself, because every drop-off point is a chance to lose the deal. Hot Tips wants no card and a money-back promise; *Forget the Funnel* names a Try-it-free call to action replacing a Schedule-a-demo one; and *Lean Marketing*'s own churn story is a 30-day trial taken *with* a card and never set up. Levels tests willingness with a Buy button that charges nothing.
- **Free.** Godin gives free two objections and then calls it a different category rather than a lower price, because it scales to infinity, and pairs free ideas with expensive expressions of them. *Company of One* says a free version feeds word of mouth and converts without much extra effort, and *The Voltage Effect* says to price low where scale economies are built in, which is why many software products run freemium. *Lean Marketing* runs free as a channel too — the flagship asset is "typically" free, and a taster, trial, or sample is a better early use of resources — while naming conversion as the risk, most free users never paying though many will; *The Startup Owner's Manual* calls free-to-paid conversion sexy but dangerous and says mapping it profitably makes or breaks the company. Against all of them, *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* and *The Mom Test* say the free user proves nothing, the first reserving the word customer for people who pay and the second pricing a trial's meaning by what it cost the person; and *The Lean Startup* confines the case for not charging to a viral product whose loop cannot afford the friction.
- **The unit.** Walling's $50 floor and Levels's 5% figure assume a monthly subscription; Levels then argues against subscriptions for the seller's own reasons; Ulwick's method prices an outcome, not a month; none of the three names a per-transaction unit. *Start Small, Stay Small* makes a recurring hosted application the default starting position for business software, *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* says every business is to carry a subscription or recurring element, *Software as a Science* computes a growth ceiling from monthly churn and ARPA, and *Company of One* treats renewals as the metric worth watching — while Godin names a subscription an overt act of permission rather than a billing shape, and Blank prices on lifetime value rather than on the first sale.
- **Tiers.** Hot Tips wants several tiers; Thomas treats a favored tier as a bias to disclose; Walling's value-metric advice does not require tiers at all. *The 1-Page Marketing Plan*, *Marketing Made Simple*, *Start Small, Stay Small*, and *Software as a Science* all want three, and *Software as a Science* adds feature fencing and depth-of-usage drivers under them; *Company of One* argues the other way from simplicity, with Casper making three mattresses rather than 108.
- **Whether the price is published, and where.** *Company of One* says publishing what you charge where anyone can compare it is itself the trust move, Godin says telling buyers the price is the price is a story in its own right, *Lean Marketing* says a products or services page carries pricing or a price range, and Hot Tips wants full transparency. *Marketing Made Simple* offers the alternative in the same sentence as the rule — three price options, or no prices at all — and names custom pricing and a long catalog as reasons to leave them off; *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* puts the offer above the fold while saying an agreement plan need not be on the home page at all.
- **Grandfathering.** Walling grandfathers unless the increase grows MRR by at least 10%, never promises it for life, and announces two to four months ahead; *Software as a Science* gives exactly six months and calls permanent grandfathering the thing that forces later corrections above 50%; *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* offers grandfathering to an owner who fears the reaction, without a term; *Start Small, Stay Small* offers the old price to the mailing list for seven days, or refunds the difference after a cut.
- **Where the evidence comes from.** Walling's percentages are surveys of SaaS companies; Hot Tips's tips are uncited; Ulwick's willingness-to-pay figure comes from a survey run on 180 to 3,000 customers (ch. 4 §IV, p. 98), which the read-back's section 8 notes a one-product company is told to outsource. *Software as a Science*'s pricing figures come from a platform its authors say was connected to nearly one SaaS company in five; *Marketing Made Simple* attributes its middle-item finding to what StoryBrand's certified guides have discovered rather than to a study; *Start Small, Stay Small*'s price ceilings are the author's own, offered because none of the 25 articles he read would give any; *The Voltage Effect*'s membership finding is a field experiment on 1.2 million riders.
