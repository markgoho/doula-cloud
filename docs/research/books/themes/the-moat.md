# Theme synthesis: the moat

## What this file is for

This is the second stage of ADR-0041 for one theme on the values track. It lays out what each of the twenty-nine values books claims about what keeps a customer from leaving, whether features count, and how narrow a first market is; where the books agree; where they conflict; and what the repo has already decided. It contains no ruling. Page citations follow each read-back's own page rule. The read-backs are in `docs/research/books/`. Where an agreement or conflict bullet names Walling without a title, it means the two 2023 books, *The SaaS Playbook* and *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*, which were the only Walling books on the track when the bullet was written; *Start Small, Stay Small* is named by title wherever it is meant. A bare Blank means *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* and Blank and Dorf means *The Startup Owner's Manual*; a bare Miller means *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* and Miller and Peterson means *Marketing Made Simple*; Dib is named by title or as both of his books wherever the two differ.

## The question

What keeps a Practice here once she has tried it, and does the feature list follow from that answer?

## What each book claims

**The SaaS Playbook** (Walling, 2023). Four moats: integrations, brand, owned traffic, and high switching costs; unique features are a false moat (Market, pp. 57–61).

**Make: The Bootstrapper's Handbook** (Levels). Start in a micro niche, such as booking software for hairdressers who focus on African hair, then widen niche by niche and later widen the market (Idea, pp. 25–28; Monetize, p. 159). A business built on another company's API is exposed: they can shut it down at any time, and renaming a key breaks the app (Build, p. 72).

**Jobs To Be Done** (Ulwick, 2016). Comparing competitors' feature sets is a waste of time; table stakes are outcomes very important and very satisfied by existing products (ch. 4 §VI, pp. 103, 105). Customers switch only when a product gets the job done upwards of 20% better; under 5% is a sustaining strategy that entices nobody (ch. 3, p. 78; ch. 2, pp. 48–49). Related jobs make a platform valuable, and the long-term strategy is a solution that gets the entire job done on one platform (ch. 2, p. 58; ch. 4 §III, p. 92; §X, p. 122).

**The Almanack of Naval Ravikant** (Jorgenson, 2020). Escape competition through authenticity; nobody can compete with a person at being that person; avoid frameworks built on what others do (Building Wealth, pp. 41, 44; Saving Yourself, pp. 158–159). Wealth comes from giving society something it wants and cannot yet get; technology is what does not quite work yet (Building Wealth, pp. 31, 39).

**Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding** (Walling, 2023 ed.). Ask customers what they would use instead ("Four Things I Learned", pp. 150–152). No position on moats as such.

**Design for Cognitive Bias** (Thomas, 2020). No position on competition. Its nearest claim, that design for the most vulnerable first (ch. 4, p. 85), is about who the product serves, carried by the who-it-is-for theme.

**Landing Page Hot Tips.** No position on moats. Its social-proof tips assume an established product.

**Copyhackers: Uplift.** No position on moats.

**The Best Interface Is No Interface** (Krishna, 2015). No direct position. Its argument against screen-time engagement (ch. 5) bears on what a "sticky" product optimizes for.

**Obviously Awesome** (Dunford, 2019). The competitive alternative is what target customers would use or do if the product did not exist, and in business software the most common one is general-purpose software plus a manual process — a spreadsheet, an intern, or doing nothing (Components, pp. 45–46; Step 4, pp. 78–79). Unique attributes are the capabilities those alternatives lack, and they can be a delivery model, a business model, or specific expertise as well as a feature (Components, pp. 46–47; Step 5, pp. 80–82). Three positioning styles are named — Head to Head, Big Fish Small Pond, and Create a New Game — where Head to Head is "rarely a good choice" for a small business launching a product and Create a New Game is a last resort that generally requires deep-pocketed, patient investors (Step 8, pp. 97–101, 112, 115). Big Fish, Small Pond wins a subsegment whose needs the category leader does not meet, and word-of-mouth marketing happens most naturally in tight market subsegments (Step 8, pp. 104–106). Narrowness is bounded by the number: targeting is as narrow as the near-term sales objective allows, and thirty deals needed against a hundred businesses means the segment is too small (Step 7, p. 90; Step 8, p. 106); narrow now is not narrow forever, and the widening path from credit unions to retail banks to insurance is given as the normal case (Step 8, pp. 106–107). Retention attributes are excluded from the consideration list while retention still matters, because poorly positioned products are bought by people who misunderstand them and leave (Step 5, pp. 82–83).

**Building a StoryBrand 2.0** (Miller, 2025 updated edition). On whether the feature list counts, the position is that the words rather than the product decide: pretty websites do not sell, and an inferior product wins if its offer is communicated more clearly (ch. 1, PDF pp. 18–19). The controlling idea is derived by asking what the main differentiator is that sets a business apart from the competition (ch. 12, PDF p. 166), and a service business is told to package its services, name them, and put the resulting menu on the website (ch. 14, PDF p. 181). The book takes no position on what keeps a customer from leaving or on how narrow a first market is; its nearest claim on staying is that the seventh campaign step is a referral system — a coupon code, a shareable tool, a reward, or an affiliate program at 10 percent commission (ch. 15, PDF pp. 211–214).

**Lean Marketing** (Dib, 2024). Customers are said to leave less for something done wrong than for having no "reason to stay", and the claim is that many businesses could double or triple revenue without a single new customer (Chapter 14, pp. 230–231). Retention starts at signup rather than later, the worked case being a 30-day SaaS trial taken with a credit card, never set up because the data import looked daunting, and then canceled (Chapter 14, pp. 231–232). Differentiation runs on "overwhelming proof" in quantity and in quality, because claims of being faster, better, and lower risk are made by everyone and cannot be verified before purchase (Chapter 14, pp. 236–238), and network effects are named the strongest form of referral while being "not always easy or possible to build" (Chapter 14, p. 244). The target is "an inch wide and a mile deep", "women over 40 years old" being 1.5 billion people, and the author "wouldn't say you can't be too niche" while adding that a reader who thinks the niching is done "probably haven't" (Chapter 2, pp. 36, 46–47). A claim of having no competitors is "a huge red flag indicating low demand" (Chapter 3, p. 54), the product is "just a delivery mechanism" for the seven core commodities (Chapter 3, p. 62), and a competitor is said not to be able to simply copy a markup (Chapter 3, p. 71).

**The 1-Page Marketing Plan** (Dib, 2018 edition). The unique selling proposition answers why a prospect buys from this business rather than from its nearest competitor, and the test is whether removing the name and logo would leave the business recognizable (Chapter 2, p. 72). Quality and service are rejected as USPs on two grounds, that they are expectations and that a buyer learns them only after purchase; if a prospect can make an apples-to-apples comparison it comes down to price, so the USP's job is to force an apples-to-oranges one (Chapter 2, p. 72). Uniqueness is not required and is called dangerous, being difficult and expensive with very few products truly unique — what is required is being different — and the uniqueness need not be in the product at all (Chapter 2, pp. 71, 73, 77). Innovation likewise need not be in the product: it can be in pricing, financing, packaging, support, delivery, management, or marketing (Chapter 7, p. 211). A niche is "an inch wide and a mile deep", a business expands by dominating one niche and then another rather than several at once, and niching is claimed to make price irrelevant, because a specialist is sought out while a generalist becomes a commodity bought on price (Chapter 1, pp. 51, 52, 54–55). A subscription is said to switch off a buyer's price-shopping radar, because no purchasing decision is made each time (Chapter 8, pp. 246–248), and a customer won on price is lost on price (Chapter 8, pp. 240–241).

**The Mom Test** (Fitzpatrick, 2013). On how narrow a first market is, the claim is that startups drown rather than starve, having too many options rather than too few, and that the large general businesses started specific — Google helping PhD students find obscure bits of code, eBay helping collectors buy and sell Pez dispensers, Evernote helping mothers save recipes (ch. 7, p. 90). A segment can be broad in a sneaky way, so that twenty conversations are one conversation each with twenty kinds of customer, and inconsistent problems and goals mean the segment is not yet specific enough (ch. 7, pp. 92–94). A good customer segment is a who-where pair, a group that cannot be found is sliced further, and the segment can be broadened again later (ch. 7, pp. 95, 97). On what holds a customer the book speaks only through commitment: commitment is giving up something valued in the currencies of time, reputation, and money, the more a person gives up the more seriously their kind words can be taken, and a trial that costs the person ten seconds carries nothing until its price is raised (ch. 5, pp. 64–66, 70). The first customers are Blank's earlyvangelists, who in enterprise software have the problem, know they have it, have the budget, and have already cobbled together their own makeshift solution (ch. 5, pp. 72–73).

**Competing Against Luck** (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan, 2016). New products succeed because of the experiences they enable and not the features they carry, and the set of experiences is the product's résumé (ch. 6, PDF p. 116); American Girl's dolls are described as nice rather than amazing, and Toys R Us, Walmart, and Disney all tried similar dolls at a fraction of the price without denting it (ch. 6, PDF pp. 116–117). Competitive advantage is conferred by processes rather than by resources or products, because resources are fungible and products are copyable, which is why Toyota opened its plants to rival executives twice a month, Pixar published its creative process, and SNHU gives tours to other institutions, and none of the three was matched (ch. 7, PDF pp. 140–142). Uncovering the job, creating the desired experiences, and integrating around the job are three layers that together give a company something competitors cannot easily copy, and IKEA is offered as a business with no secrets that nobody copies (ch. 6, PDF pp. 121–122). Amazon's reviews are called the hardest thing for a would-be competitor to copy, because they let a buyer find people who hired the product for the same job (ch. 6, PDF pp. 129–131). A purpose brand becomes synonymous with a job and is built by doing the job rather than by advertising, while a brand that fails to integrate around a job becomes a category placeholder competing on price (ch. 6, PDF pp. 132–134). Hiring anything new means firing something, the opposing forces being the habits of the present and the anxiety of the new, with loss aversion weighing about twice as heavily as gain (ch. 5, PDF pp. 93–95). Surface growth — doing many jobs for many customers — confuses buyers into hiring the wrong thing and leaves the company exposed to a rival focused on one job (ch. 8, PDF pp. 166–167).

**Forget the Funnel** (Laudi and Suellentrop, 2023). A funnel accounts only for the search and the purchase, while a recurring-revenue business has to keep proving its value, so treating marketing as something that stops at signup is named a serious mistake (ch. 1, PDF pp. 21–22). High retention potential, meaning a recurring rather than a one-off need, is one of seven criteria for choosing which Job to serve, beside urgency, willingness to pay, support burden, expansion potential, whether those customers congregate somewhere targetable, and a wildcard (ch. 5, PDF pp. 67–68); trying to solve every Job at once is named one of the biggest mistakes the authors see, because optimizing for all of them returns the team to chaos (ch. 5, PDF p. 66). At the Evaluation milestone the team maps what customers said was valuable to the features that created that value, side by side (ch. 7, PDF pp. 98–101), and Value Realization, the point where everything clicks, is called the best indicator that a customer will pay (ch. 7, PDF pp. 101–102). A success gap is the disconnect between what ideal customers need and what the current customer experience provides; SparkToro's was new users not finding the features the research had already named, and an in-app checklist and an onboarding email series doubled free-to-paid conversions in two months (ch. 8, PDF pp. 110–111). The failure mode at the first screen inside the product is throwing every feature at a new customer at once instead of the one that brought them in (ch. 6, PDF p. 74; ch. 8, PDF p. 112).

**Marketing Made Simple** (Miller and Peterson, 2020). On differentiation the position is that clarity is itself the differentiator, which is why differentiation is kept out of the website header (ch. 5, p. 63), while a reader in a crowded industry is told to name, inside the one-liner's problem statement, a problem the competition's own service creates (ch. 4, pp. 46–47). The book takes no position on what keeps a customer from leaving or on how narrow a first market is; its nearest claims are that commitment is the point at which a customer is asked to make a risky decision, a purchase being the giving up of survival resources for a hoped-for gain (ch. 2, pp. 24, 26), and that most products take about eight touchpoints before an order, with email named the best way to stay in the relationship (ch. 2, pp. 27–28).

**This Is Marketing** (Godin, 2018). Quality in the sense of meeting specifications is called required and no longer sufficient, and the commodity suckout is that anything findable on Upwork, Amazon, or Alibaba cannot carry a price raise, because the price of everything is a click away (Ch. 6, PDF p. 60); marketing is separately said not to be a race to add features for less money (Ch. 3, PDF p. 26). Better is not a property of the thing — Beer Advocate lists 250 beers that have each earned more than 3,400 ratings, every one of them somebody's favorite — so a marketer who says this is better means better for someone (Ch. 5, PDF p. 42), and the job is described as finding a spot on the map with edges, a generous beacon rather than a selfish unique selling proposition (Ch. 5, PDF p. 45). Trout and Ries's cornering of a competitor is said not to hold up in a hyper-competitive world, and choosing the popular axes puts a business in a crowded quadrant, so the substitute offered is a quadrant built from two overlooked axes (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 54-55). The smallest viable market is the minimum number of people a marketer would need to influence to make the effort worth it, and Danny Meyer's Union Square Cafe could seat six hundred people a day, so the founder's work was choosing which six hundred (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 34-35). The assets to be built are permission, the privilege of delivering anticipated, personal, and relevant messages, whose working test is that people notice and ask where the sender went once the sending stops, and trust, called as scarce as attention and located in what was done about a refund, about lost data, and about a plant closing (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 152-154; Ch. 18, PDF pp. 159-160). A social platform is called a sharecropper's arrangement, because the platform holds the permission and the asset rather than the marketer (Ch. 17, PDF p. 153).

**Start Small, Stay Small** (Walling, 2010). Six reasons are given to go niche, among them that niches have less competition and higher margins and that prospects trust a business faster because they hear of it more than once; the choice offered is 100% of a 5,000-person niche over 10% of a 50,000-person one (Ch. 2, pp. 45–50). Vertical markets beat horizontal ones on four counts: similar behavior, members who talk to one another, members who gather in the same places, and similar needs (Ch. 2, pp. 62–64). The Product Success Triangle makes the product one third of the equation beside market and execution, so a mediocre product with brilliant marketing and execution will make money while a brilliant product without either is dead (Ch. 3, p. 91), though the product still "has to be good" and selling something poor to a hungry market is "not a sustainable strategy" (Ch. 1, p. 5; Ch. 2, p. 40). A third-party plug-in on a platform such as Facebook or Salesforce carries the warning that the platform vendor may absorb the feature (Ch. 3, pp. 108–110). Competition is said to shorten the period a product can be left on autopilot, which the author ties back to the case for low-competition niches, and no product sells at the same level forever (Ch. 7, p. 192); a business's valuation is said to track profit and automation rather than features, traffic, or design (Ch. 7, p. 196).

**Software as a Science** (Martell, Verlaque, Page, and Petitpas, 2024). Four numbers run every SaaS company — current customers, new customers per month, monthly churn rate, and monthly ARPA — and only three of them can be changed, so the levers are acquisition, retention, and expansion (ch. 1, PDF pp. 30–31; ch. 2, pp. 45–46). Selling more of a product customers churn out of is likened to over-prescribing, and at 1,000 customers, 100 new a month and 10 percent churn, halving churn leaves revenue about 40 percent higher than raising sales by half (ch. 6, PDF pp. 127–130). Retention begins at activation: First Value is the one moment that makes a user understand the product, the Rule of Three caps the steps between signup and it at three, and Hiten Shah is quoted that without a single killer core use case product-market fit is a dream diluted by features (ch. 6, PDF pp. 126, 137, 139). Bundling features most customers do not need into the highest plan is named as harming retention and flattening expansion, against the intuition that more inclusion is generous (ch. 9, PDF pp. 205–206). An anointed advocate is called the least likely customer to churn, because the status was announced publicly, and if one customer in two refers another then a $100-a-month customer is really worth $150 (ch. 8, PDF pp. 174, 177). Baremetrics removed self-service cancellation and required a call, kept 15 percent of the leavers, and brought churn from 13 percent back to 5 (ch. 7, PDF pp. 163–164).

**The Lean Startup** (Ries, 2011). Sustainable growth is defined by one rule, that new customers come from the actions of past customers, which excludes one-time advertisements and publicity stunts (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 200). The sticky engine turns on retention: the product grows when new customer acquisition exceeds churn, the rate of compounding is the difference between them, and the dot-com-era PointCast is the case whose acquisition was strong and whose churn matched it (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 203-204). On the paid engine the claim is that competition bids up the cost of any acquisition source until firms pay away most of their marginal profit, so lasting growth there requires a differentiated ability to monetize a particular set of customers (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 209-211), and every engine eventually exhausts its set of customers, which can take years and then presents as a sudden crisis (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 214-215). On the feature list the book says most people overestimate how many features are needed and that how small a minimum viable product should be cannot be decided formulaically (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 95, 97). It takes no position on how narrow a first market is; its nearest claim is that a business that is an exact clone of an existing one, down to model, pricing, target customer and product, falls outside the book's own definition of a startup, because its success depends only on execution (Ch. 2 Define, PDF p. 39).

**The Four Steps to the Epiphany** (Blank, 2013 second edition). Market Type is the organizing claim: a startup is entering an existing market, creating a new market, or resegmenting an existing one as a low-cost entrant or as a niche entrant, and Market Type changes customer adoption rate, positioning, market size, launch, cash needs, and the shape of the sales growth curve (Chapter 2, pp. 87, 90). An existing market means higher performance against known competitors who define the basis of competition; in a new market product features are at first irrelevant; niche resegmentation asks whether part of a market will buy a product built for their specific needs even at a higher price, with In-n-Out Burger set against a McDonald's menu of more than fifty-five items by 2001 (Chapter 2, pp. 90–95). Over half of startups attempt resegmentation, which the author calls the trickiest choice (Chapter 2, p. 95), and Market Type "is not immutable", since almost any product fitting an existing market could be positioned as a niche in a resegmented one (Chapter 3, p. 166; Chapter 5, p. 351). First-mover advantage is treated as a fallacy, with Golder and Tellis's 1993 study of 500 brands in 50 categories reporting that almost half of market pioneers failed while early market leaders, entering an average of thirteen years later, failed at 8 percent (Chapter 5, pp. 377–379). The rule on features is that there are no new ones until the market search is exhausted, with the stated exception that if enough customers ask for the same custom features "they are not custom at all" (Chapter 3, p. 203; Chapter 4, p. 310), and the customer brief ends with the minimum feature set, the smallest or least complicated problem the customer will pay to have solved (Chapter 3, pp. 157–158).

**The Startup Owner's Manual** (Blank and Dorf, 2012). Customer relationships are laid out as a Get/Keep/Grow funnel in which retention is held to be far cheaper than acquisition (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 302-349), and getting a new customer is called an expensive process, which is the book's stated reason for planning retention before acquisition succeeds (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 341). Market type is a late-binding decision that can be deferred to customer creation while the cost of choosing wrong is severe (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 287); in a new market it is tempting to claim there is no competition and wrong to do so (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 299), and company positioning in a new market cannot rest on the product category (Web/Mobile, Ch. 10, PDF pp. 592-596). Velocity is not always the goal, and the two named exceptions are niche vertical sites and high-value products such as enterprise software, where a few high-value customers may be enough (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420); a completely open site is said to invite competitors, so an invite-only site is offered as a way to control access while the solution is tested (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420). The first paying customers are earlyvangelists, who have the problem, know they have it, are looking for a solution, have cobbled together a makeshift one, and can find or commit money for a better one (Strategy Guide, Ch. 4, PDF pp. 99-102).

**Zero to One** (Thiel with Masters, 2014). The claim is that a business worth building ends up a monopoly, meaning a company so good at what it does that no rival offers a close substitute, that "capitalism and competition are opposites", and that monopoly is "the condition of every successful business" (ch. 3, PDF pp. 21, 28). Four characteristics are given, with the caveat that they are not boxes to check and that there is no shortcut to monopoly: proprietary technology at least ten times better than its closest substitute in some important dimension, because anything less reads as marginal and is hard to sell; network effects, which are unreachable unless the product is valuable to its first users while the network is small; economies of scale, which favor software and disfavor a service business; and branding, the weakest of the four alone, since the surface techniques are imitable and Yahoo! is the counter-case (ch. 5, PDF pp. 38–41). The instruction is to start with a very small market and always to err on the side of too small, the perfect target market being a small group of particular people concentrated together and served by few or no competitors, which is why talk of 1% of a $100 billion market is called a red flag (ch. 5, PDF pp. 41–42); small is not nonexistent, the author's own PalmPilot failure being users who were scattered, had little in common, and used their devices episodically (ch. 5, PDF p. 42). Scaling up is sequenced, Amazon beginning with books and eBay with Beanie Babies (ch. 5, PDF pp. 42–43). Moving first is called a tactic rather than a goal, and what matters is making the last great development in a market and holding it (ch. 5, PDF pp. 44–45); a monopoly is only valuable if it endures, and the measure of endurance is future cash flow (ch. 5, PDF p. 35). Understating the scale of competition is called the biggest mistake a startup can make (ch. 3, PDF pp. 23, 25).

**Company of One** (Jarvis, 2019). Skills and expertise are called replicable and a personality not, on a survey of more than 10,000 of the author's own customers in which his personality was the first reason they bought from him rather than from someone else (ch. 6, PDF p. 77); a large company is the vanilla ice cream of its market and a company of one has to be pistachio, and polarization is said to shorten a sales cycle by forcing a binary choice (ch. 6, PDF pp. 79–84). Retention beats acquisition, renewals are the metric worth watching, and the Econsultancy figure of a new customer costing five times an existing one is the reason (ch. 4, PDF pp. 55–56; ch. 7, PDF p. 86); a customer paying $10 a month for ten years is said to outvalue one paying $100 who leaves in a few months (ch. 7, PDF pp. 92–93). The structural advantage claimed for a company of one is that its service does not scale and does not have to, with CDBaby answering every call within two rings and Basecamp answering support within fifteen minutes (ch. 7, PDF pp. 86–87). Word of mouth is named hard to measure and hard to scale quickly, and no incentive creates advocates without a good product and good service first (ch. 10, PDF pp. 118–119). Growth hacking is the foil: its true north is growth and its relationships exist to offset churn, and Des Traynor is quoted that credibility can be swapped for attention at any time (ch. 12, PDF pp. 141–142). Simplicity sells, the example being Casper making three mattresses rather than 108 (ch. 1, PDF pp. 25–27).

**Buy Back Your Time** (Martell, 2023). No position on what keeps a customer, on whether features count, or on how narrow a first market is. Its nearest claim, the Buyback Principle — that a founder hires to buy back time rather than to grow the business, and then spends the reclaimed hours only on the two or three tasks he excels at, enjoys, and is paid most for (ch. 1, PDF pp. 20, 22) — is about where a founder's own hours go, and is carried by the founder-time theme.

**Entrepreneur Revolution** (Priestley, 2024 third edition). The core offer is what a business is famous for, must be a remarkable solution, must never be sold at break-even or a loss, and must be de-commoditized so that it cannot be price-compared (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 118–120); products for clients are sold to people who already bought the core offer, should aim to double the profitability of the business, and must be genuinely different rather than more of the same (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 120–121). Four product strategies are named as failures for a small business: OOPS, only one product or service, too dependent on brand and workable at Zippo or Tabasco scale; the J curve, too dependent on capital; the one-stop shop, too dependent on systems, which is what Amazon and Walmart have; and the brokerage model, too dependent on time, which the author ran himself at seven-figure revenue and found unsaleable because he owned no asset (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 108–114). A product is defined as a consistent way of achieving an outcome the customer wants, so Rolex sells status rather than timekeeping against a $20 Casio, and a service business is told to make its work repeatable and to give it a name (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 107–108). Market size is called mostly irrelevant and the audience is built before the product, since Apple had no PC market in 1977, YouTube no user-generated video market, and Airbnb no market for renting a stranger's bedroom, while some markets are named as huge and still very unlikely to be entered, a new luxury watch brand and a burger restaurant chain being the examples (Chapter 4, PDF p. 94). One person working through a small distributed team is said to be able to earn seven-figure profits from a micro-niche (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 28–31).

**The Voltage Effect** (List, 2022). A membership scales only where it gives access to goods not readily available without it, and riders dual-app, so switching is nearly effortless; of six membership prices offered to roughly 1.2 million Lyft riders in March 2019, not one would scale (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 48–49, 52–53). Non-negotiables have infinite value because the enterprise will not function at scale without them and negotiables have finite value, and compliance — defined simply as use — is named a non-negotiable for nearly every enterprise (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 75–77). A person with a unique skill cannot be cloned, so a business resting on one has a limit on how big it can get, Ferran Adrià's El Bulli drawing a reported one million people a year for 8,000 seats; Jamie Oliver escaped that trap because his secret ingredient was his ingredients rather than himself, and the chain fell apart when the less visible ingredients changed (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 69–72). Program drift is the subtler failure, where the non-negotiables go unfulfilled at scale because implementors will not or cannot replicate the program faithfully, and Paul Midler's "quality fade" is the business term for it (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 79, 81). A ceiling is not called a failure, since an idea can still serve a purpose and make money with one, and Pinterest's female-majority user base and dating apps' irrelevance to people in committed monogamous relationships are the examples (Chapter 2, PDF p. 55). Where scale economies are built in, the book says to price low to get customers in the door, which it gives as the reason many software products run a freemium strategy (Chapter 5, PDF p. 116).

**Thinking in Bets** (Duke, 2018). No position on what keeps a customer, on whether features count, or on how narrow a first market is. Its nearest claim, that every decision is a bet — a choice among alternatives made under uncertainty with something at risk, usually against the other future versions of oneself that were not chosen (ch. 2, PDF pp. 43–45) — is about how a decision is made and reviewed rather than about what holds a customer.

## Where the books agree

- Features are not a moat: *The SaaS Playbook* says so directly; Ulwick calls feature comparison a waste; Naval's authenticity argument rejects competing on what others do. The new books crowd onto the same side. Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan say products succeed on the experiences they enable rather than the features they carry, and that products are copyable while processes are not (ch. 6, PDF p. 116; ch. 7, PDF p. 140). Godin calls quality that meets specification required and no longer sufficient (Ch. 6, PDF p. 60). *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* rejects quality and service as unique selling propositions because a buyer learns them only after purchase (Chapter 2, p. 72), and *Lean Marketing* calls the product "just a delivery mechanism" (Chapter 3, p. 62). Martell, Verlaque, Page, and Petitpas quote Hiten Shah that without a single core use case product-market fit is a dream diluted by features, and say bundling unwanted features into the top plan harms retention (ch. 6, PDF p. 126; ch. 9, PDF pp. 205–206). *Start Small, Stay Small* makes the product one third of the equation and says valuation tracks profit and automation rather than features (Ch. 3, p. 91; Ch. 7, p. 196). Ries says most people overestimate how many features are needed (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 97), and *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* says features are at first irrelevant in a new market (Chapter 2, pp. 90–95).
- Narrow first: Levels's micro niche and Ulwick's core functional job define a market by one group and one job. Thiel puts it strongest, that a startup should always err on the side of too small (ch. 5, PDF p. 41); Godin's smallest viable market (Ch. 4, PDF p. 34), Fitzpatrick's who-where pair (ch. 7, p. 97), and Dunford's Big Fish, Small Pond (Step 8, pp. 104–106) are the same instruction in three vocabularies. Both of Dib's books give the same phrase, "an inch wide and a mile deep" (*The 1-Page Marketing Plan*, Chapter 1, p. 52; *Lean Marketing*, Chapter 2, p. 36). *Start Small, Stay Small* prefers 100% of a 5,000-person niche to 10% of a 50,000-person one (Ch. 2, p. 46); Laudi and Suellentrop pick one Job and shelve the rest (ch. 5, PDF p. 66); both of Blank's books make niche resegmentation a named Market Type (*The Four Steps to the Epiphany*, Chapter 2, pp. 90–95; *The Startup Owner's Manual*, Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 80-82); Priestley's micro-niche (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 28–31) and Jarvis's proud exclusion (ch. 6, PDF p. 81) say it for a one-person business; and Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan give the negative case in surface growth, where doing many jobs for many customers leaves a company exposed to a rival focused on one (ch. 8, PDF pp. 166–167).
- A dependency is a risk: Levels names the API; *The SaaS Playbook*'s integrations moat is the same dependency viewed from the other side. *Start Small, Stay Small* names the platform vendor that may absorb a plug-in's feature (Ch. 3, pp. 108–110), Godin calls a social platform a sharecropper's arrangement because the platform holds the permission (Ch. 17, PDF p. 153), *Lean Marketing* says that on social media "you're a tenant" who can be evicted at any time (Chapter 13, p. 212), and *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* prefers a list you own to an audience on a platform that can change its rules (Chapter 3, pp. 119, 122).
- Retention is cheaper than acquisition, and it starts at the first use rather than at renewal. Blank and Dorf hold retention far cheaper than acquisition and plan it before acquisition succeeds (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 302-349, 341); Jarvis puts a new customer at five times an existing one (ch. 7, PDF p. 86); Martell, Verlaque, Page, and Petitpas compute that halving churn beats raising sales by half and cap the steps to First Value at three (ch. 6, PDF pp. 128–130, 139); *Lean Marketing* puts the loss at the trial that was never set up (Chapter 14, pp. 231–232); Laudi and Suellentrop name the same thing a success gap (ch. 8, PDF p. 111); Ries's sticky engine is the arithmetic of it (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 203-204); and Dunford, who excludes retention attributes from her own list, still records that poorly positioned products are bought by people who misunderstand them and leave (Step 5, pp. 82–83).
- The thing a competitor cannot copy is not the product. Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan put it in processes and in an integrated experience, with IKEA as the business whose methods are open and uncopied (ch. 6, PDF pp. 121–122; ch. 7, PDF p. 140); Jarvis puts it in the founder's personality (ch. 6, PDF p. 77); Naval puts it in authenticity; Godin puts it in trust, which is located in what was done rather than in what was said (Ch. 18, PDF p. 160); *Lean Marketing* puts it in overwhelming proof (Chapter 14, pp. 236–238); *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* says the uniqueness need not be in the product and that innovation can sit in pricing, support, or delivery (Chapter 2, p. 71; Chapter 7, p. 211); and Dunford says a unique attribute can be a delivery model or a business model as easily as a feature (Components, pp. 46–47).

## Where the books conflict

- **What the moat is.** *The SaaS Playbook* lists four and none is "a pricing model" or "execution"; Ulwick's moat is the entire job on one platform; Naval's is the founder's own authenticity; Levels's is being first in a niche too small for anyone else. The new books widen the disagreement rather than settle it. Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan put it in processes (ch. 7, PDF p. 140); Thiel gives four characteristics, of which proprietary technology ten times better is the first (ch. 5, PDF pp. 38–41); Jarvis puts it in the founder's personality (ch. 6, PDF p. 77); Godin puts it in permission and trust (Ch. 17, PDF p. 152; Ch. 18, PDF p. 159); Priestley puts it in a core offer de-commoditized so that it cannot be price-compared (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 118–120); and List runs directly against Jarvis and Naval, since his whole chapter argues that a business resting on an uncopyable person has a ceiling, and that Jamie Oliver scaled precisely because the secret was the ingredients rather than the chef (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 69–74).
- **The entire job.** Ulwick's long-term strategy is what the read-back of #995 records ten of eleven competitors already claiming, which Ulwick's own 20%-better threshold would call table stakes. Three of the new books stand against him. Laudi and Suellentrop call solving every Job at once one of the biggest mistakes they see (ch. 5, PDF p. 66); *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* ends the customer brief at the minimum feature set, the smallest problem a customer will pay to have solved (Chapter 3, pp. 157–158); and Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan name surface growth as the failure of doing many jobs at once (ch. 8, PDF pp. 166–167). Priestley stands with him, since OOPS — only one product or service — is the first of his four named small-business failures (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 108–114).
- **How much better a product has to be.** Ulwick sets the switching threshold at upwards of 20% better and calls under 5% a sustaining strategy that entices nobody (ch. 3, p. 78; ch. 2, pp. 48–49). Thiel sets it at ten times better in some important dimension, on the ground that anything less reads as marginal and is hard to sell (ch. 5, PDF p. 38).
- **First or last.** Levels's moat is being first in a niche too small for anyone else. *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* treats first-mover advantage as a fallacy, citing Golder and Tellis's 1993 study of 500 brands in 50 categories in which almost half of market pioneers failed while early market leaders entering thirteen years later failed at 8 percent (Chapter 5, pp. 377–379), and Thiel says moving first is a tactic rather than a goal and that what matters is being the last mover (ch. 5, PDF pp. 44–45).
- **Whether a subscription is itself a moat.** *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* says a subscription switches off a buyer's price-shopping radar, because no purchasing decision is made each time (Chapter 8, pp. 246–248), and Godin calls a subscription an overt act of permission that makes a subscriber worth more than a newsstand buyer (Ch. 17, PDF p. 154). List measured the opposite, that a membership scales only where it gates goods not readily available without it and that riders who dual-app switch at almost no cost (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 48–49). Martell, Verlaque, Page, and Petitpas assume a subscription throughout, since their whole calculator is built on monthly churn and monthly ARPA (ch. 1, PDF pp. 30–31). #45 records that Doula Cloud runs no recurring subscription at all.
- **Whether competition is to be escaped or welcomed.** Thiel says to escape it, that "capitalism and competition are opposites" and that monopoly is "the condition of every successful business" (ch. 3, PDF pp. 21, 28). *Lean Marketing* says a claim of having no competitors is "a huge red flag indicating low demand" (Chapter 3, p. 54), and Blank and Dorf say that in a new market it is tempting to claim there is no competition and wrong to do so (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 299). Godin refuses the framing on both sides, calling Trout and Ries's cornering of a competitor something that does not hold up in a hyper-competitive world and plotting alternatives on a map rather than competitors (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 50-55).
- **Evidence.** *The SaaS Playbook*'s four moats are asserted; Ulwick's 20% figure is his firm's rule of thumb; Naval's claims are aphorisms; Levels's examples are his own products. The new books do not raise the standard evenly. List's rests on a randomized test of six membership prices offered to about 1.2 million riders (Chapter 2, PDF p. 52); *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* rests its first-mover claim on a published 1993 study of 500 brands (Chapter 5, pp. 377–379); Thiel's cases are the companies he founded and funded; Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan's are Christensen's own consultancy's engagements, disclosed in the endnotes; Jarvis's personality finding is a survey of his own customers (ch. 6, PDF p. 77); and Martell, Verlaque, Page, and Petitpas's figures come from their own coaching practice.
