# Theme synthesis: visual craft and type

## What this file is for

This is one of the five theme syntheses for the reference track that [#1684](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1684) adds, on the path that [the forms synthesis](forms.md), [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md) and [the design system synthesis](the-design-system.md) took first. ADR-0041 gives the reference track a read-back and then an adopt-or-not decision in the shape of ADR-0021; #1684 puts a synthesis between the two, so that the founder's grilling session and the adopt-or-not ADR for this theme start from one page. The file lays out what each of the twelve reference-track books claims about visual hierarchy, spacing, color, and the type scale; where the books agree; where they conflict; and what the repo has already decided. It contains no ruling. Every book on the track is named below, with "no position" where the book has none, so that a reader can tell an omission from an absence. #1684's table names three books with a position on this theme; the books show that eight more have one, *Inclusive Design Patterns*, *Design Systems*, *Expressive Design Systems* and *Laying the Foundations* at length and *Form Design Patterns*, *Inclusive Components*, *Atomic Design* and *Giving a Damn About Accessibility* narrowly, and each is given a full entry. One book, *Going Offline*, has none.

Three neighbors share ground with this theme, and this file does not repeat them. That a system holds its values as named tokens, chosen once and changed in one place, is [the design system synthesis](the-design-system.md)'s, which hands the values themselves to this file: which steps a type scale has, how far apart the spacing steps sit, how many shades a color gets. The contrast floors, color as never the only signal, and text that resizes with the person's own setting are [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md)'s; this file names them only where a book's position on hierarchy or color rests on them. The space between a label and its own field is [the forms synthesis](forms.md)'s.

Page citations follow each read-back's own page rule, stated in its source line. *Inclusive Components* cites the PDF index, because the book prints no numbers. Every other book cites its printed page: equal to the PDF index for *Refactoring UI*, *Designing User Interfaces*, *Laying the Foundations* and *Giving a Damn About Accessibility*; the PDF index less one for *Inclusive Design Patterns* and *Design Systems*; less two for *Form Design Patterns*; less five for *Flexible Typesetting*, *Expressive Design Systems* and *Going Offline*; and, for *Atomic Design*, an offset that changes at two missing pages, as its read-back sets out. The read-backs are in `docs/research/books/`. Where a citation below differs from the one a read-back gives, it was taken from the book itself. Heydon Pickering wrote two books on the track, and each is named by its title wherever the two differ; Wathan and Schoger means *Refactoring UI*, Brown *Flexible Typesetting*, the Malewiczes *Designing User Interfaces*, Kholmatova *Design Systems*, Perez-Cruz *Expressive Design Systems*, Couldwell *Laying the Foundations*, Frost *Atomic Design*, Silver *Form Design Patterns*, and Byrne-Haber *Giving a Damn About Accessibility*. WCAG, Material Design and GOV.UK are not books on the track; where they appear below, each is either a source a book cites or a record the repo adopted. The repo records were read on 2026-10-03.

## The question

How does a screen show what matters most; how far apart do things sit, and on what scale; how many colors does a palette hold, in how many shades, and what is each one for; and what sizes, weights, line heights and line lengths make up the type scale?

## What each book claims

**Refactoring UI** (Wathan and Schoger, 2018). The whole book is on this theme, written for a developer working without a designer, and it says of its own color method "It's not a science" (Working with Color, p. 132).

*Hierarchy.* Visual hierarchy is "the most effective tool" for making an interface feel designed (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 30), and the move is to "deliberately de-emphasize" secondary and tertiary content (p. 31). Size alone is not the lever: weight and color do the job, two or three text colors and two weights, 400 or 500 for most text and 600 or 700 for emphasis, are "usually enough", and weights under 400 are for large headings only (p. 34). Where the active item will not stand out, the inactive ones are softened instead (pp. 39–40). Visual hierarchy is kept apart from document hierarchy: a "Manage Account" `h1` need not be large, and the rule is to pick an element for its semantics and style it for the hierarchy (pp. 46–47). Every page has "one true primary action", a few secondary and a few tertiary ones, and a destructive action is not automatically big and red (pp. 52–54). Early work is done in grayscale, so that spacing, contrast and size carry the hierarchy before color is added (Starting from Scratch, pp. 10–11).

*Spacing.* White space "should be removed, not added": start with too much and take it away (Layout and Spacing, pp. 56–58), while "Dense UIs have their place" when density is "a deliberate decision" (p. 59). A rule that every value be a multiple of 4px does not choose between two large values, so no two values in a scale sit closer than about 25% (pp. 61–62); the example scale starts from 16px, which "divides nicely" and is the browser default (p. 62), and runs 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 96, 128, 192, 256, 384, 512, 640 and 768px (p. 63, a figure). Relative sizing does not hold across screens: elements that are large on a large screen "need to shrink faster" than small ones, and a button's padding grows more than in proportion to its text (pp. 79–82). There is more space around a group than within it (p. 86). A design that needs 600px takes 600px (p. 65), and a sidebar gets a fixed width "optimized for its contents" while the main area flexes (pp. 72–74).

*Type.* Most interfaces use "way too many font sizes" (Designing Text, p. 88). A modular scale gives fractional pixels and jumps "too limiting" for interface work (pp. 89–90), so the scale is hand-picked, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36, 48, 60 and 72px (p. 91, a figure), and written in px or rem, never em, because nested ems fall off the scale (pp. 92–93). The safe face is a neutral sans-serif or the system stack (p. 94). Line length is 45 to 75 characters, about 20 to 35em, with wider lines "risky territory" (pp. 99–100). Line height is proportional: 1.5 for narrow text, up to 2 for wide, and taller for small text than for large (pp. 105–108). Mixed sizes on one line align by baseline (pp. 102–104). In an interface where almost everything is a link, an ancillary link can show its underline or color "only on hover" (pp. 109–110). Letter-spacing is left to the type designer, except that headlines set in a body face are tightened and all-caps text is loosened (pp. 115–117). Numbers in a table are right-aligned (p. 113).

*Color.* The book works in HSL, because browsers "only understand HSL" (Working with Color, pp. 119–122). A real palette has grays, 8 to 10 shades starting from a dark gray, since true black looks "unnatural" (p. 125); one or two primary colors; and accent colors for semantic states, to be used "pretty sparingly", so a complex interface may need ten colors at 5 to 10 shades each (pp. 126–128). Shades are defined up front, because a preprocessor's lighten and darken produce "35 slightly different blues" (p. 129): nine shades named 900 to 100 around a base at 500 (p. 131). Saturation rises as lightness leaves 50% (p. 133), hue is rotated to change perceived brightness (pp. 134–138), and grays are saturated toward blue to feel cool or toward yellow to feel warm, by an amount "completely up to you" (pp. 139–141). The contrast chapter and the color-blindness chapter (pp. 142–148) are [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md)'s.

*Depth and shape.* Light "comes from above", so a raised element has a lighter top edge and a small dark shadow below (Creating Depth, pp. 151–155); small shadows sit near the surface and large ones near the reader, and five shadows are "usually plenty" (pp. 158–161); a polished shadow has two parts (pp. 163–166); and in a flat design a lighter element feels closer and a darker one farther away (p. 168). Fewer borders: a shadow, two background colors or extra space separate as well (Finishing Touches, pp. 206–209). A small radius is neutral, a large one playful, none at all "serious or formal", and mixing square and round corners is avoided (Starting from Scratch, pp. 21–22).

The read-back records that every rule rests on the authors' own before-and-after figures, that the 25% gap, the 45 to 75 characters and the shade counts carry no source, and that the book never names Tailwind CSS, so a reader cannot tell whether its worked scales are advice or a product's defaults (read-back section 8). It also records that HSL as the only color model browsers understand was true in 2018 and is not now (read-back section 7).

**Flexible Typesetting** (Brown, 2018). The whole book is on the type half of this theme, setting body and small text rather than arranging display type or calibrating tables, which "deserve their own" books (Ch. 1, p. 8). There are no "correct" fonts, font sizes, measures or line heights, only relationships among them that hold or come under pressure (Ch. 1, p. 13).

*Size.* Each reader's default font size is "a rock" (Ch. 2, p. 40), so the root is set at 100% (p. 43); type sized in pixels "does not respect" that preference, and neither does type sized purely in viewport units (pp. 44–45), while a percentage, em or rem combined with a viewport unit in `calc()` is accessible (p. 46). A body typeface is chosen first, as the anchor (Ch. 3, p. 64). Then the text block is shaped in a fixed order, font size, then measure, then line spacing (Ch. 4, p. 91). The body size is one that feels familiar, found by matching the face's x-height to a range the author measured from common screen fonts, on what he calls "anecdotal" data, which gives 1.15rem for the worked example (pp. 93–98).

*Scale.* A modular scale is generated from that body size and a ratio, 3:5 in the example, on the idea that numbers related by a ratio "feel harmonious" (p. 99; p. 101); a reader can also "eyeball" the numbers (p. 102), and there is no single "correct" way to use a scale (Ch. 5, p. 150). Breaking type and layout into named values, the scale's numbers, the measure limits, the font stacks and the breakpoints, holds "our system of measurement" (Ch. 5, p. 166), and a grid's divisions share "proportional harmony" with the type scale (p. 170).

*Measure, line spacing and space.* Measure "is a visual decision", found by resizing a live text block (Ch. 4, p. 103). Line spacing ideally flexes between a tighter value at the narrowest measure and a looser one at the widest, which the book builds as a CSS lock (pp. 106–109), which the example does not use because it is "complicated to implement" (p. 110). The white space in and around a text block moves together as its tempo: it condenses as the block narrows and loosens as it widens (p. 113). Links are underlined (p. 136).

*Composition.* Changing the body font size as a layout widens moves readers away from their familiar size (Ch. 5, p. 146), though a deliberate increase is "a respectable choice" (p. 152). Hierarchy comes from compositional contrast (p. 159), and the rule is to decrease contrast as a composition grows more focused: strong contrast in a wide, busy layout, basic contrast in a narrow one, and in-between contrast between them (pp. 164–166). Baseline grids are "more trouble than they're worth" in CSS as it stood, and the author hopes a later mechanism will make one flex (pp. 171–173).

The read-back records that the familiar-size method rests on unpublished measurements the author calls anecdotal, that every technique is shown on one small site, and that the book's own warning against growing body text and its later allowance of it are not reconciled (read-back section 8). It also records that container queries, hoped for in the book, have since shipped, and that the property it hoped would bring flexible baseline grids has not (read-back section 7).

**Designing User Interfaces** (Malewicz and Malewicz, 2020). Several chapters carry a full position, given as the authors' studio practice.

*Grid and spacing.* Every value in a layout is divisible by a base number (Layout and grid, p. 61); the 8-point grid is "currently the most popular" (p. 63), and an 8-point fluid grid starts from 16 because 8 alone is too small to separate objects (p. 64). A soft grid aligns objects by multiples of the base number rather than by columns (p. 70), checked by the authors' Red Square Method (pp. 71–73). Body copy on a 12-column grid spans no more than eight columns, about ten words a line (p. 75). Objects placed close together read as a group (Perception, p. 37).

*Type.* Line height is the font size times 1.618 (Typography, p. 144). Hierarchy can come from the same ratio, a 10-point body giving a 16-point subheading and a 26-point title, or from multiples of the grid base, 16, 24, 32, 40 and 48 (p. 148). On mobile, body text and form fields sit at 16 to 18 points, 18 being "the safest", with smaller sizes kept for descriptions, labels and metadata (p. 159). Mobile lines run 30 to 50 characters, and larger screens 6 to 9 words (p. 151). A product keeps to one or two fonts, two weights and between 3 and 5 sizes (p. 165).

*Color.* Pure black is avoided, for text as for fills, in favor of a dark gray or a gray "mixed with your primary color" (Colors, pp. 107, 122–123; Typography, p. 151). A palette is balanced 60/30/10 (Colors, p. 120), and system states are positive, negative, neutral and warning (p. 124). A call to action is "the only element with a unique color or style on the screen" (Buttons, p. 186), which is the isolation effect at work (Perception, p. 46). Ultra-high contrast is not overdone on a screen held close to the face (Typography, p. 151). A dark mode built on dark grays is "purely an aesthetic choice" (Design Styles, p. 347).

*Depth and shape.* Slightly rounded corners, 2 to 6 points, read as friendlier than sharp ones, the radius is consistent and comes from the grid base (Objects, p. 84). A shadow's opacity is "never higher than 40%" (p. 88) and is derived from the primary color rather than pure black (p. 90); a button with a subtle shadow is noticed faster (Buttons, p. 194).

The read-back records that several figures carry no source, the "over 90%" share of liking a product that comes from its palette among them (Colors, p. 95), and that the numeric choices, the 8-point grid and the 1.618 multiplier, are the authors' studio practice stated as settled (read-back section 8).

**Inclusive Design Patterns** (Pickering, 2016). A full position on type and spacing, made from the reader's side. Browsers default to 16px, and there is "a rationale for reasonably large defaults"; the root is set at 100% so a size the person chose survives, and setting it at 16px would "diminish" that (The Document, p. 29). A paragraph is an atom (A Paragraph, p. 47). A typeface is judged by four questions rather than by the claim that sans serif is more readable (pp. 48–49), and typesetting is the choice of "a measure, justification and leading" that suit it (p. 51): a measure of 45 to 75 characters, after Bringhurst, set with a `max-width` in `rem` (pp. 52–53); no justified text (p. 53); and leading of "space-and-a-half", after WCAG 1.4.8, as a unitless `line-height` of 1.5 (p. 55). Contrast is tempered, because stark contrast troubles readers with Irlen syndrome (p. 57), and the chapter's list of things to avoid ends with "stark black on white" (p. 68). Heading levels describe structure: a level is never chosen for its size (A Blog Post, p. 78), and styling one level as another is done with caution, because "an `<h2>` should look like an `<h2>`" and a page styled one way and structured another separates seeing and non-seeing readers (p. 79). Vertical space is a unit of the paragraph's line height, 1.5rem, applied between successive elements (p. 102); a heading's margin is set in `em` so it sits closer to the content it introduces, and anything that is not a paragraph takes twice the unit (pp. 103–104). The read-back records that the book's evidence is mostly the author's experience and informal polls (read-back section 8).

**Design Systems** (Kholmatova, 2017). A full position, on color, type and space as perceptual patterns. Perceptual patterns are tone of voice, typography, color, spacing and layout, shapes and animation (ch. 1, p. 20). Density is a choice of feel: tight spacing, compact controls and small headings in one product against high-contrast type, large headings and generous white space in another (ch. 1, p. 19). Relationships matter as much as values: at TED, red "should be used sparingly", or it "doesn't feel like TED anymore" (ch. 4, p. 87). A style is defined by its purpose before its value: "twenty shades of blue" are no problem while blue keeps one meaning, and a unified type scale did not stop headings being used inconsistently until there were guidelines for when to use each size (ch. 9, p. 214). An audit found 62 shades of gray in one product; a palette starts only with what its roles need, "three variations of blue" in the example (p. 227); FutureLearn avoided shades and tints "to keep the palette crisp" (p. 228); and a base value with increments serves color, type, space and animation alike (p. 229). Typography and spacing are linked: "Large, higher-contrast typography requires more white space", and FutureLearn's modules came in three densities, spacious with high typographic contrast, regular, and compact with headings only slightly larger than body text (ch. 10, pp. 269–271). Polaris's primary buttons "should only appear once per screen" (ch. 8, p. 209). The read-back records that the evidence is six companies, one of them the author's employer (read-back section 8).

**Expressive Design Systems** (Perez-Cruz, 2019). A full position, on type, space and color as the dials a system's levers set. Four big levers, size, scale, density and weight, set how a page feels; the dials, typography, spacing and color values, follow from them, and dials set without levers feel "arbitrary" (Ch. 3, pp. 60–61). The Verge got a more pronounced size contrast than Vox because it is meant to feel rebellious (p. 65). A type system is three decisions: a base font size, larger "like 18px" where elements are large and never lower than 14px; a scale from a ratio, a higher ratio for more contrast; and a line height (pp. 66–67). A spacing system is a base number, often 4px, and a linear or a nonlinear progression (p. 68), named numerically because descriptive and T-shirt names run out (p. 69), and applied as less space within a component and more between containers (p. 70). Color is hue, saturation and luminance, and a palette fragments through variations in the last two, as when Lyft found fifteen pinks (pp. 70–71); the hue of the neutrals shifts how every action color reads, a cool gray against a warm one (p. 72). When type is themed, teams change only the base size and the ratio, "from ten to two" choices (Ch. 4, p. 93); space is themed "as a lever, not as dials", with three density settings at most (p. 95). The read-back records that most of the evidence is the author's two employers (read-back section 8).

**Laying the Foundations** (Couldwell, 2019). A full position, on the palette and the text styles as foundations. A brand's marketing and its product can differ, Dropbox's product using lighter colors, more compact spacing and a more reserved typeface than its marketing site (Chapter 3, p. 43). An audit at Hipmunk found "an alarming amount" of colors and led to a semantic palette with numbered steps (Chapter 6, pp. 116–117), and colors and text styles are the least disruptive place to start (p. 125). A color system starts from a base named 50 with three darker and three lighter steps in 10% increments, named by number so that "red30" means one thing in design and code (Chapter 8, pp. 154–155), without "too great a range" of variants (p. 155); colors fall into groups, primary, utility grays for copy and headers, and accents used less often (pp. 155–157). Text styles are few: "a few header styles, and a few copy styles" (p. 157). Spacing follows a logic, numbers divisible by 5 or an 8-point grid, and Primer's added 4px is "smart", since a minimum of 8px is "a little limiting" (p. 175). Documentation limits typefaces and weights, asking whether a product needs both semi-bold and bold and answering "Pick one", and records each text style's size, line height and letter-spacing (Chapter 9, pp. 196–198). The read-back records that every worked example is the author's own employer, client or project (read-back section 8).

**Form Design Patterns** (Silver, 2018). A narrow position, on the size and spacing of a form field. A label sits closest to its own control, because equal spacing "might improve aesthetics" at the cost of usability; the label and the box are large enough to read and tap, which "probably means a font size of at least 16 pixels" (Ch. 1, p. 32). For a large screen, "increasing the font size and white space" is usually enough (Ch. 7, p. 308). The label's spacing is [the forms synthesis](forms.md)'s.

**Inclusive Components** (Pickering, 2018 on its copyright page, 2019 in the book table). A narrow position, on measure, card spacing and a dark theme. Measure "is a question of characters", so a dialog's text is capped in `ch` (Modal Dialogs, p. 281), and a card's text container at about 60 characters (Cards, p. 299). The gap between single-column cards grows for people who target by touch with low accuracy, among them people with Parkinson's disease (p. 304). A low-light "night mode" is one of the few alternative themes that "adds real value" (A Theme Switcher, p. 116), and a dark mode chosen in the operating system is honored by default (p. 128).

**Atomic Design** (Frost, 2016). A narrow position, on how the visual values are found rather than what they are. A 20-second gut test establishes "aesthetic values", the participants scoring sites on typography, color, density and layout (ch. 4, p. 126); style tiles then explore color, typography and texture without full comps (pp. 127–129), and element collages apply them to real interface elements (pp. 129–130). In the waterfall the book argues against, a developer finds "seven typefaces" and nine button styles too late (p. 116).

**Giving a Damn About Accessibility** (Byrne-Haber, 2021). A narrow position, on color. Great accessibility makes color contrast accessible "for everyone, and in objects beyond just text", and checks it in color-blindness modes and on focus indicators (Ch. 4, pp. 41–42). The rest is [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md)'s.

**Going Offline** (Keith, 2018). No position. The book is about service workers and caching, and says nothing about hierarchy, spacing, type or a palette. The nearest is the web app manifest's `theme_color` and `background_color`, a color "that matches the branding of your site" (Ch. 9, p. 124).

## Where the books agree

- **Hierarchy is made with more than size.** *Refactoring UI* does it with weight and color as much as size (Hierarchy is Everything, pp. 32–34); Brown with compositional contrast of size, weight, color and tempo (Ch. 5, pp. 159–164); Perez-Cruz with four levers, size, scale, density and weight (Ch. 3, pp. 60–61); Kholmatova with density, from spacing, controls and heading sizes together (ch. 1, p. 19).
- **A few sizes, chosen once, not a size per screen.** *Refactoring UI*'s "way too many font sizes" (Designing Text, p. 88); the Malewiczes' 3 to 5 sizes (Typography, p. 165); Couldwell's "a few header styles, and a few copy styles" (Chapter 8, p. 157); Kholmatova's unified type scale (ch. 9, p. 214); Perez-Cruz's base size and ratio (Ch. 3, p. 66); and Frost's seven typefaces found too late (ch. 4, p. 116). How the sizes are picked is a conflict, below.
- **One or two families and few weights.** The Malewiczes' one or two fonts with two weights (Typography, p. 165); *Refactoring UI*'s two weights, and nothing under 400 at small sizes (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 34); Couldwell's "Pick one" of semi-bold and bold (Chapter 9, p. 196); Brown's single anchor typeface chosen first (Ch. 3, p. 64).
- **Space comes from a base unit, not from the eye each time.** *Refactoring UI*'s scale from 16px (Layout and Spacing, pp. 62–63); the Malewiczes' base of 8 or 10 (Layout and grid, pp. 61–64); Perez-Cruz's base of often 4px (Ch. 3, p. 68); Couldwell's numbers divisible by 5 or the 8-point grid (Chapter 8, p. 175); Kholmatova's base measurement unit (ch. 9, p. 229) and Airbnb's eight-pixel grid (ch. 6, p. 136); *Inclusive Design Patterns*' unit of one paragraph line height (A Blog Post, p. 102). The base itself is a conflict, below.
- **More space around a group than within it.** *Refactoring UI* (Layout and Spacing, p. 86); Perez-Cruz's less space within components and more between containers (Ch. 3, p. 70); *Inclusive Design Patterns*' heading margin in `em`, so a heading sits closer to the content it introduces (A Blog Post, p. 103); the Malewiczes' rule of proximity (Perception, p. 37). The label and its field are [the forms synthesis](forms.md)'s.
- **Prose has a measure.** *Refactoring UI*'s 45 to 75 characters (Designing Text, pp. 99–100), *Inclusive Design Patterns*' 45 to 75 after Bringhurst (A Paragraph, pp. 52–53), *Inclusive Components*' measure as "a question of characters" and a card's text at about 60 (Modal Dialogs, p. 281; Cards, p. 299), and the Malewiczes' eight columns of twelve, about ten words a line (Layout and grid, p. 75). How the measure is found is a conflict, below.
- **No pure black, and a gray that carries a hue.** *Refactoring UI*'s true black that looks "unnatural" and its grays saturated toward a temperature (Working with Color, pp. 125, 139–141); the Malewiczes' pure black avoided for text and fills, and a gray "mixed with your primary color" (Colors, pp. 107, 122–123; Typography, p. 151); Perez-Cruz's neutrals whose hue shifts every action color (Ch. 3, p. 72); *Inclusive Design Patterns*' tempered contrast, not stark black on white (A Paragraph, pp. 57, 68). How far contrast may go is [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md)'s.
- **The accent is spent sparingly.** *Refactoring UI*'s accents used "pretty sparingly" (Working with Color, p. 128); the Malewiczes' call to action as the only uniquely colored element, and their 60/30/10 (Buttons, p. 186; Colors, p. 120); Kholmatova's TED red, which in the wrong places or in excess "doesn't feel like TED anymore" (ch. 4, p. 87); Couldwell's accents used less often than primary and utility colors (Chapter 8, p. 157); Perez-Cruz's question of how prominently color is used (Ch. 3, p. 72).
- **A palette left to grow without set steps fragments.** *Refactoring UI*'s "35 slightly different blues" (Working with Color, p. 129), Perez-Cruz's fifteen pinks at Lyft (Ch. 3, pp. 70–71), Couldwell's Hipmunk audit (Chapter 6, pp. 116–117), Kholmatova's 62 grays at UXPin (ch. 9, p. 227). How many steps a color gets is a conflict, below.
- **Density is a choice, made on purpose.** *Refactoring UI*'s dense interface as "a deliberate decision" (Layout and Spacing, p. 59); Kholmatova's spacious, regular and compact modules (ch. 10, pp. 270–271); Perez-Cruz's density lever with three settings at most (Ch. 3, p. 68; Ch. 4, p. 95); Couldwell's Dropbox product, more compact than its marketing (Chapter 3, p. 43).
- **Text size starts from the reader's own setting.** *Inclusive Design Patterns* sets the root at 100% (The Document, p. 29), and Brown does the same and calls the default "a rock" (Ch. 2, pp. 40–43). *Refactoring UI* starts its spacing scale from 16px because it is the browser default (Layout and Spacing, p. 62). The relative units that carry this are [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md)'s.

## Where the books conflict

- **How a type scale is picked.** *Refactoring UI* picks by hand, 12 to 72px, because a modular scale gives fractional pixels and jumps "too limiting" for an interface (Designing Text, pp. 89–91). Brown builds a modular scale from the body size and a 3:5 ratio, because related numbers "feel harmonious", while allowing a reader to "eyeball" it (Ch. 4, pp. 99–102). Perez-Cruz sets a base and a ratio, a higher ratio for more contrast, and themes type by changing only those two (Ch. 3, pp. 66–67; Ch. 4, p. 93). The Malewiczes multiply by 1.618, or take multiples of the grid base (Typography, p. 148). Couldwell and Kholmatova name no method, only few styles with a documented use (Chapter 8, p. 157; ch. 9, p. 214).
- **How large body text is.** Silver: at least 16px in a form field (Ch. 1, p. 32). The Malewiczes: 16 to 18 points on mobile, 18 "the safest", smaller sizes for labels and metadata (Typography, p. 159). *Inclusive Design Patterns*: the 16px default has "a rationale" (The Document, p. 29). Perez-Cruz: 18px where elements are large, and no lower than 14px (Ch. 3, p. 66). Brown: a size that feels familiar, 1.15rem in his example (Ch. 4, pp. 93–98). *Refactoring UI*'s scale runs down to 12px (Designing Text, p. 91).
- **Whether type grows with the room it is given, and whether its steps keep their ratio.** Brown warns that changing body size as a layout widens moves readers away from their familiar size, then allows it as "a respectable choice" (Ch. 5, pp. 146, 152), and asks for less contrast between steps as a layout narrows (pp. 164–166). *Refactoring UI* says relative sizing does not scale: large elements "need to shrink faster" than small ones (Layout and Spacing, pp. 79–81). *Inclusive Design Patterns* grows everything together with `calc(1em + 1vw)` on the root, so every step keeps its proportion (The Document, pp. 31–32). Silver grows "the font size and white space" for a large screen (Ch. 7, p. 308), and Perez-Cruz lists spacing that changes with device size as one reason to theme space (Ch. 4, p. 95).
- **What sets line height.** *Refactoring UI*: it rises with line length and falls as font size grows, 1.5 to 2 for body and down to 1 for a large headline (Designing Text, pp. 105–108). Brown: it flexes with the measure, though his example keeps 1.5 (Ch. 4, pp. 106–110). *Inclusive Design Patterns*: 1.5, after WCAG 1.4.8 (A Paragraph, p. 55). The Malewiczes: the font size times 1.618 (Typography, p. 144). Perez-Cruz: one global line height for the type system, smaller to make text read denser and larger to make it airier (Ch. 3, pp. 66–67).
- **How a measure is found.** *Refactoring UI*, *Inclusive Design Patterns* and *Inclusive Components* give a character count (Designing Text, pp. 99–100; A Paragraph, pp. 52–53; Cards, p. 299). The Malewiczes give columns and words, 30 to 50 characters on mobile and 6 to 9 words on a larger screen (Layout and grid, p. 75; Typography, p. 151). Brown calls measure "a visual decision", made by resizing a text block, with a count only as a check on it (Ch. 4, p. 103; Ch. 5, p. 149).
- **The unit a size is written in.** *Refactoring UI* writes the type scale in px or rem and never em, then caps a paragraph's width in em (Designing Text, pp. 92–93, 99). Brown and *Inclusive Design Patterns* size from a percentage root in rem and em, and Brown says pixel-sized type "does not respect" the reader's setting (Ch. 2, p. 44; The Document, pp. 29–31). *Inclusive Design Patterns* sets heading margins in em on purpose, so they follow the heading's own size (A Blog Post, p. 103). The Malewiczes work in points (Objects, p. 84).
- **The base of the spacing scale.** *Refactoring UI*: 16px, with small steps of 4, 8 and 12 and no two values closer than about 25% (Layout and Spacing, pp. 61–63). The Malewiczes: 8 points, "currently the most popular", or 10 (Layout and grid, pp. 61–64). Perez-Cruz: often 4px, in a linear or a nonlinear progression (Ch. 3, p. 68). Couldwell: multiples of 5, or an 8-point grid with a 4px step added (Chapter 8, p. 175). *Inclusive Design Patterns*: one line height of the body text, 1.5rem (A Blog Post, p. 102).
- **How many steps a color gets, and how they are made.** *Refactoring UI*: 8 to 10 grays and 5 to 10 shades of each color, nine named 900 to 100, picked by hand, because lighten and darken produce near-duplicates (Working with Color, pp. 125–131). Couldwell: a base named 50 and three steps each way, generated by darkening and lightening in 10% increments (Chapter 8, pp. 154–155). Kholmatova: only the variations the roles need, "three variations of blue", or none, as at FutureLearn, which avoided tints and shades; where there are steps, increments of 20% from a base (ch. 9, pp. 227–229). Perez-Cruz: a few color groups, each a background with its foreground roles, after a first attempt of "almost three hundred" variables (Ch. 4, pp. 98–100). The Malewiczes: a 60/30/10 balance of three colors (Colors, p. 120).
- **The color model.** *Refactoring UI* works in HSL, raising saturation as lightness leaves 50% and rotating hue to change brightness, because equal HSL lightness does not look equally bright (Working with Color, pp. 119–138). Perez-Cruz names hue, saturation and luminance as the three things a palette varies in (Ch. 3, p. 70). Couldwell generates steps with Sass darken and lighten (Chapter 8, p. 154). The *Refactoring UI* read-back records that OKLCH and related color spaces reached every major browser in 2023 (read-back section 7).
- **Depth.** *Refactoring UI* gives two answers side by side: shadows from a light above, in about five elevations (Creating Depth, pp. 151–161), and a flat design where a lighter surface reads as closer (p. 168), with fewer borders throughout (Finishing Touches, pp. 206–209). The Malewiczes shadow buttons and cards, at no more than 40% opacity and in the object's own hue (Objects, pp. 88–90; Buttons, p. 194). Kholmatova lists FutureLearn's "1px light-gray strokes" among its signature patterns (ch. 4, p. 105).
- **Corner radius.** *Refactoring UI* treats radius as personality, small neutral, large playful, none formal (Starting from Scratch, pp. 21–22). The Malewiczes: 2 to 6 points reads friendlier than sharp, taken from the grid base (Objects, p. 84).
- **Whether a heading looks like its level.** *Refactoring UI*: choose the element for its semantics and style it for the hierarchy, so an `h1` can be small (Hierarchy is Everything, pp. 46–47). *Inclusive Design Patterns*: style one level as another only with caution, because "an `<h2>` should look like an `<h2>`", and a page structured one way and styled another separates seeing and non-seeing readers (A Blog Post, p. 79).
- **How a link looks.** *Refactoring UI*: in an interface where almost everything is a link, an ancillary link shows its underline or color "only on hover" (Designing Text, pp. 109–110). Brown and *Inclusive Design Patterns*: links are underlined (Ch. 4, p. 136; A Paragraph, pp. 58–60). The Malewiczes: a thicker font and a color change, never color alone (Navigation, p. 296).
- **Letter-spacing.** *Refactoring UI* leaves it to the type designer, with two exceptions (Designing Text, pp. 115–117). Brown adjusts it as part of tempo, loosened to make a longer measure feel acceptable and to help small text (Ch. 5, pp. 152, 164). Couldwell records it as one of three values every text style documents (Chapter 9, p. 198).
- **A dark theme.** *Inclusive Components*: a low-light theme is one of the few that "adds real value" (A Theme Switcher, p. 116). The Malewiczes: a dark mode on dark grays is "purely an aesthetic choice", with no data that it is easier on the eyes (Design Styles, p. 347).
- **Evidence.** *Refactoring UI* rests on its authors' own figures and cites no study (read-back section 8); Brown's familiar size rests on his own unpublished measurements (Ch. 4, p. 94); the Malewiczes' numbers are their studio's practice, with uncited figures (read-back section 8); Kholmatova, Perez-Cruz and Couldwell rest on their own employers and clients (each read-back's section 8). The one outside standard several books share is WCAG: *Refactoring UI*'s contrast ratios (Working with Color, p. 142), *Inclusive Design Patterns*' leading after WCAG 1.4.8 (A Paragraph, p. 55), and the Malewiczes' 18-point body text, which they attribute to WCAG 2.0 (Typography, p. 159).

## What the repo already decided

- **The brief.** [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../../design/brief.md), the *Plum Dusk, evolved* direction chosen on [#409](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/409) (closed 2026-08-28) and amended on [#417](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/417) (closed 2026-08-29) and [#433](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/433) (closed 2026-08-29). Its governing principle is "Conventional in pattern and behavior. Distinctive in execution.", and two of the four places it spends that distinctiveness are this theme's: "Typography, executed strictly. One family with a real scale, tracking that changes with size, tabular figures where numbers line up, and a measure on prose", and "Rhythm. One spacing scale, one card padding, one row height, one gutter, applied without exception". Its Mood is "Warm rather than clinical, but warm by a few degrees only".
- **Type, in the brief.** "One family: Hanken Grotesk. Headings, body, labels, buttons, tables. There is no second family and no display serif. Hierarchy comes from size, weight and tracking, never from a change of voice." The scale is eight steps in `rem`, "at a `16px` root", replacing a ramp that "was never chosen": `display` 36px, `heading-lg` 28px, `heading` 20px, `subheading` 16px, `body` 15px, `body-sm` 14px, `label` 13px and `meta` 12px, at weights of 600 for the four heading steps, 500 for `label` and 400 for the rest, line heights from 1.15 at `display` to 1.55 at `body`, and tracking from `-0.02em` to `0.01em`. Three standing rules follow: "Tracking tightens as size grows and loosens as it shrinks", "Tabular figures everywhere a number can be compared", and "Prose gets a measure", the `--measure: 65ch` cap, with form fields "sized to their content, not to the measure". `display` is "The one page title on a hub, and the greeting on the signed-out landing", the second added on [#1645](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1645) (closed 2026-10-02).
- **Color, in the brief.** Plum Dusk "survives, and it changes": the accent hue moves from `325` to `339` and its lightness from 48% to 41%, "so it carries text at small sizes", and the neutrals move to a hue of about `350`, so they "lean pink, not violet", in five surface steps. The rules: "The accent appears on primary actions and active state, and nowhere else"; "Neutrals carry structure", with a surface step reached for "before reaching for color"; "Status color is not accent color"; and contrast floors that are checked, which [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md) sets out. "Nothing in this product is pure white or pure black, in either theme", because those extremes "fatigue the eye over a long shift". Dark mode is "Derive, do not invent": the same hues, "Only lightness moves", with the surface ladder inverted and a lighter accent. The Laws of UX tables add the Von Restorff row, plum is memorable "*because* it is almost the only color on the page", and the conflict between restraint and the Aesthetic-Usability Effect is resolved as "Restraint wins, and the aesthetic budget is spent on typography, rhythm and smoothness instead of on color".
- **Space, depth and shape, in the brief.** Density is "compact rows, airy forms", adopted whole from the [#406](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/406) survey (closed 2026-08-28): a 40px table row at `body-sm`, a 40px control with 12px of horizontal padding, 20px between fields and 28px between groups, "The spacing scale stays on a 4px base", 40px page gutters "at desktop width", a card padding of `20px 22px`, and a 60px top bar. "No drop shadows anywhere. Not on cards, not on the top bar, not on dropdowns. Depth is expressed by the surface ladder and by one-pixel borders." "Radius is `8px` on cards, panels, inputs and buttons", with no second, larger radius. Borders are 1px, and "A `2px` rule is reserved for the active-state marker under a nav item and appears nowhere else." The Law of Proximity row reads "Spacing does the grouping before any border does", and the Law of Prägnanz row reaches "the same conclusion the direction reached on shadows".
- **The tokens.** `app/src/lib/styles/tokens.css`, whose API was settled on #417: "OKLCH is authoritative. No hex, no rgb(), anywhere in this file or in any component", because "a perceptual lightness axis is what makes the dark theme a derivation rather than a second hand-tuned palette"; color names follow Material Design 3's roles; typography is "eight steps, four axes each", whose names "are read only inside src/lib/components/** and src/lib/styles/**", so that "a route names a purpose (`<Heading variant="section">`), never a size"; three weights, 400, 500 and 600; spacing is "ten steps on a 4px base", with floors of 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 40 and 48px; "One corner radius only", `--radius: 8px`, with "deliberately no --radius-md"; and `--measure: 65ch` from [#98](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/98) (closed 2026-08-16). `app/src/lib/styles/fonts.css` loads Hanken Grotesk at weights 400 to 600 and declares a `Hanken Grotesk Fallback` face with `size-adjust`, the brief's "a font is loaded with a metric-compatible fallback". `atoms/Heading.svelte` keeps "Two axes, two owners (#417)": `level` is document structure, and `variant` is "what the heading IS, and the design system decides how big that looks"; the `display` step is "deliberately unreachable" from it.
- **The fluid scale.** [#531](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/531) (closed 2026-08-31) made "Every type size and every spacing step" a `clamp()` whose growth term is `cqi`, climbing one ramp from 320px to 1920px, built on [#540](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/540) (closed 2026-08-31). The brief's sizes "survive as the **floors**", each type ceiling is the floor times 1.2, each spacing ceiling the floor times 1.5, "Nothing in the app gets smaller than it is today", and four values stay fixed: `--hit-target-min`, `--focus-ring-width`, `--border-thin` and `--border-active`. Its resolution declines a modular ratio: "this repo's six sizes sit at roughly a **1.067** ratio, hand-picked in the brief and closed by #417", and "Reopening the ratio is a ticket against #417, not this one." `CONTEXT.md` defines a **Fluid step**, whose floor and ceiling are `rem` "so a person's own font-size setting still moves it (WCAG 1.4.4)"; **The ramp**, where "past it, more room buys more content, not larger letters"; and a **Growth factor** "chosen once for a whole scale rather than per step, so the relationships inside the scale hold at both ends", whose *Avoid* line names "Scale ratio, modular ratio" as "a different number this repo does not use". [ADR-0024](../../../adr/0024-layout-is-intrinsic-and-320px-is-a-conformance-commitment.md)'s rule 4, added on [#544](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/544) (closed 2026-08-31): "A containment context declares the base size on its children, never on itself."
- **The gates.** `tokens.spec.ts` proves the contrast floors in both themes, holds `tokens.css` to OKLCH only, asserts that no token is pure white or pure black, and re-derives every `clamp()` from the ramp, so that a literal edited by hand "fails here rather than shipping"; it also holds the web app manifest's two colors to the sRGB values of `--color-primary` and `--color-surface` ([#1486](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1486), closed 2026-10-02). `tokens.usage.spec.ts` walks every component and route in `app/` and `site/` and "fails on a raw value where a token is the answer", with three checks, "uses no raw px length", "names no color of its own", and "sets no font-size, font-weight or letter-spacing outside the scale". `zoom.svelte.spec.ts`, from [#545](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/545) (closed 2026-08-31), asserts that "every fluid step can be brought to twice its size within the 500% a browser zooms to".
- **Where a token's value was changed by what it rendered.** [ADR-0019](../../../adr/0019-pen-dev-is-the-working-surface-and-code-is-the-truth.md) records that `color-mix(in oklch, ...)` in the shipped `Badge.svelte` "collapses four of five badge tints to nearly the same lavender"; [#434](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/434) and [#659](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/659) (both closed 2026-09-02) moved the mix to `oklab`. [#509](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/509) (closed 2026-08-30): a `DataTable` column with `numeric: true` "right-aligns the header with its body cells" and "turns on `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums`", as [`docs/design/govuk-alignment.md`](../../../design/govuk-alignment.md)'s Table row records. [ADR-0039](../../../adr/0039-a-layout-primitive-spaces-its-children-with-gap.md): a layout primitive spaces its children with `gap`.
- **The brand.** [`docs/marketing/brand.md`](../../../marketing/brand.md), settled on [#1000](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1000) (closed 2026-09-27): "the brand is the app's own tokens and the app's own mark, refined", so the sheet "names tokens and files.
