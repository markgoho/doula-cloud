# Theme synthesis: the design system

## What this file is for

This is one of the five theme syntheses for the reference track that [#1684](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1684) adds, on the path that [the forms synthesis](forms.md) and [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md) took first. ADR-0041 gives the reference track a read-back and then an adopt-or-not decision in the shape of ADR-0021; #1684 puts a synthesis between the two, so that the founder's grilling session and the adopt-or-not ADR for this theme start from one page. The file lays out what each of the twelve reference-track books claims about what a design system owns, how it grows and who looks after it, and what a template is; where the books agree; where they conflict; and what the repo has already decided. It contains no ruling. Every book on the track is named below, with "no position" where the book has none, so that a reader can tell an omission from an absence. #1684's table names four books with a position on this theme; the books show that seven more have one, *Refactoring UI* and *Designing User Interfaces* at length and *Form Design Patterns*, *Inclusive Design Patterns*, *Inclusive Components*, *Flexible Typesetting* and *Giving a Damn About Accessibility* narrowly, and each is given a full entry. One book, *Going Offline*, has none.

Three neighbors share ground with this theme, and this file does not repeat them. How a control is built so that everyone can operate it, and the books' point that a design system carries an accessibility defect or fix to every instance, is [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md)'s; this file uses that point only for what it says about how a system changes. How a form asks, checks and recovers is [the forms synthesis](forms.md)'s. And the values of the scales a system holds, which steps a type scale has, how far apart spacing steps sit, how many shades a color gets, belong to the visual craft and type theme, which is not yet written; this file covers that a system holds a scale and how it holds it, not what the steps are.

Page citations follow each read-back's own page rule, stated in its source line. *Inclusive Components* cites the PDF index, because the book prints no numbers. Every other book cites its printed page: equal to the PDF index for *Laying the Foundations*, *Refactoring UI*, *Designing User Interfaces* and *Giving a Damn About Accessibility*; the PDF index less one for *Design Systems* and *Inclusive Design Patterns*; less two for the body of *Form Design Patterns*, whose Introduction prints roman numerals and is cited by them; less five for *Expressive Design Systems*, *Flexible Typesetting* and *Going Offline*; and, for *Atomic Design*, an offset that changes at two missing pages, as its read-back sets out. The read-backs are in `docs/research/books/`. Where a citation below differs from the one a read-back gives, it was taken from the book itself. Heydon Pickering wrote two books on the track, and each is named by its title wherever the two differ; Kholmatova means *Design Systems*, Perez-Cruz *Expressive Design Systems*, Couldwell *Laying the Foundations*, Frost *Atomic Design*, Silver *Form Design Patterns*, Byrne-Haber *Giving a Damn About Accessibility*, and the Malewiczes *Designing User Interfaces*. GOV.UK and Material Design are not books on the track; where they appear below, each is either a source a book cites or a record the repo adopted. The repo records were read on 2026-10-03.

## The question

What does a design system own, from tokens and components up to page templates; how does a new thing enter it, change in it, or leave it; who looks after it and where its truth lives; and what is a template?

## What each book claims

**Design Systems** (Kholmatova, 2017). The whole book is on this theme, written for "small and medium-sized" product teams from three years at FutureLearn and interviews with Airbnb, Atlassian, Eurostar, Sipgate and TED (Introduction, pp. viii, xiii–xv).

*What it owns.* A design system is "interconnected patterns" and "shared practices" organized around the product's purpose (ch. 1, p. 18). Patterns are of two kinds, functional modules such as a button and perceptual styles such as color, type, padding and animation (ch. 1, p. 27). A pattern library is a tool for collecting and sharing them and is "not the system itself" (ch. 1, pp. 35–36); what makes a system effective is the shared design knowledge in the team, not its tooling (ch. 6, p. 165). Styles are systemized by their purpose before their values: "twenty shades of blue" are no problem while blue keeps one meaning, a palette starts only with what its roles need, and a base value with meaningful increments serves color, type, spacing and animation alike (ch. 9, pp. 214, 227–229); a style is documented by how it is used, not only by its value (ch. 10, pp. 265–271).

*How it grows.* Every system sits somewhere on three scales, strict or loose, modular or integrated, centralized or distributed (ch. 6, p. 135). A strict system can turn rigid enough to force "UX compromises" for consistency's sake, so it needs room outside its rules (ch. 6, p. 145); more modular is "not always better" (ch. 6, p. 151); and modularity has costs, among them teams that force the reuse of a module and pages that end consistent without being coherent (ch. 6, pp. 154–155). The right system is the one whose "downsides" a team can manage (ch. 6, p. 165). Sipgate documented every pattern and a year later found adding a module easier than finding one (ch. 6, p. 134). A team either adds every element under strict review or adds one only on "the second, or even third use", keeping a log of what is not yet documented; both carry a risk, duplication in the first and invisibility in the second, and FutureLearn's own criterion is how specific the element's purpose is, so that a seasonal promotion stays a one-off that is shared and explained (ch. 10, pp. 274–275). The more specific a pattern is, "the less reusable" (ch. 8, pp. 195–196), and whether two elements are one pattern with variants is tested by asking whether a change to one should change the others the same way (ch. 8, pp. 197–201). A name limits a pattern's future, since a "pink" button can only be pink (ch. 5, p. 109), and naming "is also a UX decision" (ch. 8, pp. 202–203). The interface inventory is repeated "every few months" (ch. 3, pp. 71–72).

*Who looks after it, and where its truth lives.* A centralized team gives ownership and direction and becomes a bottleneck, Airbnb's new-module process taking "up to two weeks"; a distributed model gives autonomy and dilutes direction (ch. 6, pp. 157–160). Either way the systems team are "partners, rather than police" (ch. 10, p. 277). Full synchronization of library and code is "extremely difficult", and at Atlassian it is "always slightly off-sync" (ch. 10, pp. 279–280); at FutureLearn the library became the "source of truth" and the master design file kept only stable elements (ch. 10, pp. 282–283). The work "is never done" (ch. 10, p. 256).

*What a template is.* FutureLearn tried atomic design and "struggled to find a use" for templates and pages, spent too long deciding whether a thing was a molecule or an organism, merged the two, and kept two levels; more than two types of functional pattern "can get confusing" (ch. 10, p. 252). A strict system such as Airbnb's documents its larger patterns, user flows, templates and pages, while a loose one documents smaller parts and leaves designers to combine them (ch. 10, p. 253).

The read-back records that the evidence is six companies, one of them the author's employer, with no team shown abandoning a system, and that the book's practices, a pattern wall, catch-ups for 16 to 20 people, a naming pair, assume a team of some size and say nothing about a system kept by one person (read-back section 8).

**Expressive Design Systems** (Perez-Cruz, 2019). The whole book is on this theme, written for a team "already working in systems" that wants room for brand expression in them, and not as a "step-by-step guide" (Introduction, pp. 3, 5). For a small team chasing efficiency, "a small set of documented components" may be all that is needed (Introduction, p. 2).

*What it owns.* A pattern library is the reusable building blocks, a style guide documents brand language and rationale, and a design system is both plus the principles under them (Introduction, p. 2). A successful system has three parts: components, guidelines for using them, and a governance model (Ch. 5, p. 114). Components sit in four layers, given as "a general rule" and not a fixed taxonomy: basic components such as buttons, links and text fields, which stay functional; composite components; containers; and layouts (Ch. 3, pp. 51–59). Theming is a token, a role and a value, so a role resolves to a different value per theme (Ch. 4, pp. 87–89). A spacing scale grows from a small base unit (Ch. 3, pp. 68–69), density is themed as one lever with three settings at most (Ch. 4, pp. 95–97), and color is themed through a few named groups, after Vox Media's first attempt produced "almost three hundred" variables (Ch. 4, pp. 96–100).

*How it grows.* A component enters the shared system past a stated bar: Vox Media's five criteria included use by "three or more" of its eight brands, with an exception for a brand's must-have (Ch. 2, pp. 35–36). Deviation is of three kinds, unintentional divergence from a documentation gap, intentional but unnecessary divergence, and "intentional, meaningful divergence", which is the book's goal (Ch. 4, pp. 76–78). A system evolves from use: a Shopify team found that a banner did not fit inside a card, and the fix went back into Polaris for every team (Ch. 5, pp. 105–107). Governance is one of four models, solitary, centralized, federated or cyclical, and the cyclical model "sounds ideal" and is "harder than it seems" to run (Ch. 5, p. 111). A system starts simple in components, guidelines and governance alike, on Gall's law that a working complex system has "evolved from a simple system" (Ch. 5, pp. 114–115), and a system that feels too complete can discourage teams from adapting it, so the author watches its borders, where people combine its parts with their own (Ch. 5, pp. 115–116). The right granularity depends on who uses it: Bootstrap is nearly all basic components for many unrelated teams, Mailchimp's system mostly composite components for one product, a "pantry, not a cookbook" (Ch. 5, pp. 102–104).

*What a template is.* The top layer is "layouts (also known as templates)", which decide how a screen's components are arranged (Ch. 3, p. 58).

The book closes on "systems aren't neutral": a system reflects the people who build it (Conclusion, pp. 117–118). The read-back records that most of the evidence comes from the author's two employers, Vox Media and Shopify, and that the consolidation it describes in most detail is given with no measured outcome (read-back section 8).

**Laying the Foundations** (Couldwell, 2019). The whole book is on this theme, from fifteen years of practice, with "no company hat" (Introduction, p. 12). Not every project needs a design system, and every project benefits from designing systematically (Chapter 1, p. 22); a startup can treat one as a low priority while its direction changes fast (Chapter 1, p. 23).

*What it owns.* Digital Foundations, the brand, tone, formatting and accessibility guidelines for digital work, come before a system, because without a distinct brand an off-the-shelf system "would do the job" (Chapter 3, pp. 40–43). The Foundations Model then runs foundations, components, patterns, and templates and pages for a website (Chapter 4, pp. 84–91). A color system generates its steps from a base value and names them by number, not by mood (Chapter 8, pp. 152–156); text styles are few, each with a documented use (Chapter 8, pp. 157–159); and a design token names a use rather than a raw value, so it can change without side effects where the raw value is reused, for spacing as for color (Chapter 8, pp. 165–175). Names match exactly across design, code and documentation (Chapter 8, pp. 146–151). Documentation is what separates a design system from a UI kit or a style guide, and it is written as the system is built: the author did not document Adobe Portfolio's system and says "I should have" (Chapter 9, pp. 181–187).

*How it grows.* A system is built iteratively, sprint by sprint, or wholesale, and the author recommends "a mix of the two" (Chapter 5, pp. 111–113). An audit once found "26 different styles of buttons", and the answer was not one button but variants named for their functions (Chapter 6, pp. 121–123). Iterating piece by piece without a look at the whole risks a "Frankenstein monster" (Chapter 6, p. 126). The model's terms are the team's to change (Chapter 4, p. 84), and the system speaks in "guidelines" rather than rules, because rules feel like "laws that can't be broken" and push designers to go rogue, though a global nav or a footer may warrant rigidity (Chapter 4, pp. 101–103). The design, the build and the rules are not "set in stone" (Chapter 1, p. 21).

*Who looks after it.* A change follows a defined process, the library updated, the code work tracked, the documentation updated and the team told (Chapter 11, pp. 247–251), and ownership runs from a single lead to a team to ambassadors on each product team reporting to a lead (Chapter 11, pp. 254–257). A system is "a marathon, not a sprint" and is never finished (Chapter 11, p. 258).

*What a template is.* A template is a consistent layout used many times with different content, a page is either a template applied or a bespoke one-off such as a home page, and a site keeps as few templates as possible, for the person learning it and for the cost of keeping it (Chapter 4, pp. 91–92). For a product rather than a website, "features" and "screens" are the more fitting layers, a screen being a step in a user journey whose content may differ per account (Chapter 4, pp. 93–94), and a team that finds neither set of terms fits is told to "re-work the model" (Chapter 4, p. 95).

The read-back records that every worked example is the author's own employer, client or project, that the gallery of systems is drawn from large organizations with dedicated teams, and that the book's tool advice rests on Sketch (read-back section 8).

**Atomic Design** (Frost, 2016). The whole book is on this theme, and its taxonomy is the one the repo's component library is organized by (read-back section 6).

*What it owns.* An interface is a system of components, not a collection of pages, a metaphor that has "overstayed its welcome" (ch. 1, p. 10). The method has five stages, atoms, molecules, organisms, templates and pages, held "at the same time" as a whole and as parts, not a sequence (ch. 2, p. 42). Atoms are elements such as labels, inputs and buttons that cannot be broken down "without ceasing to be functional" (ch. 2, p. 43); molecules are simple groups that do one thing; organisms are distinct sections and may contain other organisms (ch. 2, pp. 44–47). A ready-made framework dresses every site alike and imposes its own names, and the answer is "tiny Bootstraps for every client" (ch. 1, pp. 21–23). The names are "not rigid dogma", and GE's team replaced every one of them (ch. 2, pp. 59–60).

*How it grows.* A new pattern is treated as a one-off until a second application says "I want that"; adding one on every whim makes a "Wild West" (ch. 5, p. 158). A defect found on one product page, such as a custom dropdown that does worse than the native one, is fixed in the system so the fix reaches every instance (ch. 5, pp. 144–146). Patterns are modified, added and removed, and removal is planned and announced (ch. 5, pp. 157–159). A name is context-agnostic, "card" and not "product card" (ch. 5, pp. 176–177). The time to start is "now", and where stakeholders refuse, the author's advice is to "Do it anyways", the way performance and accessibility are built in (ch. 4, pp. 96, 107).

*Who looks after it, and where its truth lives.* A system needs makers and users; a large company keeps full-time makers, a smaller one gives the role to senior staff, and at a "scrappy startup" the same small team may be both (ch. 5, pp. 150–154). The "holy grail" is a pattern library and production kept in sync from one source (ch. 5, pp. 159–160), and markup is the hard part to share (ch. 5, p. 162). Designs move into the browser early, "deciding in the browser", and stay there (ch. 4, pp. 139–140). A pattern library is made public, because its patterns are "not nuclear codes" (ch. 5, pp. 173–175).

*What a template is.* At templates the chemistry ends: a template is a page-level object that places components in a layout and shows the page's "underlying content structure" rather than its final content (ch. 2, pp. 49–50). A page is a template with real content poured in; it is what stakeholders sign off, it tests the system, and it carries the template's variations, a cart with one item or ten, a dashboard section hidden from a first-time user, an administrator's extra buttons (ch. 2, pp. 52–55).

The read-back records that the evidence is the author's own projects and tool, that Pattern Lab, the tool chapter 3 teaches through, was archived in May 2026, that the book gives no test for which tier a component belongs to, and that its template is defined by Pattern Lab's data files, with one dashboard as the only stateful screen (read-back sections 7 and 8). It also records the author's own later addition of design tokens as "subatomic particles" before atoms, in a 2019 post (read-back section 7).

**Refactoring UI** (Wathan and Schoger, 2018). A position on what a system owns at the lowest level, and on how it grows. Choosing from unlimited values is "a paralyzing curse", and three buttons in three nearly identical blues make the point (Starting from Scratch, p. 24). The answer is to "Define systems in advance", a set of 8 to 10 shades and a restrictive type scale chosen once, so the hard work of picking values is done once and not per screen (pp. 25–26), and then to pick by elimination among values that look clearly different (pp. 26–27). The book lists what gets a system: font size, weight, line height, color, margin, padding, width, height, shadows, radius, border width and opacity (pp. 27–28). Not all of it is defined ahead of time: the reader keeps a "system-focused mindset", introduces a new system as each new decision comes up, and avoids making the same minor decision twice (p. 28). The spacing and sizing scale, the type scale and the palette are each built later in the book (Layout and Spacing, pp. 60–64; Designing Text, pp. 88–93; Working with Color, pp. 124–131), and their values are the visual craft theme's. The read-back records that the book never mentions Tailwind CSS, the framework its two authors also made (read-back section 8).

**Designing User Interfaces** (Malewicz and Malewicz, 2020). The Design Systems chapter carries a full position. A design system is "a documented library" of components ready to use in code, from base values such as typography, margins and colors up to nested components, with code-ready definitions for developers, and Brad Frost's atomic design is named as "an outline" for a good one (Design Systems, p. 359). Its purpose is consistency as a team grows past informal channels: one brand the authors analyzed styled its login and registration forms differently inside one app (p. 360). The documentation is the "Source of Truth", it is never complete, and its first rules say who accepts a change and how; a system "should match your company" and is treated as an internal product (p. 361). Colors, fonts and the grid are defined first, because they influence every component (p. 363); each element has rules for which colors it may use, and objects take their colors only from the library so that a change to the system changes them all (p. 364). Values are design tokens, defined in one place, on a soft grid from a base of 8 or 10 points, ideally read by both the wiki and the code from one JSON file, with no raw pixel values in components or documentation (pp. 365–366). The documentation is built "in code only", with the developer as the one person able to change it, on the authors' experience that harder changes mean "less chaos" (p. 367). A larger component's page links each part down to its atoms, and the atomic names are "not necessary" so long as the team agrees on its own (p. 369). A research page between versions records why a change was made, such as an A/B test that enlarged a radio button's active area (p. 370). The minimum team is a UI/UX designer, a product owner and a front-end developer; a new function may still need non-standard UI, which is then added to the system, and the system is a baseline to "expand" (p. 372). Elsewhere, an early style guide is a bridge to a system (Design Process, pp. 352–357), and a UI audit counts the typefaces, colors and radius and shadow values that drift across similar objects (UI Audit, pp. 375–386). The read-back records that nearly every worked example is one invented product, and that several of the book's numeric choices are the authors' own studio practice stated as settled (read-back section 8); the Random Soda product card of the chapter's own figure is that product (p. 369).

**Form Design Patterns** (Silver, 2018). A narrow position, on why a pattern is reused and where composing from parts fails. Patterns exist so that a team does not solve the same problem from scratch, and so that a person meets one problem solved one way, which makes a service familiar; a pattern should be "unique, but reusable across projects and organizations" (Introduction, pp. ix–x). One template-language component that serves both the email and the password field helps to enforce the principle "be consistent" (Ch. 1, p. 38), and a later form reuses the email pattern rather than solving it again (Ch. 2, p. 75). The book also names "one of the dangers" of composing an interface from predefined smaller components: the overall design "can end up neglected", which is why a checkout page gets its own streamlined layout with no navigation or search (Ch. 2, p. 113).

**Inclusive Design Patterns** (Pickering, 2016). A narrow position, on the pattern as the unit of a library. A paragraph is an atom in Frost's terms (A Paragraph, p. 47). An audit reported by pattern rather than by WCAG principle lets one inclusive replacement go into the organization's pattern library "one-for-one", so the next developer has an exemplar, and tickets organized by pattern "can be easily reconciled" with a library (Evaluation By Pattern, pp. 109–116); [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md) has that audit's accessibility side. A pattern documented in a library defines the structure of one item, and its context comes first (A List Of Products, p. 195). Tests are written against a pattern's own prescribed structure, beside it, and kept out of the production build (Test-Driven Markup, pp. 304–307).

**Inclusive Components** (Pickering, 2018 on its copyright page, 2019 in the book table). A narrow position, on a component that has to work wherever a system puts it. A component built well once can "live in your pattern library" and be reused indefinitely (A Todo List, p. 63). In a modular design system, where a component will sit in the document is not known ahead of time, so its heading level is set by nesting logic rather than fixed (A Todo List, pp. 38–39). A component in a design system may be "borrowed and used" by many people, so it carries tests and warnings against misuse (Tooltips & Toggletips, p. 112). Giving different things the same name, "dropdown" being the example, harms the clarity of a component library and can lead to the wrong component being reused (Menus & Menu Buttons, p. 64). The more of a design system written in native code, as web components, the more "interoperable, reusable, and future proof" it is (Collapsible Sections, p. 167). A design system will probably need a modal dialog, and it is kept to no more than it "should have to handle" (Modal Dialogs, pp. 273–274).

**Flexible Typesetting** (Brown, 2018). A narrow position, on a system of measurement held as named values. The book's method is offered for websites, apps and "design systems" alike (Introduction, p. 3). `rem` is for "connected, systematic measurements" such as font sizes, and `em` for self-contained refinements (Ch. 2, p. 46). Variables hold "our system of measurement": numbers from the modular scale, the measure and line-spacing limits, the font stacks, and the breakpoints, each breakpoint named for the layout change it handles (Ch. 5, pp. 166–168). Breaking a layout into isolated components makes each one's integrity easier to keep, and the next step is how components relate to one another (Ch. 5, p. 173). The scale's values are the visual craft theme's.

**Giving a Damn About Accessibility** (Byrne-Haber, 2021). A narrow position, on a system as something other software consumes. A design system is reusable components, "the software version of Legos", and a quick start to building; because other software consumes its building blocks, one accessibility defect in it "will show up in every single component" (Ch. 4, p. 45). A team building its own system or component library makes the components accessible and exposes the ARIA properties developers must set; a team using an open-source system that is not accessible fixes it and contributes the fix back, as PayPal did with Bootstrap (Ch. 4, p. 46). The read-back records that two of the four open-source systems the book names have since been archived (read-back section 7).

**Going Offline** (Keith, 2018). No position. The book is about service workers and caching, and names no design system, pattern library or component library. The nearest is a line about code rather than design: whenever the same kind of code is written more than once, it may belong in a reusable function (Ch. 7, p. 99).

## Where the books agree

- **A system is more than its library.** Kholmatova calls the pattern library "not the system itself" (ch. 1, pp. 35–36); Couldwell says documentation is what separates a design system from a UI kit, which "could be applied to anything" (Chapter 9, p. 181); the Malewiczes say a component library is a start and its documentation makes the system work (Design Systems, p. 361); Perez-Cruz names components, guidelines and governance as its three parts (Ch. 5, p. 114).
- **A value is chosen once, held in one place, and named for its use.** *Refactoring UI* defines systems in advance (Starting from Scratch, pp. 25–28); Couldwell's token names a use and not a raw value (Chapter 8, pp. 165–170); Perez-Cruz's token, role and value, with semantic names (Ch. 4, pp. 87–89); the Malewiczes' tokens, with no raw pixel values in components or documentation (Design Systems, pp. 365–366); Kholmatova's purpose before value and a base value with increments (ch. 9, pp. 214, 227–229); Brown's variables for "our system of measurement" (Ch. 5, pp. 166–168). *Atomic Design* has no tokens at all, and its author added them in 2019 (read-back sections 7 and 8).
- **A change made in the system reaches every instance.** Frost's dropdown fixed once (ch. 5, pp. 144–146); Couldwell's one template change that updates every page, and one button change that applies everywhere the button is used (Chapter 4, pp. 92–93); the Malewiczes' library colors that update every object (Design Systems, p. 364); Perez-Cruz's banner fix folded back into Polaris (Ch. 5, pp. 105–107); Byrne-Haber's one defect in "every single component" (Ch. 4, p. 45). The accessibility side of this point is [the inclusive components synthesis](inclusive-components.md)'s.
- **Reuse, not creation, is what earns a place.** Frost's one-off until a second application wants it (ch. 5, p. 158); Kholmatova's second or third use, as one of two approaches (ch. 10, pp. 274–275); Perez-Cruz's "three or more" brands (Ch. 2, pp. 35–36); *Inclusive Design Patterns*' one-for-one replacement that becomes the library's exemplar (Evaluation By Pattern, pp. 115–116). The Malewiczes and *Refactoring UI* do not, below.
- **The names of the tiers are the team's to change.** Frost's "not rigid dogma" (ch. 2, pp. 59–60); Couldwell's terms changed "to suit your team" and the model reworked where it does not fit (Chapter 4, pp. 84, 95); the Malewiczes' atomic names "not necessary" (Design Systems, p. 369); Perez-Cruz's layers as "a general rule" (Ch. 3, p. 52); Kholmatova's FutureLearn, which merged two of them (ch. 10, p. 252).
- **A name is not the look.** Kholmatova's "pink" button (ch. 5, p. 109), Frost's structure named with the content blurred (ch. 5, pp. 176–177), Couldwell's numbered color steps in place of mood names (Chapter 8, pp. 152–156), Perez-Cruz's "River" replaced by "Story Feed" (Ch. 2, pp. 43–44), and *Inclusive Components*' "dropdown" given to different things (Menus & Menu Buttons, p. 64). What a name describes instead is a conflict, below.
- **Parts composed are not a whole designed.** Silver's warning that the overall design "can end up neglected" (Ch. 2, p. 113); Kholmatova's consistency with "little coherence" (ch. 6, p. 155); Couldwell's "Frankenstein monster" (Chapter 6, p. 126); Perez-Cruz's harmony, because components are used "around and within each other" (Ch. 3, pp. 50, 72); Frost's "foolish" isolated button (ch. 2, p. 57); Brown's next step after isolated components, how they relate (Ch. 5, p. 173).
- **Real content tests the system.** Frost's pages, where a page that breaks sends the team back to a molecule (ch. 2, pp. 52–55); Couldwell's "unreasonably long (and short) titles" (Chapter 7, p. 129); Kholmatova's content as a hypothesis (ch. 3, pp. 78–80).
- **Start small, and it is never finished.** Perez-Cruz's Gall's law (Ch. 5, pp. 114–115); Frost's "Make a thing", show it is useful, then make it official (ch. 5, pp. 148–149); Kholmatova's small test project (ch. 7, p. 173); *Refactoring UI*'s systems introduced as decisions come (Starting from Scratch, p. 28). And Kholmatova's work that "is never done" (ch. 10, p. 256), Couldwell's marathon (Chapter 11, p. 258), the Malewiczes' system never complete (Design Systems, p. 361), Frost's system that depreciates "much like a car" without upkeep (ch. 5, p. 179).

## Where the books conflict

- **What a template is.** Frost: a page-level object that shows a page's content structure and not its content, filled into pages that carry its variations and test the system (ch. 2, pp. 49–55). Perez-Cruz: "layouts (also known as templates)", the layer that arranges components on a screen (Ch. 3, p. 58). Couldwell: a consistent layout used many times, as few of them as possible, beside bespoke one-off pages such as a home page; and for a product, "features" and "screens" in place of templates and pages (Chapter 4, pp. 91–95). Silver: a checkout page given its own layout because composed parts neglect the whole (Ch. 2, p. 113). The Malewiczes name templates and pages only inside Frost's frame (Design Systems, p. 359).
- **Whether a system holds templates at all.** Frost and Couldwell put them in the system (ch. 2, p. 42; Chapter 4, pp. 84–91). Kholmatova's FutureLearn "struggled to find a use" for them and kept two levels, and she ties the choice to strictness: a strict system documents templates and pages, a loose one leaves designers to combine smaller parts (ch. 10, pp. 252–253).
- **How many tiers.** Frost five (ch. 2, p. 42); Perez-Cruz four (Ch. 3, pp. 51–59); Couldwell three plus two for a website or three for a product (Chapter 4, pp. 84–94); Kholmatova two, since more "can get confusing" (ch. 10, p. 252). The Frost read-back records that the book gives no test for which tier a component belongs to (read-back section 8).
- **When a new thing enters.** Frost waits for a second application (ch. 5, p. 158), Perez-Cruz for three of eight brands with an exception for one brand's must-have (Ch. 2, pp. 35–36), and Kholmatova sets adding everything under review against adding on reuse, each with its risk, and chooses by specificity of purpose (ch. 10, pp. 274–275). The Malewiczes add a new function's non-standard UI to the system when it is made (Design Systems, p. 372); *Refactoring UI* introduces a new system as each new decision comes up (Starting from Scratch, p. 28); Couldwell keeps an audit's 26 buttons as named variants rather than one (Chapter 6, pp. 121–123).
- **Who may change it, and where its truth lives.** The Malewiczes: documentation built "in code only", the developer the one person able to change it (Design Systems, p. 367). Couldwell: designers get access to the code, so a token change needs no developer (Chapter 8, pp. 175–176; Chapter 11, p. 252), with a shared design library synced to every designer's file (Chapter 11, pp. 243–246). Kholmatova: the library as "source of truth", with full sync of library and code "extremely difficult" and Atlassian content to be "always slightly off-sync" (ch. 10, pp. 279–283). Frost: one source feeding library and production, the "holy grail" (ch. 5, pp. 159–160), and design decided in the browser (ch. 4, pp. 139–140).
- **How strict, and what deviation is.** The Malewiczes want changes made hard, for "less chaos" (Design Systems, p. 367). Kholmatova sets strict against loose and gives each a cost (ch. 6, pp. 144–145). Couldwell writes "guidelines", not rules (Chapter 4, pp. 101–103). Perez-Cruz makes "intentional, meaningful divergence" the goal (Ch. 4, pp. 76–78) and watches the system's borders for it (Ch. 5, pp. 115–116). Frost makes the system adaptable, with governance that answers what happens when a pattern does not fit (ch. 5, pp. 155–157).
- **How a name is chosen.** Kholmatova names by purpose and favors names with personality, "Whisperbox" among them, and calls a hard naming problem "a red flag" (ch. 5, pp. 111–117). Perez-Cruz replaces the metaphor "River" with the plainer "Story Feed" because the metaphor had to be explained to every new person (Ch. 2, pp. 43–44). Frost names by structure, "card" and not "product card" (ch. 5, pp. 176–177). The Kholmatova read-back records that the book itself does not reconcile metaphor with functional precision (read-back section 8).
- **Who looks after it, and how many people it takes.** Frost: makers and users, and at a "scrappy startup" the same small team is both (ch. 5, pp. 150–154). Perez-Cruz: four governance models, and for a small team "a small set of documented components" may be enough (Ch. 5, p. 111; Introduction, p. 2). Couldwell: a lead, a team, or ambassadors (Chapter 11, pp. 254–257), and a startup may give a system low priority (Chapter 1, p. 23). The Malewiczes: three people at least (Design Systems, p. 372). Kholmatova: centralized or distributed (ch. 6, pp. 157–160), with nothing on a system kept by one person (read-back section 8).
- **Whether to ask permission.** Frost's "Do it anyways" (ch. 4, p. 107) sits beside his "Make it official" with real time and money (ch. 5, pp. 148–149), which the read-back calls a tension resolved by sequence (read-back section 8). Kholmatova says a side project "is not enough" without senior stakeholders (ch. 7, pp. 166–167), and Couldwell pitches the same system differently to leaders, engineers and designers (Chapter 2, pp. 30–31).
- **Public or private.** Frost makes the library public, its patterns "not nuclear codes" (ch. 5, pp. 173–175). Couldwell calls publishing optional, since "outside attention" is not the reason (Chapter 3, pp. 77–78). Kholmatova observes that teams who made some of their work public "tend to progress faster", with Eurostar's public style guide as the case (ch. 7, p. 176).
- **Evidence.** Each of the four books with the theme as its whole subject rests on its author's own work: FutureLearn and five interviews for Kholmatova, Vox Media and Shopify for Perez-Cruz, the author's employers and clients for Couldwell, the author's projects and tool for Frost (each read-back's section 8). The Malewiczes rest on one invented product, and *Refactoring UI* cites no study (each read-back's section 8). No book measures a team with a system against a team without one (*Design Systems* read-back, section 8).

## What the repo already decided

- **The tiers.** [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93), still open on 2026-10-03, is the ticket for a "custom Atomic Design component library", and its scope note set that library to "atoms, molecules, organisms only", with templates and pages left as ordinary routes; [ADR-0018](../../../adr/0018-templates-are-a-design-system-layer-with-two-named-exits.md) reversed that on [#410](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/410) and added Templates as a fourth tier in `app/src/lib/components/templates/`, keeping the book's names because Atomic Design "is one of two things the map declared it would not change"; `PageShell` and `Scaffold` were rejected for the cost of "no longer speaking Atomic Design". Components carry no tier suffix, because "the directory carries the tier", and `GLOSSARY.md` reads a bare "Template" as the Atomic Design layer, with the domain's Plan Template and Client Field Template always written qualified. #424's rule, as ADR-0018 states it, is that a molecule is "a part of a section" and an organism "a whole one". On 2026-10-03 the directory holds 17 atoms, 25 molecules, 21 organisms and 10 Templates as Svelte files, with `PageTitle.svelte` at its root outside any tier; ADR-0018's table has a row for nine of the Templates, and `ErrorPage` is named in its 2026-09-02 amendment.
- **What a Template is, in the repo.** ADR-0018: a Template owns "page-level arrangement, and nothing else"; chrome belongs to `+layout.svelte`, gutters and the width cap to the Template, and layout primitives stay internal, so "region-internal arrangement is the page's". Its regions are Snippet props, extending [#97](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/97)'s rule that typed props are the sole configuration surface, with no `class` or `style` passthrough. `OverviewHub` "cannot be instantiated without" an empty state; the amendment of 2026-08-30 makes loading and load-error states the Template's too, and the amendment of 2026-09-29 restates it as "a frame lives only on a Template". The amendment of 2026-10-02 makes the shell own the window's height, and a Template "asks for the height; it does not measure it".
- **How the repo's system grows.** ADR-0018's "Two named exits, and no third": a route opts out and composes raw, findable by a grep, or a variant prop or a new Template is proposed, and the bar between them is "One consumer stays a raw exception; two identical consumers earn a variant or a Template", from [#196](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/196), [#187](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/187) and [#189](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/189). The ADR says the rule "is expected to be revised" and fixes only that deviation is never "anonymous". Its amendment of 2026-09-20 records that the bar does not govern reuse of a Template whose region already fits, and its amendment of 2026-10-02 gives `LandingPage` a Template on one consumer, "and the reason is not a new exit": the frame and the brief's `display` step both have to be in a Template. [`docs/design/workflow.md`](../../../design/workflow.md): atoms, molecules, organisms and Templates come first, raw `<a>` and `<button>` are forbidden outside the atoms by `svelte/no-restricted-html-elements`, and "When the design needs something an atom cannot do, grow the atom." The brief's Occam's Razor row: "a new atom must justify itself against composing existing ones".
- **What the tokens own.** `app/src/lib/styles/tokens.css`, whose API was settled on [#417](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/417) per the brief's "What this brief does not decide": OKLCH is authoritative, with "No hex, no rgb()" in the file or any component; color names follow Material Design 3's roles; eight type steps at four properties each; and the step names are read only inside `src/lib/components/**` and `src/lib/styles/**`, so "a route names a purpose (`<Heading variant="section">`), never a size". `GLOSSARY.md` defines a **Fluid step** as a token whose value is a `clamp()` between a floor and a ceiling, and a **Growth factor** "chosen once for a whole scale rather than per step". The values are the visual craft theme's.
- **The gates that hold it.** `tokens.spec.ts` proves the contrast floors in both themes. `tokens.usage.spec.ts` walks every component and route, in `app/` and `site/`, and fails a commit "on a raw value where a token is the answer", added after [#452](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/452) shipped five hard-coded lengths; its `tokens:ignore` marker carries a reason and covers one rule block. [ADR-0020](../../../adr/0020-smoothness-is-gated-on-causes-because-the-outcome-is-not-measurable-where-the-gate-lives.md)'s `motion.spec.ts` gates durations and easing the same way, with `motion:ignore`. [ADR-0039](../../../adr/0039-a-layout-primitive-spaces-its-children-with-gap.md) moved spacing into the layout primitives, so 45 margin resets "are fixed by the mechanism, all at once". `style-guide.spec.ts` reads the four tier directories off disk and fails CI for any component with no `/style-guide` page, the gate [#104](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/104) asked for, and ADR-0018 put the Templates under it because they are "the layer most likely to drift". [ADR-0025](../../../adr/0025-layout-is-verified-across-the-continuum.md): "A component with no fixture fails the build", and a component demo declares every state its page renders.
- **Where the truth lives.** [ADR-0019](../../../adr/0019-pen-dev-is-the-working-surface-and-code-is-the-truth.md), decided on [#412](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/412): `app/src/lib/components/**` and `tokens.css` "are canonical", a canvas node is "a sketch of something, never the definition of it", and "When the two disagree the code wins"; generating `DESIGN.md` was rejected, because "`tokens.css` is the machine-readable truth and [the brief] is the prose one". The style guide is a route of the app, `app/src/routes/style-guide/`, and its layout's comment reads "Never deployed -- this route only exists for local `vite dev`".
- **How it is built and shared.** [ADR-0003](../../../adr/0003-css-layout-primitives-as-native-custom-elements.md): the library styles itself with scoped Svelte `<style>` blocks reading tokens, not a utility-first framework, under one `@layer` order, with the layout primitives as light-DOM custom elements. [ADR-0051](../../../adr/0051-the-marketing-site-is-sveltekit-not-hugo.md), on [#1467](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1467): "One design system, by import", the marketing site importing `app.css` whole, and "One set of gates", `tokens.usage.spec.ts` and `layout.usage.spec.ts` reading `site/` too; the brief's "Adopting this in `site/`" says the site spends the app's tokens "rather than a copy of them". [ADR-0021](../../../adr/0021-govuk-is-the-reference-for-service-patterns.md): GOV.UK is a reference for decisions and never for markup or look, and adopting `govuk-frontend` outright was rejected because "It would replace tokens.css and the brief in one move".
- **What a component may assume.** [ADR-0024](../../../adr/0024-layout-is-intrinsic-and-320px-is-a-conformance-commitment.md): a component adapts to "the space it is actually given", never to a device, and its section "No named set of widths, ever" rules out shared width names. `CLAUDE.md`'s Layout expectation: "Choosing a component is a layout decision".
- **The brief.** [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../../design/brief.md), chosen on [#409](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/409): "Conventional in pattern and behavior. Distinctive in execution."; its third source of character is Rhythm, "One spacing scale, one card padding, one row height, one gutter, applied without exception", where "consistency is what a Template layer exists to enforce"; and the four named sources come with "This is not a license to add a fifth", any other being "a change to the brief".
- **Who looks after it.** [ADR-0049](../../../adr/0049-doula-cloud-is-one-person-by-design-and-no-night-needs-him.md): "Doula Cloud is one person by design", the founder building it "with the agents he runs". No record names a design-system team or an ambassador; the gates above and the Experience axis of `/code-review` that the brief names as the reviewer of its Laws of UX tables are the mechanisms on record.
- **Two records read differently from the decisions they rest on**, as of 2026-10-03. The brief's Depth and shape section says "The `--radius-sm: 8px` token in `tokens.css` is already right". `tokens.css` has no `--radius-sm`: commit `c0c1549` on #417, which closed on 2026-08-29, renamed it `--radius: 8px` "so there is no --radius-md to reach for", and the file's own comment says the same. Two read-backs, *Design Systems* and *Atomic Design*, quote a brief section titled "Adopting this in `hugo/`" in their section 6 entry 27, and a third, *Inclusive Design Patterns*, names `hugo/layouts/page.html` and `hugo/layouts/index.html` in its entry 1; ADR-0051 replaced the Hugo site on #1467, and the brief's section now reads "Adopting this in `site/`".
