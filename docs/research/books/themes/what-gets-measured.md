# Theme synthesis: what gets measured

## What this file is for

This is the second stage of ADR-0041 for one theme on the values track. It lays out what each of the twenty-nine values books claims about what a product like this one counts, how it attributes a customer to a source, and what it refuses to collect; where the books agree; where they conflict; and what the repo has already decided. It contains no ruling. Page citations follow each read-back's own page rule. The read-backs are in `docs/research/books/`. Where an agreement or conflict bullet names Walling without a title, it means the two 2023 books, *The SaaS Playbook* and *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*, which were the only Walling books on the track when the bullet was written; *Start Small, Stay Small* is named by title wherever it is meant. A bare Blank means *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* and Blank and Dorf means *The Startup Owner's Manual*; a bare Miller means *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* and Miller and Peterson means *Marketing Made Simple*; Dib is named by title or as both of his books wherever the two differ.

## The question

What does the product count, what does it refuse to count, and what number stands in for churn when nobody subscribes?

## What each book claims

**The SaaS Playbook** (Walling, 2023). The 80/20 metrics chapter: monthly recurring revenue, churn, and the timed emails that move them (80/20 SaaS Metrics, pp. 151–161). Measure every marketing channel and keep a changelog (Marketing, pp. 102–106).

**Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding** (Walling, 2023 ed.). Measure every source with analytics goals and drop the ones that do not convert; a mailing list converts up to ten times website traffic ("Nine Levels", pp. 18–21). Ask customers how they found you and what they would use instead; last-click analytics under-credits the first touch; the benefit customers name may not be the one marketed ("Four Things I Learned", pp. 150–152).

**Make: The Bootstrapper's Handbook** (Levels). Google Analytics is good enough, Mixpanel is expensive, Amplitude is mostly free, and Hotjar's session streaming is useful though its ethics might be questionable (Launch, pp. 80–81).

**The Almanack of Naval Ravikant** (Jorgenson, 2020). Systems, not goals, citing Scott Adams; the author rejects self-measurement and says to help others without counting (Saving Yourself, pp. 183, 188; Building Wealth, p. 75).

**Design for Cognitive Bias** (Thomas, 2020). Clicks are easy to measure and may say nothing; there is close to an inverse relationship between how easy a metric is to measure and its value; Goodhart's Law (ch. 3, pp. 48–49). Whatever is incentivized gets gamed (ch. 3, pp. 41, 49).

**The Best Interface Is No Interface** (Krishna, 2015). Chapter 5 argues that engagement measured as screen time rewards the wrong thing, since the best interface is the one a person does not have to look at.

**Jobs To Be Done** (Ulwick, 2016). The outcome survey measures importance and satisfaction per desired outcome, on 180 to 3,000 respondents (ch. 4 §IV, p. 98; ch. 6, pp. 170–172). No position on product analytics.

**Landing Page Hot Tips.** Track marketing channels with platform-specific coupon codes (Tip #15, PDF p. 42). Its usage-figures tip (Tip #83, PDF pp. 154–155) is about what a page displays, carried by the persuasion theme.

**Copyhackers: Uplift.** An A/B test yields data for the hypothesis; failing that, user testing with five to ten people like the target audience (Section 5, pp. 49–50). No other position on measurement beyond reporting each page's test result.

**Obviously Awesome** (Dunford, 2019). No position on product analytics or on what a company counts from day to day. Its nearest claims are diagnostic and arithmetical. Four signs of weak positioning are listed, one of them high churn shortly after purchase, which the author calls particularly bad for subscription businesses that depend on renewals, beside long sales cycles, low close rates, and price pressure (Introduction, pp. 20–22). An opinion of one's own value does not count as proof, and third-party validation does, whether an independent reviewer's judgment or an approved customer quote; a measurable time to proficiency is among the ways an ease-of-use claim could be proved (Components, pp. 47–48; Step 5, pp. 81–82). The segment test is deals needed next year against businesses in the segment, and thirty deals needed against a hundred businesses means the segment is too small (Step 7, p. 92; Step 8, p. 106).

**Building a StoryBrand 2.0** (Miller, 2025 updated edition). The measurement claims sit in the campaign chapter. A 20 percent open rate is called the industry standard, a deleted email is said to accomplish the goal anyway because the sender is branding itself into the reader's universe, and an unsubscribe is called a good thing (ch. 15, PDF p. 204). Statistics are one of five ways to show competency, modeled on a platform's claim that 125,000 users trust its software (ch. 6, PDF p. 99). The grunt test is a five-second reading of a site's header rather than a number (ch. 2, PDF p. 38), and the BrandScript and the one-liner are rough drafts tested on friends, spouse, customers, and strangers until they produce the reaction wanted (ch. 13, PDF p. 172; ch. 15, PDF p. 192).

**Lean Marketing** (Dib, 2024). Chapter 15 is a metrics chapter. Lagging metrics are results — profit, churn, revenue — about which there is nothing much to do after the fact; leading metrics are early warnings, the worked case being a daily opt-in count that suddenly falls (Chapter 15, pp. 253–254). Lifetime value is named the metric that matters, computed from profit rather than revenue, worked as $700 of annual profit over three years' average tenure for $2,100, recalculated regularly and per major segment, with venture-backed growth businesses named as the exception to the profit rule (Chapter 15, pp. 254–256). Customer acquisition cost follows at $100,000 over 1,000 customers, and return on ad spend and return on investment are distinguished as per-campaign and per-strategy (Chapter 15, pp. 256–257). Monthly recurring revenue and churn are given as the two subscription metrics, MRR the water in the bucket and churn the leak (Chapter 15, p. 257). Thirteen micrometrics are then listed — conversion rate, click-through rate, cost per click, traffic, bounce rate, engagement rate, lead-to-customer rate, marketing qualified lead, sales qualified lead, net promoter score, email open and click rates, average order value, products per customer — prefaced by the warning that more fiction gets written in spreadsheets than in books and that in isolation they do not mean much (Chapter 15, pp. 257–259). The dashboard holds only the handful that matter and is expanded when troubleshooting (Chapter 15, p. 259). Elsewhere the book defines brand marketing as marketing that is not measurable (Chapter 1, pp. 23–24), calls likes vanity metrics and advises not looking at the numbers during the rookie phase (Chapter 13, pp. 219–221), records that the consumer and government backlash removed targeting, tracking, and attribution capabilities from digital advertising (Chapter 13, pp. 223–225), and says a per-person metric must be easy to measure and hard to game, with the Delhi cobra bounty as the case against (Chapter 11, pp. 186–187).

**The 1-Page Marketing Plan** (Dib, 2018 edition). Not tracking is called the mark of an amateur, and Wanamaker's half-wasted advertising is declared inexcusable now that toll-free numbers, website analytics, and coupon codes exist (Chapter 3, p. 111). The author refuses to say what a good response or open rate is — sometimes 50% is a disaster and sometimes 0.01% is a success — and concludes that response and conversion rates are useless in themselves and exist only to produce acquisition cost and lifetime value (Chapter 3, pp. 112, 114–115). Four key numbers are named for any business: leads, conversion rate, average transaction value, and break-even point, worked through an online store where improving the first three by 10% each raises net profit 431%, which the author labels cowboy math for demonstration (Chapter 8, pp. 252–254). Monthly recurring revenue, churn rate, and customer lifetime value are added for subscription or recurring models, with the claim that a business should have a recurring element (Chapter 8, pp. 254–255). A company dashboard is recommended, from a whiteboard to a real-time screen, with customer satisfaction rate and complaint count as candidates and incentives tied to metrics (Chapter 8, pp. 255–256), and the Net Promoter Score is given in full, promoters at 9–10, passives at 7–8, detractors at 0–6 (Chapter 8, pp. 258–259). Marketing that works needs no budget, because setting one implies the marketing either fails or is unmeasured, and unmeasured or failing marketing is told to stop (Chapter 3, pp. 128–129). The book also disowns two of its own figures: a footnote says the follow-up statistics could be traced no further than major national research firms and should be taken with a grain of salt, and the 21-times statistic gets a footnote saying the exact number is irrelevant (Chapter 5, p. 171; Chapter 8, p. 263).

**The Mom Test** (Fitzpatrick, 2013). The book's position is that the early numbers are the wrong instrument. What convinced Buffer's founder was not the metrics or the conversion rate but the conversations that followed from emailing every person who signed up, and the author says he is skeptical of the quantitative validation of landing-page metrics and values a landing page as a source of qualified leads (ch. 6, p. 78). Bad data is sorted into compliments, fluff, and ideas, with false positives called the more dangerous kind because they confirm a dead idea (ch. 2, p. 24). The measure of a useful conversation is whether it produced concrete facts about the customer's life and world view (ch. 1, p. 12), and the measure of a meeting is commitment, in the currencies of time, reputation, and money (ch. 5, pp. 64–66); a trial counts only in proportion to what it costs the person, since the author has tried countless contact-management apps at ten seconds each (ch. 5, p. 70). A one-to-five scale is named as a marker of the formality the book is arguing against (ch. 4, pp. 57–59), the cold-outreach rejection rate is dismissed as irrelevant since ninety-eight refusals still leave two conversations (ch. 6, p. 76), and the stopping rule is to keep talking until new information stops arriving, which can be three to five conversations in a simple industry with a focused segment (ch. 6, p. 89). Exact quotes are wrapped in quotation marks so a later reader knows they are verbatim (ch. 8, p. 105).

**Competing Against Luck** (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, Duncan, 2016). The book's central claim about data is that correlation is what most customer data records and never says why a person chose one thing over another (Introduction, PDF pp. 11–13). Chapter 8 names three data fallacies. Active data, generated after launch by sales and benchmarks, is easy to track and shouts, while the passive data of the customer's struggle has no voice, so managers move from the hazy story to the crisp spreadsheet and end up managing the number — schools teaching to the test, medicine treating blood pressure rather than its cause, a buyback that raises earnings per share and changes nothing (ch. 8, PDF pp. 163–166). Surface growth confuses customers, and conforming data bends toward the view it was asked to support (ch. 8, PDF pp. 166–169). The chapter's strongest general claim is that all data is man-made and carries the agenda of whoever chose what to collect, that the subjectivity of numbers hides behind their precision while ethnography's is obvious, and it offers Google Flu Trends, which a 2014 *Science* paper found had overestimated US flu cases for more than two years, as the worked case (ch. 8, PDF pp. 169–171). Against that the book sets metrics that follow the job: SNHU counts minutes to respond, Amazon counts when an order is delivered rather than shipped, and Intuit builds a metric per product from the customer benefit — with Scott Cook quoted that the metrics do not fall out of the systems, that there is no automatic way to measure the hours an accountant did not spend, and that Intuit interpolates from surveys and server data (ch. 7, PDF pp. 147–148; ch. 9, PDF p. 186). Most data records the Big Hire, the purchase, and misses the Little Hire, the moment of use, so an app vendor tracking downloads learns nothing about whether the app does a job (ch. 5, PDF pp. 92–93). SNHU is reported as keeping one statistic in front of it, whether graduates would do it again, which stood at 95 percent in early 2016 (ch. 9, PDF p. 187). On sampling, insights come from many data points on a small sample rather than few on a large one, and a single interview yields hypotheses and better questions rather than a conclusion (ch. 5, PDF pp. 99, 111–113).

**Forget the Funnel** (Laudi and Suellentrop, 2023). Chapter 7 is about choosing KPIs. Goldratt is quoted that measurement determines behavior, glossed as people minimizing their risk of punishment, with a support team measured on ticket volume or speed pushed toward quick boilerplate replies, since a closed ticket does not mean a solved problem (ch. 7, PDF pp. 95–96). Active-user counts are attacked through FeedOtter, a tool whose value is not having to log in, where measuring engagement by daily logins would push the team to nudge customers back into a product they bought to avoid; the value-aligned measure offered instead is newsletters consistently automated and error-free (ch. 7, PDF pp. 96–97). Lagging indicators are dollar results — ARR, CAC, ARPU, lifetime value — critical to watch and not actionable, because they say what happened and not why; leading indicators are actions customers take, and improving the right ones improves the lagging ones (ch. 7, PDF pp. 97–98). Each milestone then gets one KPI, with new unique website visits chosen over total traffic because the intent is awareness among new people (ch. 7, PDF pp. 98–99), and Value Realization is defined as a threshold of customer actions — for SparkToro, ten or more searches, two or more lists, one or more exports — with First Value set lower (ch. 7, PDF pp. 101–102). Buyer journey maps and pirate metrics are rejected for rewarding transactional moments such as signup or paid over customer value moments, and a trial user who enters a card and never finds the feature they came for is said to have made a worthless card entry (ch. 6, PDF pp. 73–75). The book also charts struggle themes by frequency to find the most common (ch. 5, PDF p. 63), sets interviews rather than surveys below 500 ideal customers (ch. 3, PDF pp. 43–44), tells the reader to benchmark current performance before setting targets (ch. 7, PDF pp. 105–106), and reports Tiller Money's page value in Google Analytics rising 237% after a rewrite from survey responses (ch. 4, PDF pp. 51–52).

**Marketing Made Simple** (Miller and Peterson, 2020). A thin position, held to the campaign. The campaign is reviewed on its data: open rate per email, landing-page conversion, and which email to replace (ch. 10, pp. 165–166). The lead-generator hit rate is given as roughly six in ten, with one of the company's own thorough PDFs named as a dud, and the instruction is to test, keep what works, and start over on what does not (ch. 6, p. 125). Pop-ups are conceded to be annoying and defended only on click rate (ch. 6, p. 123). The book's own evidence is a survey of StoryBrand customers by an unnamed independent research company, reported as a correlation between how much of the checklist a company implemented and its growth (ch. 3, pp. 36–37), and the eye-path advice is hedged at its source, since different studies revealed different patterns and only the weaker claim that eyes do not move randomly is kept (ch. 5, p. 66).

**This Is Marketing** (Godin, 2018). The book's organizing distinction is Lester Wunderman's: direct marketing is action oriented and measured, brand marketing is culturally oriented and cannot be measured, and the danger named is confusing them (Ch. 15, PDF p. 139). The author says the instruction that precedes it — measure everything for direct and refuse to measure for brand, while being consistent and patient — ought to have repaid the price of the book (Ch. 15, PDF p. 140). The online direct chain assigns a value to each step, and no direct-response ads are run until that can be done (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 140–141). Chapter 19 works the funnel arithmetic — a thousand dollars of ads, twenty clicks at fifty dollars, one in ten converting for a $500 order — and adds that very few organizations do the math carefully, that fewer than 5 percent of advertisers measured results when he started, and that he guesses about 60 percent do now with thoughtful analysis still missing (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 163–165). Online advertising is credited with psychographic precision, immediacy, and measurability and then called the most ignored advertising ever created (Ch. 15, PDF p. 138). For a permission asset the proposed metric is how many people would reach out or complain if the next send did not go out (Ch. 17, PDF p. 154), and his own Yoyodyne numbers are given as open rates over 70 percent and an average 33 percent response (Ch. 17, PDF p. 152). Traction is tested by who would miss the thing if it were gone and by how many insist their colleagues use it (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 79–80), remarkability is explicitly not the creator's decision (Ch. 17, PDF p. 157), and the author notes that recent decades have lazily tied status to bank balances and follower counts (Ch. 11, PDF p. 107).

**Start Small, Stay Small** (Walling, 2010). The Market First Approach measures demand before code exists: the Google AdWords Keyword Tool on exact match, halved because the tool overestimates and then multiplied by four, worked as 1,900 global monthly searches becoming about 3,800 visitors and, at 0.2% and a $500 price, $3,800 a month (Ch. 2, pp. 78–79). The author then limits the instrument himself — keyword tools are flawed and nothing more than estimates from extrapolated data, the free difficulty tools are to be taken with a grain of salt and most are called junk, search queries are only one way to gauge demand against a survey of an existing audience, and marketing is fuzzy and imprecise with a leap of faith at the end (Ch. 2, pp. 51, 71, 75–76, 83). The Mini Sales Site, two or three pages and an AdWords campaign with a tracked buy-now click for $50–500, is the last test before building and cannot give 100% certainty either way (Ch. 2, pp. 82–86, 90). Conversion bands are given as 1–4% for consumer products at $1–50 and 0.5–2% above $50 or on recurring pricing, with the author's own figures at just under 3%, about 2.5%, and 0.5–0.7% for a $329 product (Ch. 2, pp. 66–68), and lifting conversion is said to beat buying traffic, since one more sale a month is 100 more visitors or 0.1 points of conversion (Ch. 4, pp. 115–116). The home page's key metric is abandonment rate, cited to *Web Design for ROI* at 40–60% before optimization (Ch. 4, p. 125). Every traffic source is measured with analytics goals and the ones that do not convert are dropped (Ch. 5, pp. 147–148); email is measured by clicks and opens, with subject lines and send times tested and the spam score watched (Ch. 4, pp. 137, 141–143). Automation has a threshold set in advance, the day the assistant's bill for a task exceeds a stated amount (Ch. 6, p. 168), and the metrics to keep for a later sale are traffic, revenue and expenses computed monthly, the names watched through Google Alerts, and every hour split into development, support and marketing (Ch. 7, pp. 197–198).

**Software as a Science** (Martell, Verlaque, Page, Petitpas, 2024). The book treats a SaaS company as a math problem: four numbers — current customers, new customers per month, monthly churn rate, and monthly ARPA — predict the month growth stops, which the authors name the Growth Ceiling and place between $10,000 and $30,000 MRR for most companies' first encounter with it (ch. 1, PDF pp. 30–31). Only three of the four can be changed, so there are three levers, and churn is the one with the largest effect: on their worked company, lifting sales buys twelve months of runway, cutting churn from 10 to 7 percent buys twenty-four, and lifting ARPA makes the company more valuable while moving the ceiling not at all, because the customer count is unchanged (ch. 2, PDF pp. 45–46, 52–55). A channel is scored red, yellow, or green on five questions, one of them a CAC payback period under ninety days, and the column closest to green is taken all the way green before another is stacked (ch. 3, PDF pp. 79–81). First Value is the one moment that makes a user understand the product and is tracked as a core KPI, with activation measured weekly (ch. 6, PDF pp. 126, 143). The Customer Health Index scores accounts red, yellow, and green; the authors say flatly that it will be wrong, tell the reader to start with logins or a guess they call a SWAG — daily healthy, weekly at-risk, none in seven days unengaged — and to leave it alone long enough to collect data, noting that HubSpot revises its own criteria every four to six months (ch. 7, PDF pp. 154–157, 165–166). A mature index combines activity data, feature usage, and sometimes NPS or an account manager's subjective rating (ch. 7, PDF p. 159), the daily dashboard is watched with the note that the yellows hold more opportunity than the reds (ch. 7, PDF p. 162), and self-service cancellation is removed because a free-text reason box teaches nothing (ch. 7, PDF p. 163). Rivera's six questions test a value metric — does it grow with usage, is it easy to track, easy for the customer to understand, predictable, perceived as fair, and does it grow with the customer's business (ch. 9, PDF p. 203) — and NPS is reported to take a large hit above a 50 percent price increase (ch. 10, PDF p. 224).

**The Lean Startup** (Ries, 2011). The unit of progress is validated learning, and progress is tracked by a discipline the author names innovation accounting: use an MVP to establish a real baseline, tune the engine toward the model in the business plan, then pivot or persevere, with a quantitative prediction made for each leap of faith before the test so a result can be read as failure rather than rationalized afterwards (Introduction, PDF pp. 22–23; Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 117–119). Cohort analysis is called the gold standard, each cohort an independent report card that cannot be blamed on legacy customers or market conditions; the same IMVU board data plotted as gross totals shows a hockey stick and plotted as cohorts shows a flat paying rate of about 1 percent, which is the book's illustration of a vanity metric (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 121–123, 127–128). Split-testing is built into product development, and a user story is not done until validated, usually by a split test showing changed behavior, with an unvalidated feature removed; Grockit's lazy-registration test found an industry best practice to be waste (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 134–139). The chapter's three A's close it: actionable means the report demonstrates clear cause and effect, against which a website hit fails because nobody can say what it is; accessible means concrete units and people rather than piles of data points; and auditable means the data can be tested by hand against real customers and drawn from the master data rather than an intermediate system, because the loser of an argument challenges the data and a team whose judgment is overturned by a data fault loses confidence and discipline (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 140–144). Vanity metrics are also named as one of three reasons companies pivot too late (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 159–160). The engines of growth carry their own numbers: the sticky engine compounds at the difference between acquisition and churn, the viral engine at a coefficient above 1.0, and the paid engine at the margin between lifetime value and cost per acquisition (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 203–211). The epilogue names pseudoscience as a danger of the movement, defined as highlighting a graph of gross metrics to assert cause and effect (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF pp. 266–267).

**The Four Steps to the Epiphany** (Blank, 2013 second edition). The book's complaint about the product development model is that sales and marketing have no meaningful milestones before first customer ship, and it proposes questions in their place: whether customers' problems are understood, what they will pay, whether visionary customers exist, and whether the sales roadmap is understood well enough to sell consistently (Chapter 1, pp. 41–43). Launch success is measured by re-running the pre-launch audits afterwards and by a press audit checking whether the company's own framing was picked up, with the author's own trade-show measurement finding more than 78 percent of booth visitors who had not heard of the company able to describe it correctly (Chapter 5, pp. 418–420, 426–427). Demand-creation goals must equal the sales goals, worked as $3 million at a $500 average selling price into 6,000 units and back into qualified and raw leads with a cost per lead and per customer (Chapter 5, pp. 421–426). Customers are asked how they hear about new companies and products (Chapter 3, pp. 163–165, 195). The word customer is reserved for people who pay, and giveaways are said not to prove customers will buy (Chapter 4, pp. 299, 301). The worksheets ask what happens if a dependency does not arrive and what metric would measure that change (Appendix B, p. 610). The chapter's own share thresholds come from the New Lanchester Strategy, about which the author says he does not swear he can derive the formulas or prove the theorems, only that the results come out looking like the real world (Chapter 5, pp. 352–357), and the disclaimer is on the first of those pages (Chapter 5, p. 353).

**The Startup Owner's Manual** (Blank and Dorf, 2012). Manifesto Rule 8 is that startup metrics differ from those in existing companies (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF p. 83). A metrics toolset is built before selling, and the section opens on Wanamaker's complaint that half his advertising was wasted and he did not know which half (Web/Mobile, Ch. 8, PDF pp. 515–523), with a dedicated data analytics chief on the senior management team (Web/Mobile, Ch. 8, PDF pp. 524–526). Optimization is diagnosed on a failing funnel and on cohort analysis of a sales funnel, and growth is monitored by the viral growth factor and a second metric, with the caution that not every product spreads virally (Web/Mobile, Ch. 9, PDF pp. 553–578). Customer validation is proved by orders or usage rather than opinions, with per-category thresholds for how much response counts as enough (Strategy Guide, Ch. 5, PDF p. 124; Web/Mobile, Ch. 6, PDF p. 445), and the high-fidelity MVP test asks not whether customers like the product but how intensely they want it, measured by watching behavior rather than by asking (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF pp. 419–428). The early pass/fail experiments are described as short, simple and objective, looking for a strong enough signal rather than a rigorous one, the example being five of the first twelve customers called on (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF p. 79). The authors bound their own instrument twice: they allow that on rare occasions investors ignore most metrics and vote to scale, while saying that nine times in ten the metrics matter (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 163), and they close the burn-rate guidelines by saying there are zillions of exceptions (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 141).

**Zero to One** (Thiel with Masters, 2014). The book's measurement claim is an attack on the easy number: growth is easy to measure and durability is not, so measurement mania fixes on weekly actives and quarterly earnings while missing whether the business will exist in a decade, with Zynga and Groupon as the named cases of fast growth masking a long-term problem (ch. 5, PDF p. 37). The measure the author substitutes is future cash flow, and he reports his own March 2001 projection putting 75% of PayPal's present value in profits from 2011 onward, which he says still underestimated it (ch. 5, PDF pp. 35–36). Returns follow a power law rather than a normal distribution, and the error named is expecting normally distributed results and diversifying in the hope that winners offset losers (ch. 7, PDF pp. 63–65). Statistics is said not to work at a sample size of one, because companies are not experiments and you cannot rewind to 2004 and start Facebook in a thousand copies of the world (ch. 6, PDF pp. 46–47), and definite planning is contrasted with designing by focus-group feedback, since small changes to what exists reach a local maximum rather than a global one (ch. 6, PDF pp. 59–60). Big data is called usually dumb data, because computers find patterns and cannot compare them across sources or interpret complex behavior (ch. 12, PDF pp. 112–113). Distribution is bounded by two numbers, customer lifetime value against customer acquisition cost (ch. 11, PDF p. 99), and PayPal's own viral figures are given as a $20 per customer signup and referral strategy producing 7% daily growth (ch. 11, PDF p. 103).

**Company of One** (Jarvis, 2019). The book's proposed measure is minimum viable profit, the point at which the business is in the black, which the author argues determines sustainability rather than customer count, measured growth, or gross revenue, and which arrives sooner the lower it is set (ch. 11, PDF p. 127). The Epilogue restates it as Natasha Lampard's exist strategy — sticking around, profiting, serving customers — against quarterly increases or an exit (Epilogue, PDF p. 167). Vanity metrics, named as followers, subscribers, and clicks, are said to show how many people took the marketing bait rather than to measure engagement or trust, with 100 passionate fans set against 100,000 followers chasing a free iPad (ch. 4, PDF p. 60; ch. 10, PDF pp. 121–122). Churn is one of four reasons companies grow, and the author's objection is that it is usually answered by adding customers rather than by finding out why people leave, citing an Econsultancy and Responsys report that a new customer costs five times as much as keeping one, with Kate O'Neill at Magazines.com finding that reducing cancellations beat acquiring subscribers (ch. 4, PDF pp. 54–56; ch. 7, PDF p. 86). Word of mouth is named as hard to measure, since a recommendation can happen in a coffee shop or an untrackable private message, and as hard to scale quickly, which he concedes is bad for a business focused on exponential growth (ch. 10, PDF p. 118). The email channel is measured by open rates and personalized subject lines, on Campaign Monitor's 26 percent and the Epsilon Email Institute's 70.5 percent (ch. 8, PDF p. 102), and the referral pitch in his worked automation goes only to customers who rated their enjoyment above a seven (ch. 10, PDF p. 120).

**Buy Back Your Time** (Martell, 2023). No position on what a product counts. What it counts is the founder's own hours. The Buyback Rate divides what the business pays the founder by a work year the author gives as two thousand hours and calls a rough estimate, and the rule is that nobody performs a task they could outsource for one-fourth of that rate (ch. 2, PDF pp. 55–57; ch. 4, PDF p. 92). The Time and Energy Audit highlights calendar entries by whether they drain or energize, with a footnote saying tasks that do neither can be ignored (ch. 4, PDF pp. 88–89, and the footnote at PDF p. 321). The nearest the book comes to this theme's question is the middle of its delegation framework: check measure, which gives every person one number they can move, with a Volkswagen dealership's absorption rate and a hotel's average daily rate as examples and *Moneyball* for the principle (ch. 11, PDF pp. 196–198); and a Definition of Done which, for larger tasks, states facts, feelings and functionality — the hard metric, how the result must feel, and what it must let others do (ch. 9, PDF pp. 171–172). The book also holds delegated work to an eighty percent standard rather than the founder's own (ch. 2, PDF p. 53).

**Entrepreneur Revolution** (Priestley, 2024 third edition). The measurement instrument is the Weekly LAPS Dashboard, tracking leads, appointments, presentations, and sales each week, improved by a list of three small changes per stage rather than a single fix (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 102–103). The closing figure is a lead-to-sale conversion the author puts at under 5% across the data he sees, so roughly 20 warm leads are needed per sale, illustrated by his own presentation to 40 ideal prospects producing 12 trials and 8 clients (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 103–104); earlier he puts it at roughly 1 in 20 buying at once, so an audience must exceed 200 people for every 10 sales wanted at launch (Chapter 4, PDF p. 96). The brochure that precedes any expensive build comes from a survey of 150 people in the audience and interviews with at least 30, checked back with 10 to 30 (Chapter 4, PDF p. 99). Reputation is named the most prized asset and its measure is the first page of a search for your name (Chapter 8, PDF pp. 173–175). Market size is called mostly irrelevant, with the counter-case that some markets are huge and still very unlikely to be entered (Chapter 4, PDF p. 94), and money is cited as making people happier only up to a point, after which writing millions or billions down as a target is called a recipe for lifelong disappointment (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 91–92).

**The Voltage Effect** (List, 2022). The book is about whether a number measured on a small group survives a larger one. The author reports, citing Straight Talk on Evidence, that between 50 and 90 percent of programs lose voltage at scale (Introduction, PDF p. 16), and the first of his Five Vital Signs is the false positive: the person with an idea should not be its only tester, independent replication is called just as crucial for businesses as for scientists, and at Uber the manager with an idea was the one who tested it (Chapter 1, PDF p. 44). Where three or four replications are not feasible, his stated fallback is caution rather than speed (Chapter 1, PDF p. 38). The second vital sign is that the initial audience may not represent the population served at scale, because people who opt in to a pilot are the ones most likely to benefit from it (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 55, 57–58). Chapter 7 is an argument about which number to read: his colleagues' benefit-cost data were averages, and inside the EPA removing tailings dams cost tens of millions per life saved while compliance standards with the same effect cost tens of thousands, so the rule he draws is to look at the positive impact of the last dollar spent rather than the average (Chapter 7, PDF pp. 155–156). The worked commercial case is a company whose last bucket of dollars on Facebook ads yielded a fiftieth of the business of the last bucket on Google ads while it had been reading average returns (Chapter 7, PDF pp. 161–162), and the practice is to collect data granularly over time and across strategies, run multisite trials, and look per region rather than at a national daily average (Chapter 7, PDF pp. 166–167). Two limits are stated in his own voice: the Virgin Atlantic control group, told only that fuel use would be measured, improved too, which he attributes to the Hawthorne effect (Chapter 6, PDF p. 138); and asked by the Make-A-Wish Foundation to value gifts to terminally ill children, he answered that some things worth doing and scaling transcend monetization and conventional measurement (Chapter 7, PDF p. 167). Counting spillovers expanded the Chicago Heights program's measured impact at least tenfold (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 99–101).

**Thinking in Bets** (Duke, 2018). The book's subject is how to read a number after the fact. Decision quality and luck are the two things that determine how a life turns out, and the author's name for reading decision quality off outcome quality is resulting (Introduction, PDF p. 11; ch. 1, PDF pp. 13–14). A forecast pitched anywhere between 0% and 100% is not shown to have been mistaken merely because the likelier of its futures failed to arrive, which she works through Brexit and through Nate Silver's 30% to 40% for Trump in the week before the 2016 election (ch. 1, PDF pp. 32–34). A single result carrying hidden information and luck supports very little, and the correct answer to her four-coin-flip question is that one is not sure, because the arithmetic answer of 6.25% assumes facts about the coin nobody has (ch. 1, PDF pp. 26–28, 34). Her remedies are to express belief as a percentage or a range and recalibrate as information arrives rather than reversing wholesale (ch. 2, PDF pp. 62–63), to sort each outcome deliberately into a luck bucket or a skill bucket rather than by what flatters (ch. 3, PDF p. 73), and to keep a record of the alternatives weighed, because hindsight bias cuts off every branch that did not happen (ch. 6, PDF pp. 178–181). On the number nobody wants to state, she argues that a number somebody hesitates to guess at is already being guessed at, and that a guess between 20% and 80% beats not guessing at all (ch. 6, PDF p. 166). She also bounds the method: it leaves self-serving bias and motivated reasoning in place and only makes them a little better (ch. 3, PDF p. 96).

## Where the books agree

- Attribution by asking beats attribution by pixel: Walling's "ask how they found you", Hot Tips's per-channel coupon codes, and Thomas's "clicks may say nothing" point the same way from marketing and from ethics. Blank joins from the customer-discovery side, asking customers how they hear about new companies and products (Four Steps, Chapter 3, pp. 163–165); Fitzpatrick joins from the opposite direction, saying what convinced Buffer's founder was the conversations with everybody who signed up rather than the conversion rate (Mom Test, ch. 6, p. 78); and Christensen and his coauthors make the same point about product data, that an app vendor tracking downloads learns nothing about whether the app does a job (Competing Against Luck, ch. 5, PDF pp. 92–93).
- A number made a target stops being a number: Thomas states Goodhart's Law; Naval's "systems, not goals" is the same claim in another register. Laudi and Suellentrop quote Goldratt to the same effect and give a support team measured on ticket volume replying with boilerplate (Forget the Funnel, ch. 7, PDF pp. 95–96); Dib requires a per-person metric to be easy to measure and hard to game, with the Delhi cobra bounty as the failure (Lean Marketing, Chapter 11, pp. 186–187); Christensen and his coauthors call it managing the number rather than the thing, with schools teaching to the test and a buyback that raises earnings per share (Competing Against Luck, ch. 8, PDF p. 166); Ries names the same shape a vanity metric (Lean Startup, Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 127–128); and Jarvis and Thiel both name the metric that is watched because it is easy — followers and clicks for Jarvis, weekly actives and quarterly earnings for Thiel (Company of One, ch. 10, PDF pp. 121–122; Zero to One, ch. 5, PDF p. 37).
- Measure sources, not people: Walling measures channels; nothing in Levels, Walling, or Thomas measures an individual user's behavior except Levels's session streaming, which he himself flags. Laudi and Suellentrop reach the same place from the value side, arguing that measuring engagement by daily logins would push a team to nudge customers back into a product they bought to avoid (Forget the Funnel, ch. 7, PDF pp. 96–97).
- A metric that no action follows from is not worth holding. Laudi and Suellentrop split lagging from leading and call ARR, CAC, ARPU and lifetime value critical to watch and not actionable (Forget the Funnel, ch. 7, PDF pp. 97–98); Dib splits the same pair and puts profit, churn and revenue on the lagging side (Lean Marketing, Chapter 15, pp. 253–254); Ries makes actionable the first of his three A's, meaning the report demonstrates clear cause and effect (Lean Startup, Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 140–142); Godin refuses to run a direct-response ad until a value can be assigned to each step (This Is Marketing, Ch. 15, PDF pp. 140–141); and Dib, in the earlier book, calls response and conversion rates useless in themselves and says they exist only to produce acquisition cost and lifetime value (1-Page Marketing Plan, Chapter 3, pp. 114–115).
- A first number is a guess and is better stated as one. Duke argues that a number somebody hesitates to guess at is already being guessed at and that a range of 20% to 80% beats no guess (Thinking in Bets, ch. 6, PDF p. 166); Martell and his coauthors prescribe a SWAG where usage data is missing (Software as a Science, ch. 9, PDF p. 209); Walling calls his keyword tools flawed estimates from extrapolated data and puts a leap of faith at the end of the method (Start Small, Ch. 2, pp. 51, 83); Ries asks for a quantitative prediction before each test so a result can be read as failure (Lean Startup, Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 117–119); Dib labels his own 431% improvement cowboy math for demonstration (1-Page Marketing Plan, Chapter 8, p. 254); and Blank says his New Lanchester numbers only come out looking like the real world (Four Steps, Chapter 5, p. 353).
- What one group's number says about the next group is the thing to doubt. List says people who opt in to a pilot are the ones most likely to benefit and calls independent replication as crucial for businesses as for scientists (Voltage Effect, Chapter 1, PDF p. 44; Chapter 2, PDF pp. 55, 57–58); Ries answers the same problem with cohorts, each an independent report card (Lean Startup, Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 121–123); Christensen and his coauthors say one interview yields hypotheses rather than a conclusion (Competing Against Luck, ch. 5, PDF pp. 111–113); Fitzpatrick stops at three to five conversations only for a simple industry and a focused segment (Mom Test, ch. 6, p. 89); Duke says a single result carrying hidden information supports very little (Thinking in Bets, ch. 1, PDF p. 34); and Thiel says statistics does not work at a sample size of one (Zero to One, ch. 6, PDF pp. 46–47).
- The email open rate is the number most of these books reach for: Miller calls 20 percent the industry standard (StoryBrand 2.0, ch. 15, PDF p. 204); Miller and Peterson review a campaign on open rate per email (Marketing Made Simple, ch. 10, pp. 165–166); Dib lists who opened a message among a CRM's required reporting (Lean Marketing, Chapter 4, p. 86) and says a subject line's job is to get the email opened (1-Page Marketing Plan, Chapter 3, pp. 123–124); Walling tracks clicks and opens and tests subject lines (Start Small, Ch. 4, pp. 141–143); Jarvis cites Campaign Monitor's 26 percent and Epsilon's 70.5 percent (Company of One, ch. 8, PDF p. 102); and Godin reports his own Yoyodyne open rates over 70 percent (This Is Marketing, Ch. 17, PDF p. 152).

## Where the books conflict

- **Whether to count at all.** Walling's metrics chapter is a dashboard; Naval refuses to keep one. The new books widen the gap on both sides: Martell, Verlaque, Page and Petitpas reduce a whole company to four numbers and a ceiling (Software as a Science, ch. 1, PDF pp. 30–31), Dib builds a dashboard of the handful that matter (Lean Marketing, Chapter 15, p. 259), and Martell's earlier book counts the founder's own hours against a Buyback Rate (Buy Back Your Time, ch. 2, PDF pp. 55–57); against them Godin refuses to measure brand marketing at all and says so as the book's most valuable instruction (This Is Marketing, Ch. 15, PDF pp. 139–140), and Jarvis replaces the dashboard with minimum viable profit and an exist strategy (Company of One, ch. 11, PDF p. 127; Epilogue, PDF p. 167).
- **How much tooling.** Levels names four tools; Thomas warns that the easiest tool measures the least; Krishna's screen-time argument rejects the engagement metric those tools produce. Blank and Dorf put a data analytics chief on the senior management team and a metrics toolset in place before selling (Startup Owner's Manual, Web/Mobile, Ch. 8, PDF pp. 515–526), and Martell and his coauthors run a customer health index, session recordings and click streams (Software as a Science, ch. 6, PDF pp. 139–141; ch. 7, PDF p. 159); Fitzpatrick is skeptical of the quantitative validation those tools produce (Mom Test, ch. 6, p. 78), and Dib records that the backlash against digital advertising removed targeting, tracking and attribution capabilities outright (Lean Marketing, Chapter 13, pp. 223–225).
- **Whether a named person's behavior is measured.** Ries's cohorts and split tests follow individuals through registration, activation and retention (Lean Startup, Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 138–139), Martell and his coauthors score each account red, yellow or green on its own logins and feature usage (Software as a Science, ch. 7, PDF pp. 155–159), and Blank and Dorf run cohort analysis of a sales funnel (Startup Owner's Manual, Web/Mobile, Ch. 9, PDF pp. 564–578). Godin's permission asset is measured only by how many people would complain if the next send did not go out (This Is Marketing, Ch. 17, PDF p. 154), Jarvis counts nothing about a person beyond a rating they volunteered (Company of One, ch. 10, PDF p. 120), and Laudi and Suellentrop reject the login count as a measure of value (Forget the Funnel, ch. 7, PDF pp. 96–97).
- **Whether a split test earns its place.** Ries makes a feature not done until a split test shows changed customer behavior and removes an unvalidated one (Lean Startup, Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 136–137), Copyhackers reads the test result as the data for the hypothesis, Dib names always be testing a durable best practice for paid advertising (Lean Marketing, Chapter 13, pp. 225–227), and Walling tests price by changing it (Start Small, Ch. 3, pp. 100–101). Thiel argues the opposite from planning: small changes to what exists reach a local maximum rather than a global one, and he praises designing by multi-year plan rather than by focus-group feedback (Zero to One, ch. 6, PDF pp. 59–60).
- **The average or the margin.** List's whole seventh chapter is an argument that the average hides the answer and that the last dollar spent is the number, worked on a company reading average ad returns while its marginal Facebook dollar returned a fiftieth of its marginal Google dollar (Voltage Effect, Chapter 7, PDF pp. 155–156, 161–162). Dib's lifetime value is an average over an average tenure (Lean Marketing, Chapter 15, pp. 254–256), Walling's conversion bands are averages (Start Small, Ch. 2, pp. 66–68), and Martell and his coauthors run the Growth Ceiling on average revenue per account (Software as a Science, ch. 1, PDF p. 31).
- **What replaces churn.** Walling's metrics assume a subscription, and so do Martell and his coauthors, whose four numbers include a monthly churn rate and whose largest lever is that rate (Software as a Science, ch. 2, PDF pp. 45–46). Four of the new books name a figure that does not need one, and none of the four names the same figure: Dib computes lifetime value from profit, an average tenure and a purchase frequency, which does not require a recurring charge (Lean Marketing, Chapter 15, pp. 254–256), and in the earlier book reserves MRR, churn and lifetime value for recurring models while giving leads, conversion rate, average transaction value and break-even point to every business (1-Page Marketing Plan, Chapter 8, pp. 252–255); Godin proposes how many people would reach out or complain if the next send did not go out (This Is Marketing, Ch. 17, PDF p. 154); Christensen and his coauthors report SNHU keeping one statistic in front of it, whether graduates would do it again, at 95 percent in early 2016 (Competing Against Luck, ch. 9, PDF p. 187); and Jarvis makes minimum viable profit the determinant of sustainability rather than customer count or measured growth (Company of One, ch. 11, PDF p. 127).
- **Evidence.** Walling's ten-times figure is uncited; Thomas cites Goodhart; Levels's tool opinions are his own. The new books run the same range. Dib prints a footnote saying he could trace his follow-up statistics no further than major national research firms and that they should be taken with a grain of salt (1-Page Marketing Plan, Chapter 5, p. 171); Miller and Peterson rest their strongest claim on a survey by an unnamed independent research company (Marketing Made Simple, ch. 3, pp. 36–37); Priestley gives a lead-to-sale rate of under 5% as what he sees across his own data (Entrepreneur Revolution, Chapter 4, PDF pp. 103–104); List cites Straight Talk on Evidence for a 50-to-90-percent voltage-drop range and flags the Peltzman paper as contested (Voltage Effect, Introduction, PDF p. 16; Chapter 4, PDF p. 88); and Duke records in a footnote that the reverse of self-serving bias exists, is rarer, and is no better because it is equally inaccurate (Thinking in Bets, ch. 3, footnote, PDF p. 231).
- **Whether a measured thing is the whole thing.** List, who built the book on field experiments, says that asked to value the Make-A-Wish Foundation's gifts to terminally ill children he answered that some things worth doing transcend monetization and conventional measurement (Voltage Effect, Chapter 7, PDF p. 167), and reports that his Virgin Atlantic control group improved on being told only that fuel use would be measured (Voltage Effect, Chapter 6, PDF p. 138). Christensen and his coauthors quote Scott Cook that there is no automatic way to measure the hours an accountant did not spend (Competing Against Luck, ch. 7, PDF p. 148). Against them Martell and his coauthors treat a SaaS company as a math problem rather than a snowflake (Software as a Science, ch. 1, PDF pp. 29–30), and Blank and Dorf hold that nine times in ten the metrics matter (Startup Owner's Manual, Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 163).
