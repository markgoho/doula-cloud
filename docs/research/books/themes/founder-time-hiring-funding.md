# Theme synthesis: founder time, hiring, funding

## What this file is for

This is the second stage of ADR-0041 for one theme on the values track. It lays out what each of the twenty-nine values books claims about working alone, the first hire, cofounders, outside money, and the expenses a founder forgets; where the books agree; where they conflict; and what the repo has already decided. It contains no ruling. Page citations follow each read-back's own page rule. The read-backs are in `docs/research/books/`. Where an agreement or conflict bullet names Walling without a title, it means the two 2023 books, *The SaaS Playbook* and *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*, which were the only Walling books on the track when the bullet was written; *Start Small, Stay Small* is named by title wherever it is meant. A bare Blank means *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* and Blank and Dorf means *The Startup Owner's Manual*; a bare Miller means *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* and Miller and Peterson means *Marketing Made Simple*; Dib is named by title or as both of his books wherever the two differ.

## The question

Who works on Doula Cloud, who owns it, whose money is in it, and what changes at the first hire?

## What each book claims

**The SaaS Playbook** (Walling, 2023). Delegate roles, not tasks; support is usually the first hire; a founder who does not plan to hire is challenged to hire at least a support person (Team, pp. 117–126). Equity in an LLC passes a K-1 on paper profits; profit sharing suits a run-it-forever company; a cofounder is not needed and is the largest equity ever given away (Team, pp. 135–141).

**Make: The Bootstrapper's Handbook** (Levels). Work alone early because collaborations can be very dangerous; never pay a developer in equity for an idea, since an unexecuted idea's market value is zero (Idea, pp. 31–32; Build, p. 48). Build it yourself; outsourcing loses the race to the maker who fixes bugs over coffee; avoid hiring and build robots, because a hire makes the founder liable for their income; keep contractors on standby (Build, pp. 41–43; Automate, pp. 172, 177).

**The Almanack of Naval Ravikant** (Jorgenson, 2020). Labor is the worst form of leverage; code and media are permissionless and earn while you sleep (Building Wealth, pp. 34–35, 58, 60). Pick partners for intelligence, energy, and above all integrity; never partner with cynics (Building Wealth, p. 32; Learning Happiness, p. 148; Naval's Writing, p. 224). Own equity; capital is permissioned leverage that someone else has to grant (Building Wealth, pp. 31, 34–35, 53–54). Take business risks under your own name; avoid the risk of ruin (Building Wealth, pp. 34, 52, 66–67). Set an aspirational hourly rate and outsource anything that costs less (Building Wealth, pp. 36, 71–72).

**Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding** (Walling, 2023 ed.). The expenses a founder forgets: entity filing fees, an accountant, a lawyer, errors-and-omissions insurance, health and disability insurance, the employer half of FICA ("Expenses", pp. 81–83). Code, marketing, and money: a founder needs two of the three, and an idea is not one ("How to Recruit a Developer Entrepreneur", p. 155).

**Jobs To Be Done** (Ulwick, 2016). A small one-product company is told to hire the author's firm rather than run the method alone (ch. 7, pp. 177, 182, 186). No other position on staffing or funding.

**Design for Cognitive Bias** (Thomas, 2020). No position.

**Landing Page Hot Tips.** No position.

**Copyhackers: Uplift.** No position.

**The Best Interface Is No Interface** (Krishna, 2015). No position.

**Obviously Awesome** (Dunford, 2019). Positioning is a cross-functional team exercise, and the person who owns the business has to be in the room and be seen to drive it — in a startup the CEO or the founders — because an exercise that is not a team effort driven by the business leader will fail; one or at most two senior people per function is enough, the author has run sessions with as few as three people and as many as twelve, and she recommends an outside facilitator (Step 2, pp. 68–71). On money: customer positioning and investor positioning are different stories, and the process is built for customers, so an investor deck may look dramatically different from the sales deck without either being wrong (Step 1, pp. 63–64). Two of the three positioning styles are costed in outside money — Head to Head is rarely a good choice for a small business launching a product, and taking the lead in an understood category where no leader has emerged often needs outside funding, which the author says may not be how a bootstrapper wants to enter the market, while Create a New Game generally suits established companies with resources or, for smaller ones, deep-pocketed and patient investors (Step 8, pp. 99–101, 115).

**Building a StoryBrand 2.0** (Miller, 2025). No position on who works on a business, who owns it, whose money is in it, or the first hire. Its nearest claim is about who speaks rather than who works: the customer is the hero and the brand is the guide, a brand that positions itself as the hero is destined to lose, and the two characteristics of a guide are empathy and competency, the 2.0 edition having withdrawn the first edition's third word, authority (ch. 3, PDF p. 42; ch. 6, PDF pp. 90–92, 97).

**Lean Marketing** (Dib, 2024). Marketing strategy is the entrepreneur's own responsibility, because no one outside will ever understand the business as well, and routine execution belongs in-house; the attack is on generalist agencies and on abdication rather than on outside help, since specialist agencies are worth their weight in gold and the author says he frequently hires agencies and freelancers, with experts recommended for setting up digital advertising (Chapter 11, pp. 180–181; Chapter 13, pp. 225–226). On hiring into marketing: some top-gun marketers worked their way up from execution, it is the pure managers a reader is told to beware of, and an experienced CMO may be a good hire once there is already a large marketing team and real traction (Chapter 11, p. 182). Outside money appears once, as an exception: computing lifetime value from revenue rather than profit is normally a mistake, except in typically venture-backed businesses focused wholly on growth and market penetration (Chapter 15, p. 255).

**The 1-Page Marketing Plan** (Dib, 2018 edition). Media technique is out of scope and sent to hired specialists rather than learned by the owner (Chapter 3, p. 112). Delegated work is not held to the owner's standard: the rule of thumb is eighty percent as good as you, and the author concedes outright that quality will probably be lower (Chapter 5, p. 168). The book's scope carries the funding position: large-company mass marketing is called effective, expensive and slow, and unavailable below millions of dollars of budget, so the whole plan is written for a business without that money (Introduction, pp. 30–33).

**The Mom Test** (Fitzpatrick, 2013). Customer learning cannot be outsourced or hired out until there is a working business model and a repeatable process, because a hired gun's bad news is hard to assimilate and a lukewarm signal is tempting to gloss over (ch. 8, p. 104). The rule has two stated limits in the same passage: going solo on the conversations is fine once note-taking is concise, with the caveat that it is harder to catch your own mistakes, and hiring people to set up and lead meetings is allowed as long as the founder is in the room listening; the author also allows exceptional team dynamics where outsourcing the learning works (ch. 8, p. 104). Verbatim quotes are kept because their later uses are marketing language, fundraising decks, and settling arguments (ch. 8, p. 105).

**Competing Against Luck** (Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan, 2016). No position on who works on a business, who owns it, whose money is in it, or the first hire — the book's word "hire" is the construct term for a customer pulling a product into their life, not a staffing claim (ch. 2, PDF p. 36). Its nearest claim sits in organizational design: a job spec works like the military's commander's intent, letting people decide without being told, and a start-up's organizing unit is the customer's job until growth replaces it with customers, products, competitors and investors (ch. 9, PDF pp. 180–181, 192).

**Forget the Funnel** (Laudi and Suellentrop, 2023). The framework's precondition is a cross-functional team with four named roles — a Primary Stakeholder, a Champion, contributors, and an optional facilitator — and senior leadership has to lead it, with the authors saying nothing happens without the Primary Stakeholder, who has to protect the process (ch. 2, PDF pp. 27–30, 34). The requirement is then loosened: nothing falls apart if a role has no one in it, the reader is told to go with the people available, the facilitator is optional throughout, and Sprout Social at 200 employees ran workshops instead of involving its leaders directly (ch. 2, PDF pp. 30–33). The authors print the objection that about 75 percent of cross-functional teams fail and answer it by locating the failures where a team lacks a systematic approach, clear ownership and specific goals (ch. 2, PDF p. 29). On money, their own evidence is two seed-funded companies, Unbounce and Calendly, each said to have grown sharply in two years on a seed round and without paid acquisition, with Suellentrop as Calendly's second employee (ch. 1, PDF pp. 17, 21). Where a team has resources for only one research method, interviews are named the choice (ch. 3, PDF pp. 43–44).

**Marketing Made Simple** (Miller with Peterson, 2020). Execution is six scheduled meetings with designers, copywriters, approving managers and support staff in the room, so the work has objectives, owners and deadlines; a reader working alone is told to keep the schedule anyway and to fill the room with outside contractors, and meeting 4 does not need the whole team (ch. 10, pp. 153, 161). The book's founding story is a funding one told against raising: Miller says he lost his savings in a failed investment and rebuilt by running the same five steps repeatedly (ch. 1, pp. 12–14).

**This Is Marketing** (Godin, 2018). No position on hiring, equity, or outside money. Its nearest claims are two. On outside help, hiring a professional designer is advised, with the admission that every once in a while an amateur happens on a vernacular that reminds the right people of the right story (Ch. 13, PDF p. 121). On who stands behind the work, a faceless corporation speaking in the second person is hiding, and the alternative offered is a person taking responsibility for the work (Ch. 6, PDF p. 67).

**Start Small, Stay Small** (Walling, 2010). The book splits its readers into two shapes and names them: a Micropreneur, who wants to stay solo, with no employees under any circumstance, and a bootstrapper, who intends to grow and hire (Ch. 1, pp. 1–2). Self-funding is the premise — the book deliberately skips the investor pitch, securing funding, and what to do with investor cash, and assumes the reader has no $6M in the bank — while saying there is nothing wrong with pursuing venture funding and that most people are simply not positioned to (Preface, PDF 9). Believing you have to do everything yourself is the fifth of five roadblocks, and the fix is drip outsourcing: small tasks sent to a virtual assistant at about $6/hour overseas or $10–20/hour in the United States, which the author says saves him 60–100 hours a month, with graphic design and HTML outsourced every time (Ch. 1, pp. 22–25). The arithmetic behind it is dollarizing your time: set an hourly value from a freelance rate or from salary plus about 30% benefits divided by 2,000 hours, lean toward the higher freelance figure, and treat $100/hour as the long-term target against a reality nearer $25/hour for one-person software companies (Ch. 1, pp. 26–28). A virtual assistant is defined as any remote contract worker, priced task-based at $3–10/hour overseas and $12–50 in the United States, part-time at $2–7 and $10–40, and full-time at $1–5 and $8–35, the last needing 160+ hours of work to fill; screening is deliberately light, an exchange of a few emails with three to five candidates at once, because reliability cannot be tested before hiring (Ch. 6, pp. 165–166, 173–177). Work is done by hand before it is automated, with the automation threshold set in advance at the day the VA bill for that task exceeds a stated amount (Ch. 6, pp. 166–170). The book states its own limits: outsourcing is a learned skill the reader is likely to get wrong the first time, marketing is among the hardest things to outsource while support and feature development are the easiest, product architecture is outsourced only for small applications, building your own product is a great option for a founder with no money and plenty of time, solo assistants suit work that is not time-sensitive while recurring critical work reverses toward a team or firm, and finding a VA is trial and error — the author worked through more than six (Ch. 1, pp. 24–25, 38; Ch. 3, pp. 94, 96; Ch. 6, pp. 176, 179). Running several small products one after another is gated behind a stern warning that no second product is built or bought until the first is outsourced and automated as far as possible (Ch. 7, p. 190). And reading business books is named a distraction masquerading as productivity that gets a founder no hour closer to launch (Ch. 1, p. 21).

**Software as a Science** (Martell, Verlaque, Page and Petitpas, 2024). The book declines this theme's question twice over. It says it is not addressed to the choice between bootstrapping and raising: the authors say they are not bashing venture capital, that Martell raised it for two companies and invests as an angel, and that the frameworks hold either way (Introduction, PDF pp. 13, 20). And it says it is not a treatise on how a founder spends their own time, which it sends to Martell's earlier *Buy Back Your Time* (ch. 2, PDF p. 46; One Last Story, PDF p. 248). What remains is a staffing limit inside one chapter: a company adding services is told not to build a second business and not to hire a half dozen people, and to sacrifice services revenue rather than recurring revenue when something must be discounted, with the venture-capital position conceded where services become two-thirds of revenue or the company effectively becomes an agency (ch. 11, PDF pp. 231, 238, 242). Two claims turn on self-funding: paid ads are rarely recommended as a first channel for a self-funded company (ch. 3, PDF p. 74), and selling a year of the current price at a price increase pulls cash forward for one (ch. 10, PDF pp. 225–226). One marketing channel is taken all the way green before another is stacked, and the founder's own voice carries it (ch. 3, PDF pp. 79–83).

**The Lean Startup** (Ries, 2011). No position on working alone, cofounders, equity, or raising money for an independent company; the book defines a startup without reference to size, industry or sector, as a human institution creating a new product or service under conditions of extreme uncertainty (Ch. 2 Define, PDF p. 38). Its nearest claims are about a team inside a larger organization: three structural attributes are named as prerequisites for an internal startup team — scarce but secure resources, because a startup is more sensitive to a mid-course cut than a division is; independent development authority, cross-functional and able to ship without an excess of approvals; and a personal stake in the outcome, which the book says need not be financial, since credit and named ownership work in nonprofits and government (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 244–246). From the same chapter comes the proposal that entrepreneur be an actual job title inside large organizations, held accountable through innovation accounting, and the observation that letting the inventor follow a product through every phase is one reason established companies cannot find creative managers for the next thing (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 254–256).

**The Four Steps to the Epiphany** (Blank, 2013 second edition). Through Customer Discovery and Customer Validation there are no Sales, Marketing or Business Development organizations and, more importantly to the author, no executives carrying those titles, because people default to what they did before and a title tells them what that is (Appendix A, pp. 584, 594–595). He works through each function: a world-class VP of Sales brings hiring, scaling, forecasting and budgeting skills he calls toxic in the first two steps; marketing's demand creation and requirements documents are futile while end users, markets and channels are unknown; and business development has no use in the first two stages at all (Appendix A, pp. 585–591). In their place he puts a Customer Development Team with two roles, Customer Execution and Customer Vision, led by a founder or someone with an equal vote, with up to fifty percent of the product visionary's time spent in front of customers, and the test for any function, title or hire is whether it moves the company closer to knowing the customer's problem, the product's match to it, the sales roadmap, and orders that validate it (Appendix A, pp. 583–584, 596–598). One hire is timed rather than refused: a company may go around the Customer Validation loop once without a sales closer, then hire when it can see where the missing sales skill is stunting progress (Chapter 4, p. 288). On who keeps the company, the author grants that the case for discarding founders is often true, particularly in an existing market where execution is paramount, and then says the jury is out on whether more startups fail from removing founders or from keeping them too long (Chapter 6, pp. 487–488).

**The Startup Owner's Manual** (Blank and Dorf, 2012). The founders do the search work themselves: getting out of the building cannot be assigned to junior staff and must be driven by the founders, repeatedly over weeks or months, and the founders must lead validation as well, because only founders can call a pivot and anyone else who hears of a serious flaw has neither the authority to pivot nor, usually, the nerve to carry the news (Strategy Guide, Ch. 4, PDF p. 97; Ch. 5, PDF p. 122). On money, Manifesto Rule 12 is to preserve all cash while searching and spend only once the model is found, where scalable is defined as one more salesperson or one more marketing dollar returning more gross profit than it cost (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 87–88). Two of the nine deadly sins are staffing and money sins: confusing traditional job titles with a startup's needs, and presumption of success leading to premature scaling; a separate Manifesto rule states that startup job titles differ from a large company's (Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF pp. 41–53; Ch. 3, PDF p. 86). The handoff from search to execution is drawn as a change of who runs the company: IMVU's founders recruited a professional CEO and moved to the board (Strategy Guide, Ch. 2, PDF p. 70). The book prints Fred Wilson's per-stage burn rates and then says there are zillions of exceptions (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 141), and it separates the two sides of a funded company's interests with the line that an investor holds a portfolio of startups while the founder holds a portfolio of one (Web/Mobile, Ch. 4, PDF p. 394).

**Zero to One** (Thiel with Masters, 2014). Choosing whom to start with is the first decision and is likened to marriage: the author's worked case is an investment in a company a friend founded with someone met at a networking event, which failed, and the rule drawn from it is that founders should share a prehistory rather than roll dice (ch. 9, PDF pp. 82–83). Three things are then separated — ownership, who legally holds the equity; possession, who runs the company day to day; and control, who formally governs it — allocated in a typical startup to founders, employees and investors, to managers and employees, and to a board of ideally three, five being the maximum for a private company (ch. 9, PDF pp. 84–86). Everyone involved should be full-time, since anyone without options or a salary is biased toward near-term value, which the author gives as his case against consultants, part-time employees, and even remote work, with outside lawyers and accountants named as the exception (ch. 9, PDF p. 86). On pay, he reports that a company does better the less it pays its chief executive, that no early-stage venture-backed one should pay more than $150,000 a year, and that the founder's own salary sets the ceiling (ch. 9, PDF pp. 86–87). Equity is the compensation that orients people to the future, and he states its problems in the same passage: equal shares seem arbitrary, unequal ones seem unfair, no formula avoids resentment, later employees can matter more than earlier ones who hold more, and most people do not want equity at all — so he advises keeping the allocation secret (ch. 9, PDF pp. 87–88). Recruiting is called a core competency that must never be outsourced; the author's paired rules are that from the outside everyone should be different in the same way, early staff being as personally similar as possible, while from the inside every individual is made responsible for exactly one unique thing (ch. 10, PDF pp. 91–94). The power law reaches the founder's own time: a founder invests by spending her time and an individual by choosing a career, so diversification is the wrong instinct in both cases, and not everyone talented should found a company, since 0.01% of Google was worth more than $35 million at the time of writing while 100% of a failure is nothing (ch. 7, PDF pp. 69–71). The scale of the funding path is given as fewer than 1% of new US businesses each year receiving venture funding, with total venture investment under 0.2% of GDP (ch. 7, PDF p. 69).

**Company of One** (Jarvis, 2019). A company of one is a business that questions growth, resisting some forms of traditional growth not on principle but because growth is often neither the most beneficial nor the most financially viable move, and treating staying small as an end goal rather than a stepping stone; its four traits are said to raise revenue, enjoyment, focus and experience while deliberately refusing to raise payroll, expenses and stress (Prologue, PDF p. 10; ch. 1, PDF pp. 15, 18–27; ch. 2, PDF p. 35). It is not necessarily a one-person business, and it is not a freelancer, because a freelancer trades time for money one-to-one while a company of one profits outside the hours worked (Prologue, PDF p. 10). Investors are named as one of the four reasons companies grow at all, alongside inflation, churn and ego, and the evidence offered is the Startup Genome Project's finding that 74 percent of high-growth tech startup failures came from scaling too quickly, a Kauffman Foundation and *Inc.* follow-up putting almost 86 percent of long-term successes as having taken no venture money, and Paul Graham quoted to the effect that venture firms invest what their own portfolios need rather than what a company needs (ch. 2, PDF pp. 30–31; ch. 4, PDF p. 56). The worked cautionary case is Danielle LaPorte, who took $400,000 from private investors on condition she hire a chief executive and was pushed out of her own eponymous business six months later, then rebuilt alone (ch. 1, PDF p. 18). On staffing, the book prefers contractors on standby to employees, because hiring makes a founder responsible for someone else's mortgage, and the author says he works only with contractors who need no management; where more work arrives, process rather than hiring is offered as the answer, on Hudl's shared style guide and streamlined revision process (ch. 2, PDF p. 38; ch. 3, PDF pp. 45–46). The setup it prescribes is its own legal entity, an LLC in the United States, so liability stops at the business, money out as salary or dividends, and a lawyer on a small retainer as prevention, with an accountant sometimes needed too (ch. 13, PDF pp. 159–161). It concedes several things back: growth is sometimes required where it aligns with the purpose, economies of scale really are necessary in certain markets, venture capital is not as clear-cut as bad, since capital can bring mentorship, connections and business experience, crowdfunding is no sure thing at about 35 percent of Kickstarter campaigns funded, insurance costs more outside an employer or group plan, and a large employer's benefits — not worrying about insurance, administration or expenses — are real (ch. 2, PDF p. 34; ch. 4, PDF pp. 61–62; ch. 8, PDF p. 98; ch. 11, PDF pp. 131–132; ch. 13, PDF p. 163). Its distinctive move is the upper bound: most businesses set a floor on a goal and none on the ceiling, and the author proposes stating both (ch. 2, PDF pp. 39–40).

**Buy Back Your Time** (Martell, 2023). The thesis is that a founder should not hire in order to grow the business but to buy back time, then spend the reclaimed hours only on the two or three tasks he excels at, enjoys, and is paid most for, since about 95 percent of the rest belongs to somebody else (ch. 1, PDF pp. 20, 22). The loop is audit, transfer, fill, repeated, and the sorting tool is the DRIP matrix, which puts administration, billing, invoicing, travel and email in the Delegation quadrant to be transferred as fast as possible and onboarding, selling, marketing and managing in the Replacement quadrant, which the author calls the pitfall because those cost real money to hand over (ch. 1, PDF p. 30; ch. 2, PDF pp. 41, 44–51). The first hire is named flatly: an administrative assistant is nonnegotiable and is the first hire every entrepreneur should make, including a virtual one hired abroad for less than $6 an hour, with the calendar and the inbox as the two responsibilities that define the role (ch. 2, PDF p. 57; ch. 6, PDF pp. 118, 121–124, 129). The arithmetic is the Buyback Rate — what the business pays the founder, including salary, profits and discretionary expenses, divided by 2,000 work hours and then divided by four — and the rule is that no one should perform a task they could outsource at or below it (ch. 2, PDF pp. 55–57; ch. 4, PDF p. 92). Eighty percent done by someone else is stated as a deliberate lowering of the bar (ch. 2, PDF p. 53). Hires then run in a fixed order up a Replacement Ladder — administration, delivery, marketing, sales, leadership — with sales held second-to-last because the founder is usually the best seller and that revenue funds the earlier rungs, and with ownership of a rung required to sit with one named person before the founder can climb (ch. 5, PDF pp. 99–108). The ceiling the ladder exists to beat is the Pain Line, placed at about twelve direct reports and just over $1 million in revenue (ch. 1, PDF p. 25). The book's own exceptions: wearing every hat may be inevitable early and a founder can ride out the first days that way; the 10-80-10 rule keeps the founder's hand on craft he genuinely enjoys; and at $50,000 a year the rate is about $6.25 an hour, which he says may still buy a useful job, with interns and commission-only arrangements named as transfers that cost nothing at all (ch. 1, PDF pp. 32–33; ch. 2, PDF p. 57; ch. 5, PDF pp. 103–104).

**Entrepreneur Revolution** (Priestley, 2024 third edition). The book's premise is that one person, working through a small distributed team, can build a global small business that trades worldwide and earns seven-figure profits from a micro-niche, and that the constraint is not capital, skill, or market conditions but a mindset installed by a school system built for factories (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 13–15, 22, 27–31). Team shapes are taken from the British Army and sized to the stage: a scout team of two, one market-facing and one operational, to test an idea and build a minimum viable product; a fire team of four — a lead, a salesperson, an operator and an administrator — to run a launch or a funding round and find product-market fit; a section of eight; and a platoon of 30 to 50 under a leadership team of three to six, with the claim that above 150 people the organization stops being entrepreneurial and needs governance (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 129–133). The founder is deliberately demoted: entrepreneurship is said to be about the team rather than the founder, founders get too much credit, high-performing teams exist because the risk-taking is shared by collaborators rather than by whoever registered the company, and the author states there is no such thing as a self-made millionaire (Chapter 1, PDF p. 18; Chapter 3, PDF p. 76). The tenth of his ten challenges puts five roles around the table on day one, none of whom need be full-time employees, and the ninth is to meet an accountant and a lawyer before the money arrives, because a wealth structure has to exist before there is wealth (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 73–76). Income follows assets, an asset being anything still valuable if the owner were hit by a bus, and his own case is a business he considered selling for £300k that was valued at £4m twelve months after his team spent a year documenting what it knew (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 137–138). On outside money, the J curve — losing money for three to five years — is called too dependent on capital and an advanced strategy for entrepreneurs who can access capital rather than a bad one, and the core offer's floor of over $1,000 of revenue per customer per year carries the explicit condition unless the business has funding (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 108–111, 120). A lifestyle business with a healthy income is stated to be enough to count as living in the Entrepreneur Revolution (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 91–92), and the partnership maxim is qualified in its own section: a poor-performing staff member can still be fired (Chapter 6, PDF p. 139).

**The Voltage Effect** (List, 2022). A person with a unique skill cannot be cloned, so a business resting on one has a limit on how big it can get, and the author's own family trucking firm is that case; he adds that there are legitimate reasons for staying small or medium-sized (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 69, 74; Conclusion, PDF pp. 216–217). The book is explicit that it is not a theory of the founder: in most cases it is not the who that matters but the what, and if incentives are right character becomes largely irrelevant (Chapter 6, PDF p. 128; Conclusion, PDF p. 217). On staffing at scale, the instruction is to design for average staff, bounded by a non-negotiable — where hardware or a digital interface must be high quality, do not hire mediocre engineers, and only the technicians maintaining it at scale are the negotiable part (Chapter 5, PDF p. 122). Unsustainable costs are one of the five vital signs, and the check he prescribes is to find out not only how many people like the idea but what they are willing to pay and how much it will cost to provide (Chapter 5, PDF p. 111). Optimal quitting is one of the four voltage gains: grit is redefined rather than rejected, since it is not about persisting with something hopeless, and quitting late is said to be preferable to never (Chapter 8, PDF pp. 181, 185). On pay transparency, employees told a peer earned 10 percent more than they thought worked 9.4 percent less over the next ninety days and were likelier to leave, and he predicts sharing lower-level employees' salaries would produce a voltage drop at scale (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 97–98).

**Thinking in Bets** (Duke, 2018). No position on who works on a business, who owns it, whose money is in it, or the first hire. Its nearest claim is about whom a decision-maker recruits to think with rather than to work: a group of three is enough, two to disagree and one to referee, not every friendship has to convert, and the group runs on a charter of accuracy over confirmation, accountability with advance notice, and openness to diverse ideas (ch. 4, PDF pp. 103, 105–106).

## Where the books agree

- No cofounder: Walling's *The SaaS Playbook*, Levels, and Naval all say so, for three different reasons (equity, danger, integrity). Jarvis joins from a fourth direction, staying small as an end goal rather than a stepping stone, and Walling's *Start Small, Stay Small* names the solo shape outright as a Micropreneur with no employees under any circumstance.
- Own the equity and take no permissioned money early: Naval directly; Levels, Walling's *The SaaS Playbook* and Walling's *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding* by silence on raising and by their bootstrapping premise. Walling's *Start Small, Stay Small* makes the silence explicit, skipping the investor pitch by design; Jarvis brings the two numbers, 74 percent of high-growth failures from scaling too quickly and almost 86 percent of long-term successes having taken no venture money; Dunford puts funding on the cost side of two of her three positioning styles, and says a bootstrapper may not want to enter a market that way.
- A hire is a liability before it is leverage: Levels's "liable for their income", Naval's "worst form of leverage", Walling's forgotten employer FICA in *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*, and Jarvis's contractors on standby, because hiring makes a founder responsible for someone else's mortgage.
- Outsource below a rate: Naval's hourly rate and Levels's contractors on standby, joined by Walling's dollarized hour in *Start Small, Stay Small* and Martell's Buyback Rate in *Buy Back Your Time*, which divides the founder's effective hourly rate by four and outsources anything at or below it.
- Delegated work is not held to the owner's standard: Dib's *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* and Martell's *Buy Back Your Time* both set the bar at eighty percent as good as the founder, and both say so as a concession rather than a claim about quality.
- Buy professional advice rather than employ it: Walling's *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding* lists an accountant and a lawyer among the forgotten expenses, Jarvis prescribes a lawyer on a small retainer and sometimes an accountant, Priestley makes meeting both before the money arrives his ninth challenge, and Thiel names outside lawyers and accountants as the one break in his full-time rule.
- The learning work stays with the owner of the business: Fitzpatrick says customer learning cannot be hired out before there is a working business model, Blank's *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* keeps up to half the product visionary's time in front of customers, Blank and Dorf's *The Startup Owner's Manual* says the work cannot be assigned to junior staff because only founders can call a pivot, Dunford says a positioning exercise not driven by the business leader will fail, Laudi and Suellentrop say senior leadership has to lead the framework, and Martell, Verlaque, Page and Petitpas put cold outbound early because it teaches the founder about the customer faster than any other channel.
- Premature scaling rather than competition is where the failure is located: Jarvis's 74 percent of high-growth failures, Blank and Dorf's eighth deadly sin, and List's voltage drop, which he reports takes between 50 and 90 percent of programs when an idea moves to a much larger group.

## Where the books conflict

- **The first hire.** Walling's *The SaaS Playbook* says hire support, and challenges the founder who will not; Levels says avoid hiring and build robots; Naval says labor is the worst leverage; Martell's *Buy Back Your Time* says an administrative assistant is the first hire and calls it nonnegotiable; Blank's *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* admits no Sales, Marketing or Business Development executive in the first two steps at all, and times a sales closer to after one pass around the validation loop, when the missing skill can be seen stunting progress; Jarvis keeps contractors on standby rather than employees. Walling's *The SaaS Playbook* argues from the founders he has advised, Martell from coaching clients and his own calendar, Blank from eight startups; Levels, Naval and Jarvis argue from their own solo practice.
- **Walling against Walling on the first person.** *The SaaS Playbook* challenges a founder to hire at least a support person, while *Start Small, Stay Small*, written thirteen years earlier, names the Micropreneur shape with no employees under any circumstance and answers the same work with a virtual assistant on contract at $3–10 an hour. The read-back for *Start Small, Stay Small* records that the later books restate several of its positions thirteen years on.
- **Build versus outsource.** Levels says build it yourself; Naval says outsource anything below your rate; Walling's *The SaaS Playbook* delegates roles; Walling's *Start Small, Stay Small* drip-outsources tasks and says to outsource graphic design and HTML every time, while naming marketing the hardest thing to outsource and allowing that building your own product is a great option for a founder with no money and plenty of time; Dib's *Lean Marketing* keeps strategy in-house and hires specialist agencies; Dib's *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* sends media technique to hired specialists; Thiel refuses the shape entirely, since consultants and part-time people are biased toward near-term value.
- **Full-time staff or contracted help.** Thiel says everyone involved should be full-time, and names that as his case against consultants, part-time employees, and remote work, with lawyers and accountants excepted. Against it: Jarvis works only with contractors who need no management, Walling's *Start Small, Stay Small* runs on remote contract workers, Priestley puts five roles around the table on day one and says none need be full-time employees, Dib's *Lean Marketing* frequently hires agencies and freelancers, and Miller's *Marketing Made Simple* tells a solo reader to fill the meeting room with outside contractors.
- **Partners.** Naval spends three passages on choosing partners; Walling's *The SaaS Playbook* and Levels say to have none; Thiel calls choosing whom to start with the first and most crucial decision, likens it to marriage, and wants founders who share a prehistory; Priestley says entrepreneurship is about the team rather than the founder and that there is no self-made millionaire.
- **Outside money.** Walling's *Start Small, Stay Small*, Levels, Naval and Jarvis build without it, Jarvis adding that investors are one of the four reasons companies grow at all. Thiel, Blank and Dorf, and Laudi and Suellentrop write from inside the funded world — a board holding control, per-stage burn rates, a professional CEO at the handoff, two companies grown on a seed round. Martell, Verlaque, Page and Petitpas decline the question outright and say their frameworks hold either way. Walling's *Start Small, Stay Small* takes a middle position, that there is nothing wrong with pursuing venture funding and that most readers are simply not positioned for it; Priestley's is that the capital-hungry J curve is an advanced strategy for entrepreneurs who can access capital rather than a bad one. Dunford treats funding as a cost attached to a positioning choice rather than a stance.
- **Equity as compensation.** Thiel offers equity as the thing that orients people to the future, with no fair formula and the allocation kept secret; Walling's *The SaaS Playbook* calls a cofounder the largest equity ever given away and prefers profit sharing in a run-it-forever company; Levels says never to pay a developer in equity for an idea; Naval says own it rather than grant it.
- **Whether one person is a ceiling or a design.** List says a business resting on one uncloneable person has a limit on how big it can get, and Priestley puts the entrepreneurial ceiling at 150 people and the starting team at two. Jarvis and Walling's *Start Small, Stay Small* treat the same ceiling as the goal, Jarvis proposing that a business state an upper bound as well as a floor.
- **Whether the founder or the system carries it.** Thiel's founder's paradox argues a company led by a distinctive individual is more powerful than one led by an interchangeable manager, and Blank and Dorf say only founders can call a pivot. List says in most cases it is not the who but the what, and that with the right incentives character becomes largely irrelevant; Priestley says founders get too much credit; Ries proposes that entrepreneur be an actual job title inside large organizations, held accountable through innovation accounting.
- **Titles early on.** Blank's *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* wants no Sales, Marketing or Business Development executives at all in the first two steps, and Blank and Dorf make confusing traditional job titles with a startup's needs a deadly sin. Martell's *Buy Back Your Time* says the key hire's title does not matter, only the responsibilities; Dib's *Lean Marketing* allows an experienced CMO once there is a large marketing team and real traction; Priestley names a lead, a salesperson, an operator and an administrator in a team of four.
