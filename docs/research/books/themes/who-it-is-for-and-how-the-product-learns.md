# Theme synthesis: who it is for, and how the product learns

## What this file is for

This is the second stage of ADR-0041 for one theme on the values track. It lays out what each of the twenty-nine values books claims about naming the customer, what a persona is worth, and how many people a product has to hear from before its makers know anything; where the books agree; where they conflict; and what the repo has already decided. It contains no ruling. Page citations follow each read-back's own page rule. The read-backs are in `docs/research/books/`. Where an agreement or conflict bullet names Walling without a title, it means the two 2023 books, *The SaaS Playbook* and *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*, which were the only Walling books on the track when the bullet was written; *Start Small, Stay Small* is named by title wherever it is meant. A bare Blank means *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* and Blank and Dorf means *The Startup Owner's Manual*; a bare Miller means *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* and Miller and Peterson means *Marketing Made Simple*; Dib is named by title or as both of his books wherever the two differ.

## The question

Who is the buyer, what do the nine proto-personas count as, and how many real people has the product heard from?

## What each book claims

**Jobs To Be Done** (Ulwick, 2016). Personas built on demographic and psychographic data create phantom targets; segments come from statistically valid research, not observation (ch. 2, p. 52; ch. 4 §IV, pp. 95–96); a segment description is the same thing as an outcome-based persona (ch. 8, pp. 195–196). Asking what job a customer hired the product for is a common mistake; the question is what job they are ultimately trying to get done (ch. 4 §II, p. 87). Managers first agree exactly who the customer is; there are three job executors, and buyer and user can be one person (ch. 4 §I, pp. 83–86; ch. 2, p. 61). A segmentation survey runs on 180 to 3,000 customers, a small one-product company is told to hire the author's firm, and a sprint takes four weeks (ch. 4 §IV, p. 98; ch. 7, pp. 177, 182, 186). A large and a small dairy can share the same unmet needs; demographics nearly always fail to explain them (ch. 5, p. 138; ch. 4 §IV, p. 96).

**The SaaS Playbook** (Walling, 2023). Have conversations with prospects, customers, the people who declined, and the people who canceled; ask open-ended questions; sort feature requests by use case, share of customers, and vision (Market, pp. 44–50).

**Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding** (Walling, 2023 ed.). Working directly with at least two customers is the test of whether the features add value ("Five Reasons You Haven't Launched", p. 88). Ask customers how they found you and what they would use instead; the benefit they name may not be the one marketed ("Four Things I Learned", pp. 150–152).

**Make: The Bootstrapper's Handbook** (Levels). Ideas come from solving your own problems; the founder is the greatest expert at them; talking to customers to find problems is an outside perspective; users who write to the feedback box are usually the best users (Idea, pp. 20–23; Grow, pp. 126–127). Start in a micro niche and widen niche by niche (Idea, pp. 25–28).

**Design for Cognitive Bias** (Thomas, 2020). Users lie: memory is reconstructed from current belief, people are poor reporters of themselves, and Netflix's survey answer lost to its A/B test (ch. 2, pp. 10–12). Nothing about us without us: participatory design's top rung implements what the participants decide, and the people most impacted tend to have the least input (ch. 4, pp. 77, 80–83). Survivorship bias and duty of care: consider the unhappy path as thoroughly as the happy one and design for the most vulnerable first (ch. 4, p. 85; Resources, p. 96).

**Copyhackers: Uplift.** Go back to voice-of-customer research; the winning team spoke to customers about the customer's needs, not the product (Section 2, p. 20; Section 4, p. 35). The One Reader and her stage of awareness decide the page (Section 1, p. 11; Section 5, p. 48).

**Landing Page Hot Tips.** No position on research or personas; its reader is assumed.

**The Almanack of Naval Ravikant** (Jorgenson, 2020). No direct position. Its nearest claim, that a founder escapes competition through authenticity (Building Wealth, pp. 41, 44), sits in the moat theme.

**The Best Interface Is No Interface** (Krishna, 2015). No position on research method. Its chapter 5 argument that screen time is the wrong measure of engagement sits in the measurement theme.

**Obviously Awesome** (Dunford, 2019). The process assumes enough happy customers to see a pattern in who loves the product and why, and where that pattern is not visible the author keeps the positioning loose and tests the product on a deliberately wide audience (Step 1, pp. 61–62), which exempts the pre-customer startup her own introduction addresses (Introduction, p. 19). Target market characteristics are the identifiable traits that make a buyer care a lot, and industry, location, and company size are rarely enough on their own (Components, pp. 48–49; Step 7, pp. 88–89). Narrowness is bounded by the near-term sales objective rather than by a principle: where the best-fit segment cannot hit the number, the definition of best-fit is widened instead, and thirty deals needed against a hundred businesses means the segment is too small (Step 7, p. 90; Step 8, p. 106). A subsegment has to be identifiable enough to build a prospect list from, which is why she sets listing companies with more than 1,000 employees against listing companies that value design (Step 8, pp. 107–108). Asking customers what market a product is in works, she answers her own question, "sometimes", because customers place it in the market nearest their own industry or job (Step 8, pp. 95–96). An opinion of one's own value "does not count as proof", and third-party validation is what counts (Components, pp. 47–48). Positioning is not permanent and is expected to evolve with the best-fit customers, so narrow now is not narrow forever (Step 7, p. 91; Step 8, pp. 106–107).

**Building a StoryBrand 2.0** (Miller, 2025). The customer is the hero and the brand is the guide, and a brand that casts itself as the hero is said to be destined to lose (ch. 3, PDF p. 42; ch. 6, PDF pp. 90–92). One BrandScript carries one desire, and further desires belong in subplots, meaning a BrandScript per division, per product, and optionally per customer segment (ch. 3, PDF p. 52; ch. 4, PDF pp. 62–63). The book's whole account of who a product hears from is that the script and the one-liner are rough drafts, tested on friends, spouse, customers, and strangers and edited until they produce the reaction wanted (ch. 13, PDF p. 172; ch. 15, PDF p. 192), against a bar set by the grunt test, five seconds for a stranger to say what is offered, how it improves their life, and what to do to buy it (ch. 2, PDF p. 38). It takes no position on research method and none on what a persona is worth.

**Lean Marketing** (Dib, 2024). Market comes before product, and a target market stated as women over 40 years old is 1.5 billion people; the niche is cut on location, demography, shared values, industry, desire, problem, or trend (Chapter 2, pp. 34, 36, 46–47). The market need not be huge, only "the right market for you", and the author "wouldn't say you can't be too niche" while telling a reader who thinks the niching is done that "you probably haven't" (Chapter 2, p. 36). Whether two audiences need separate plans turns on whether they are truly different target markets or only segments of one, answered by five questions rather than by a rule (Chapter 2, p. 39), and the unaware "are usually not worth marketing to" (Chapter 2, p. 42). Whether a product is a vitamin or a painkiller is a property of the market and not of the product, a Mercedes-Benz S-Class reading as either depending on the segment (Chapter 3, p. 62). On how a founder learns, the book sends him undercover: a day at an industry conference beats six months of online research, and long-standing advertisers in a trade publication are worth attention because they are likely getting a return (Chapter 2, pp. 49–50). It also endorses doing things that do not scale, naming manual recruitment of users and close work with early customers (Chapter 14, pp. 232–233).

**The 1-Page Marketing Plan** (Dib, 2018). A niche is a tightly defined portion of a subcategory, and targeting everyone means targeting no one (Chapter 1, pp. 49–52). Each candidate segment is rated out of ten on personal fulfillment, value to the marketplace, and profitability, and the highest total wins (Chapter 1, pp. 56–57). The named artifact is an avatar, a detailed composite of the target customer together with every decision maker and influencer beside her (Chapter 1, pp. 59–62), and a reader who does not belong to the target market is told to research it in depth (Chapter 1, p. 58). Niching does not narrow the business: a broad range of services is allowed, each category is a separate campaign, work outside the ideal target market may still be taken, and only the marketing is focused (Chapter 1, pp. 52, 54–55, 57). The author's position on asking is split down the middle — research is called the most neglected part of copywriting and the reason powerful copy fails (Chapter 2, p. 101), while asking the market is not enough on its own, because people do not know what they want until shown it and answer surveys with logic while buying with emotion, so asking is supplemented with observing (Chapter 2, p. 87). A customer with a genuine complaint is called a valuable intelligence asset (Chapter 8, p. 260).

**The Mom Test** (Fitzpatrick, 2013). Customer conversations are bad by default, and the fix is in the questions rather than in the person answering: ask about the other person's life instead of about the idea, ask for specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future, and talk less than you listen (ch. 1, pp. 8, 13). Only the market can say whether an idea is good and everything else is opinion, and opinions are worthless outside an industry expert who has built a very similar business (ch. 1, p. 16; ch. 2, p. 25). The number is small and conditioned: keep talking until you stop hearing new information, which can be as few as three to five conversations for a relatively simple industry and a focused customer segment (ch. 6, p. 89). A good customer segment is a who-where pair, and a group nobody knows how to reach is sliced further until they do (ch. 7, pp. 94–97); a segment can also be broad in a sneaky way, so that twenty conversations turn out to be one conversation each with twenty kinds of customer (ch. 7, pp. 92–93). Stakeholders beside the buyer are counted too, since an app for children is sold to the children and to their parents and a product for a public school can be affected by five constituencies (ch. 7, pp. 97–98). Customer learning is not outsourced or hired out until there is a working business model and a repeatable process, because a hired gun's bad news is hard to assimilate and a lukewarm signal is tempting to gloss over (ch. 8, p. 104). The author states his own limits: where the risk is in the product rather than the market, conversations prove less and building starts earlier and with less certainty (ch. 3, pp. 53–54); friends make acceptable interviewees under the Mom Test as long as the questions are good (ch. 4, p. 56); and talking to customers is called a tool rather than an obligation, to be skipped by a founder it will not help (ch. 8, p. 108).

**Competing Against Luck** (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan, 2016). Jobs Theory is explicitly not a segmentation of customers by their characteristics: it is not about who did something or what they did, but why (ch. 2, PDF p. 41), and a need is not a job, nor is a life theme such as being a good father, because the concept is too abstract to build against without the circumstance (ch. 2, PDF pp. 39–40). Existing research survives the reframing rather than being replaced — personas, ethnography, focus groups, customer panels, and competitive analysis all stay valid as a starting point, used with a different lens, and the five hunting grounds are offered as a sampling rather than a step-by-step manual, because the authors say plainly they do not believe there is any one right way to identify jobs (ch. 4, PDF pp. 73–74). On sample size their position is the inverse of a survey's: insights come from many data points on a small number of people rather than few data points on a large number (ch. 5, PDF p. 99). One interview decides nothing, yielding hypotheses and better questions for the next one because uncovering jobs is clustering rather than a single eureka, and the ambling mattress interview is explicitly not a prototype for all customer research (ch. 5, PDF pp. 100, 111–112). Customers cannot reliably say what they want: the chain that asked how to make its milk shake cheaper, chunkier, chewier, and chocolatier shipped the answers and saw no change in sales (ch. 1, PDF pp. 19–20; ch. 5, PDF pp. 92–93). The interview instead builds a timeline from before the first thought of buying, in the manner of a documentary and with a beginner's mind, asking what happened rather than what the customer would want (ch. 5, PDF pp. 99–100, 101–110). Nonconsumption is often the largest competitor (ch. 3, PDF pp. 56–57), and conveying which customers a product is not for is treated as an obligation rather than an option (ch. 6, PDF p. 131).

**Forget the Funnel** (Laudi and Suellentrop, 2023). The method's premise is that the information a team needs is in one place, inside its best customers' heads, and it says which customers those are: people who have struggled with the problem, pay without hesitation, have an ongoing need, have reached value realization, and started paying three to six months ago (ch. 1, PDF p. 18; ch. 3, PDF pp. 40–41). The instrument is chosen by pool size — a survey at 500 or more ideal customers, interviews below that with 10 to 15 interviewees as the target, and interviews again for a team with resources for only one method (ch. 3, PDF pp. 43–44). Research built from assumption rather than from customers answers the wrong questions, and a product team believing it is its own customer is treated as an objection to answer rather than a license (ch. 3, PDF p. 39); demographic and product-usage data are not called worthless, only aimed at different questions, and demographics return in exactly one place, the criterion about whether a group congregates in ways a team can target (ch. 3, PDF p. 39; ch. 5, PDF p. 67). Customers are granted not to be product experts, and the method confines itself to the psychology under the answer (ch. 3, PDF p. 41). Where there are no customers to learn from, the authors offer audience research — mining reviews of competing solutions, listening in forums and communities, studying how the audience describes its pain — and rank it below customer research themselves, since not everyone in an audience is an ideal customer, so its output is hypotheses to prove or disprove rather than a path to take (ch. 4, PDF pp. 47–50). The words a customer arrives with are the words to use, and open-ended questions are the only way to collect them (ch. 3, PDF pp. 41–42; ch. 4, PDF p. 52).

**Marketing Made Simple** (Miller with Peterson, 2020). The book takes no position on research method and none on what a persona is worth; its nearest claim to this theme's question is that the funnel's audience is segmented, with a lead generator and an email campaign written per segment (ch. 6, pp. 109–110; ch. 8, p. 143). Its own evidence about customers is secondhand — StoryBrand's work with more than ten thousand businesses, a survey of StoryBrand customers by an unnamed independent research company reporting a correlation between checklist implementation and growth, and Peterson's doctoral dissertation (Introduction, p. 9; ch. 3, pp. 36–37) — and the one place it has a customer speak, the testimonial, allows the text to be drafted for the customer, states plainly that this is not making something up, and sends it back for approval (ch. 5, pp. 86–87).

**This Is Marketing** (Godin, 2018). Two questions govern every decision, who is it for and what is it for (Ch. 3, PDF p. 27), and the answer to the first is the smallest viable market, the minimum number of people worth influencing, with mass rejected because mass means the center of the curve (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 34–35). People are chosen by what they dream, believe, and want rather than by what they look like, and the author names worldviews and personas as the shortcut for doing it (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 33–34), while granting that grouping people into stereotyped clumps works often rather than always (Ch. 2, PDF p. 22). The size is put in numbers through Kevin Kelly's true fan, roughly a thousand of whom support a living but not a fortune, with the author's gloss that the hard part is the micro market and that people bend themselves for anonymous masses before fifty or a hundred people would miss them (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 80–81); traction is tested by how many people outside headquarters use the thing daily, how many insist their friends use it, and who would miss it if it were gone (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 79–80). Saying it is not for you is offered as respect rather than rejection, and a person who understands the offer and declines is not a failure of the marketing, only somebody it is not for at that price or with that structure (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 36, 38–39; Ch. 5, PDF p. 43); the closing exercise asks why the people who do not choose you are right, on the argument that people decide rationally given what they see, believe, and want (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 85–86). On asking, his position is flat: asking people what they want will not produce a breakthrough, because they are terrible at inventing new ways to meet a want they already know (Ch. 7, PDF p. 69), and seeking feedback is called asking to be proven wrong, with advice offered as the substitute, phrased as asking how the work could fit the other person's worldview more closely (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 84–85).

**Start Small, Stay Small** (Walling, 2010). The Market First Approach inverts the usual order by finding a small online niche before any code is written (Ch. 2, p. 39). A vertical niche beats a horizontal market, and a warm niche, one the founder already has an association with, beats a cold one, though a cold niche can be entered by working a network to warm it, with the author calling the chances without an insider dubious at best (Ch. 2, pp. 45–51, 62–65). Size is read off a dedicated publication and a handful of websites, with wariness below about 10,000 members, and the test does not run backwards, since a niche with no magazine devoted to it is not necessarily too small (Ch. 2, pp. 57–60). The human step is a single call: grill your niche contact on what pains her in the job that software could solve (Ch. 2, p. 72). The customer profile is imagined rather than gathered, by picturing the ideal customer's life, arrival, frustrations, and jargon (Ch. 4, pp. 119–120). The author limits his own instruments at length: keyword tools are flawed estimates extrapolated from other data and most of the free difficulty tools are junk, search queries are only one way to gauge demand and a survey of an existing audience is better where one exists, the Mini Sales Site cannot give complete certainty either way and does not work for anything resting on a network effect, and marketing is called fuzzy and imprecise with a leap of faith at the end of it (Ch. 2, pp. 51, 71, 75–76, 83, 86, 90).

**Software as a Science** (Martell, Verlaque, Page, and Petitpas, 2024). The book says little about who the buyer is, treating every SaaS company as the same four numbers whatever it sells and to whom (ch. 1, PDF pp. 30–31). On how a company learns it is direct: cold outbound is worth running early because it teaches the founder about the customer faster than any other channel, and those one-to-one conversations are what reveal who the buyer is (ch. 3, PDF p. 75; One Last Story, PDF p. 246). The counterpart claim is that written self-report teaches nothing, so a free-text cancellation reason box is dismissed and a call put in its place (ch. 7, PDF p. 163), and that customers are poor storytellers who have to be coached, with only the happiest and most engaged ever completing the value chain (ch. 8, PDF pp. 179–180). The authors say flatly that their own customer health index will be wrong, point at HubSpot revising its criteria every four to six months, and tell a reader to start from logins or a guess and iterate (ch. 7, PDF pp. 154, 157, 165–166); they also note that calling a few dozen unengaged accounts surfaces people who had been meaning to cancel (ch. 7, PDF p. 163).

**The Lean Startup** (Ries, 2011). The customer archetype is a hypothesis rather than a fact, and the customer profile stays provisional until validated learning shows the type can be served sustainably (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF pp. 92–93). The facts that would settle it exist only outside the building, and Scott Cook's two phone books are offered as the minimum version of going to get them (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF pp. 89–91). Learning is called the oldest excuse for a failure of execution, since anybody who fails can tell a compelling story about what was learned, and the counter offered is empirical data from real customers (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF pp. 46, 57), with metrics held to being testable by hand against real customers and drawn from the master data rather than an intermediate system (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 143–144). What pulls the work is not a stated want — the book names as a misunderstanding the reading that customers pull product development, because customers often do not know what they want — but the hypothesis, expressed as an experiment (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF p. 195). Not knowing who the customer is means not knowing what quality is, so an early product is judged against learning rather than against a professional standard (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 108–110). Inside an innovation sandbox the customers are treated as real, and the team is allowed to attempt a long-term relationship with them rather than running a concept test (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF p. 252).

**The Four Steps to the Epiphany** (Blank, 2013). The greatest cause of failure is that startups lack customers and a proven financial model rather than a product (Chapter 1, p. 34), and the remedy puts numbers on the search: the facts live outside the building, the prospect list starts at fifty names, and fifty follow-up calls yield five to ten visits (Chapter 3, pp. 110, 180, 182, 186). Verifying the problem assumes ten or twenty customers have been talked to, more where the loop has run more than once, and a startup is meant to try out several markets and users rather than one (Chapter 3, pp. 224, 230). What those conversations are not is stated as carefully as what they are: the first product specification comes from the founders' vision, Customer Discovery is neither a collection of feature lists from prospects nor a focus group (Chapter 3, pp. 79–80, 111), and the problem presentation is built to elicit information rather than to convince a customer they have the problem described (Chapter 3, pp. 187–189). Who counts is defined narrowly — the customer brief separates end users, influencers, recommenders, the economic buyer, and the decision-maker, and the author says the same hierarchy exists in a consumer sale under more familiar titles (Chapter 3, pp. 140–145); the word customer is reserved for people who pay and giveaways do not prove customers will buy (Chapter 4, pp. 299, 301); and alpha and beta testers are not customers at all, though they can be influential recommenders (Chapter 4, pp. 299–301). Consumer categories that address no problem, clothing, fashion, and entertainment among them, get a different hypothesis, about emotional wants and the justification a buyer gives themselves (Chapter 3, p. 145). The author names a method more rigorous and disciplined than his own, Eric von Hippel's four-step lead-user process (Bibliography, p. 556).

**The Startup Owner's Manual** (Blank and Dorf, 2012). The first of the nine deadly sins is the assumption that the founders know what the customer wants, and the Customer Development Manifesto opens on the rule that there are no facts inside the building (Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF pp. 41–53; Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 71–87). The archetype is built from what customers say and kept updated as more is learned (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 258–259). Getting out of the building cannot be assigned to junior staff and is driven by the founders, repeatedly over weeks or months (Strategy Guide, Ch. 4, PDF p. 97). The people who matter first are earlyvangelists, a small subset of visionary buyers who have the problem, know they have it, and have cobbled together a makeshift solution, and failure to sell to them is treated as decisive (Strategy Guide, Ch. 4, PDF pp. 99–102; Strategy Guide, Ch. 5, PDF p. 123). The early tests look for a strong enough signal rather than a rigorous one, the authors' own example being five of the first twelve customers called on, and they need not be built from real code, hardware, or product, since a mock-up, a demo, or a physical prototype is enough (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 79–80). Two limits are stated on the data that comes back: the process is not a giant focus group, a great entrepreneur may weigh all of it, listen to instinct, and explain why the feedback is being ignored, and in a new market there is no data at all (Strategy Guide, Ch. 5, PDF p. 124); and velocity is not always the goal, since a niche vertical site or a high-value product may need only a few high-value customers (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420).

**Zero to One** (Thiel with Masters, 2014). The perfect target market is a small group of particular people concentrated together and served by few or no competitors, and a plan that talks about winning 1 percent of a huge market is named a red flag (ch. 5, PDF pp. 41–42). Small is not the same as nonexistent, and the author's own PalmPilot failure is the bound he puts on his own rule: the users were scattered, had little in common, and used their devices episodically, so nobody needed the product (ch. 5, PDF p. 42). Understating the scale of competition is called the biggest mistake a startup can make, and the non-monopolist's habit is to define a market as the intersection of smaller ones (ch. 3, PDF pp. 23, 25). His position on learning from people is the sharpest on the track: definite planning means not designing by focus-group feedback, and LinkedIn is praised for not polling recruiters for discrete pain points before building (ch. 6, PDF p. 60; ch. 12, PDF p. 112). The epistemic limit is stated in the same chapter: companies are not experiments, every company starts once, and statistics does not work at a sample size of one (ch. 6, PDF pp. 46–47).

**Company of One** (Jarvis, 2019). The audience is chosen by exclusion: pick a niche and proudly exclude people, because a message aimed at everyone reaches nobody in particular (ch. 6, PDF pp. 81, 84; ch. 10, PDF pp. 121–122), and polarization is granted an effect that is not always bad, with placation named as the first of three responses and a move toward the people who dislike the product rather than away from them (ch. 6, PDF p. 82). On learning, direct one-to-one communication with customers is called always required, to learn, empathize, adapt, and revise, and collaboration is the one thing the book tells a company of one to scale down rather than up (ch. 8, PDF pp. 101, 105). The case for listening rests on Eric von Hippel's research putting more than 60 percent of profitable innovations with customers, and on 3M's Medical-Surgical division averaging $146 million of revenue from lead-user ideas against $18 million from internal ones (ch. 7, PDF p. 90); a company that does not listen to a complaint is said to pay for it, on Ruby Newell-Legner's figures that only 4 percent of dissatisfied customers complain and 91 percent simply never return (ch. 7, PDF pp. 85–86, 95). Against that, followers, subscribers, and clicks are counted as vanity metrics that show how many people took the marketing bait rather than measuring engagement or trust (ch. 4, PDF p. 60; ch. 10, PDF pp. 121–122), and word of mouth is granted to be hard to measure, since a recommendation can happen in a coffee shop or an untrackable private message (ch. 10, PDF p. 118).

**Buy Back Your Time** (Martell, 2023). No position on who the buyer is, on what a persona counts as, or on how a product learns from customers; the book is about a founder's own calendar and payroll, and its nearest claim, the Buyback Principle that a founder hires to buy back time rather than to grow the business (ch. 1, PDF pp. 20, 22), sits in the founder-time theme.

**Entrepreneur Revolution** (Priestley, 2024). The buyer is chosen twice over: sell to a business rather than a consumer, because lifetime value is higher and a business is incentivized to spend rather than pay tax on profit, and inside an audience talk to the minority who can spend rather than the larger crowd who cannot (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 92–93, 96). Market size is called mostly irrelevant, with the author's own counter-case that some markets are huge and still very unlikely to be entered, a new luxury watch brand and a burger restaurant chain being his examples (Chapter 4, PDF p. 94). The numbers he attaches to learning are the largest on the track: the prescribed artifact is a brochure built before anything expensive, from a survey of 150 people in the audience, interviews with at least 30 of them, a compiled specification list, and a return to 10 to 30 of them with basic diagrams (Chapter 4, PDF p. 99). Testing whether the audience exists at all comes first, by survey, quiz, introduction event, mock-up brochure, or waiting list (Chapter 4, PDF p. 95; Chapter 6, PDF pp. 144–147), and he sizes it: roughly 1 in 20 of an audience buys at once, so an audience has to exceed 200 people for every 10 sales wanted at launch, against a lead-to-sale rate he puts under 5 percent across the industries he sees (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 96, 104). Selling is founder-led for the same learning reason — he sold ScoreApp face to face to well over 1,000 business owners, and says the meetings produced the language, the objections, and the product changes that observing from a website would not have (Chapter 4, PDF p. 101).

**The Voltage Effect** (List, 2022). The second of the five vital signs is misjudging how representative the initial population is, and the failure has a name, selection bias: people who opt in to a pilot are the ones most likely to benefit from it (Introduction, PDF pp. 19–20; Chapter 2, PDF pp. 55, 57–58). His examples are all a group failing to stand for a larger one — McDonald's Arch Deluxe, which focus groups liked and customers did not; an iron-fortified-salt program tested only in adolescent women that did not reduce anemia in the general population; Opower's energy-comparison letters, which ran in 111 randomized controlled trials across 8.6 million households and still failed to generalize because utilities in environmentalist areas were likelier to take part; and Chicago Heights, where an average gain turned out to hold only for Hispanic families (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 57–61). The counter-case he offers is the Nurse-Family Partnership, piloted by David Olds in Memphis, Denver, and Elmira for demographic variation, whose results replicated (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 59–60). A ceiling is not itself a failure: Pinterest's female-majority user base and dating apps' irrelevance to people in committed monogamous relationships are given as real limits an idea can still earn money inside (Chapter 2, PDF p. 55). On who does the testing, the person with an idea is not its only tester and independent replication is called as crucial for a business as for a scientist, with caution rather than speed as the stated fallback where three or four replications are not feasible (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 38, 44). And a perfect customer cannot be imagined: the author says it would have been impossible to guess how customers misused the Opower thermostat without testing it on real users (Chapter 3, PDF p. 83).

**Thinking in Bets** (Duke, 2018). The book takes no position on who a buyer is or on what a persona counts as, and its position on how much a small sample supports is epistemic rather than commercial: a single result carrying hidden information and luck supports very little, and the correct answer about four coin flips is that one is not sure (ch. 1, PDF pp. 28, 34). A belief carries a stated level of confidence and is recalibrated as information arrives rather than being reversed wholesale from right to wrong (ch. 2, PDF pp. 62–63), and a wide range beats none, because a guess between 20 percent and 80 percent is better than not guessing at all and a number somebody hesitates to guess at is already being guessed at (ch. 6, PDF p. 166). On the mechanics of asking, beliefs are contagious, so a person seeking a group's opinion withholds their own conclusion, since telling listeners what the asker believes creates an ideological conflict of interest (ch. 5, PDF p. 134; ch. 6, PDF p. 163), and outcome blindness has its own limit, since in poker results arrive within seconds of the decision, which is why the workaround is to omit the ending when asking for advice (ch. 5, PDF pp. 133–134). Experience still counts: an experienced player makes a better guess than a novice, and what neither gets is knowledge of how the next flip lands (ch. 1, PDF pp. 30–31).

## Where the books agree

- A persona built from assumptions is a hypothesis, not evidence: Ulwick says so directly; Thomas's "users lie" and Copyhackers's voice-of-customer both reach for observed behavior or the customer's own words over a constructed profile. Ries states it as a rule — the customer archetype is a hypothesis rather than a fact, and the profile stays provisional until validated learning shows the type can be served sustainably — Blank and Dorf keep the archetype but build it from what customers say and keep updating it, and Laudi and Suellentrop put the same point negatively, that research built from assumption answers the wrong questions.
- Talk to real people, including the ones who said no: Walling (all three books), Copyhackers, Thomas. The new books make this the widest agreement on the track — Fitzpatrick, Blank, Blank and Dorf, Laudi and Suellentrop, Christensen and his coauthors, Priestley, Jarvis, Martell and his coauthors, and Dib in both of his books all send the reader to the people themselves, and Ries states the ground for it, that the facts exist only outside the building.
- Name the customer before anything else: Ulwick's job executors, Copyhackers's One Reader, Levels's micro niche. Godin's smallest viable market, Thiel's small concentrated group, Walling's warm niche, Dib's niche in both books, Jarvis's proudly excluded audience, and Priestley's business-rather-than-consumer buyer all put the same step first, and Ries gives the consequence of skipping it: not knowing who the customer is means not knowing what quality is.
- A stated want is not a finding. Christensen's milk shake chain shipped every survey answer and saw no change in sales; Godin says people are terrible at inventing new ways to meet a want they already know; Ries names as a misunderstanding the reading that customers pull product development; Dib says surveys are answered with logic while the buying is done with emotion, so asking is supplemented with observing; Laudi and Suellentrop grant that customers are not product experts and confine the method to the psychology under the answer; Thiel refuses the focus group outright; and Martell and his coauthors dismiss the free-text cancellation box. They reach Thomas's "users lie" from a different direction: not that the person is unreliable, but that the question was the wrong one.
- The asking is the founder's own job. Fitzpatrick says customer learning cannot be outsourced or hired out until there is a working business model and a repeatable process; Blank and Dorf say getting out of the building cannot be assigned to junior staff and is driven by the founders over weeks or months; Priestley sold face to face to well over 1,000 business owners on the ground that the meetings produced what a website could not; Martell and his coauthors say cold outbound teaches the founder about the customer faster than any other channel. Ulwick is the one voice on the other side, telling a small one-product company to hire the author's firm.
- The first group heard from is not yet the population. List names misjudging representativeness as one of five vital signs and selection bias as its mechanism, since the people who opt in to a pilot are the ones most likely to benefit from it; Blank says a startup is meant to try out several markets and users rather than one; Fitzpatrick warns that a segment can be broad in a sneaky way; Laudi and Suellentrop rank audience research below customer research because not everyone in an audience is an ideal customer; Duke puts the general form of it, that a single result carrying hidden information and luck supports very little.

## Where the books conflict

- **How many.** Walling's test is two customers; Ulwick's survey is 180 to 3,000; Levels says the founder's own problem is enough. The three answers differ by three orders of magnitude, and the twenty new books spread the question wider rather than settling it: Fitzpatrick says three to five for a relatively simple industry and a focused segment, Blank and Dorf five of the first twelve customers called on, Blank ten or twenty off a list of fifty names, Laudi and Suellentrop 10 to 15 interviews below 500 ideal customers and a survey above that, Priestley 150 surveyed and at least 30 interviewed, and Christensen and his coauthors refuse the axis entirely, asking for many data points on a small number of people rather than few on a large number. Godin puts a fourth figure on the other end of the same axis, Kevin Kelly's roughly a thousand true fans, with his own gloss that people bend themselves for anonymous masses before fifty or a hundred people would miss them. Miller's answer, for a different artifact, is friends, spouse, customers, and strangers.
- **Whose problem.** Levels's founder-as-expert assumes the founder is the customer; Ulwick and Thomas assume the founder is not and warn against the founder's own reconstruction. Walling's 2010 customer profile, imagined from the ideal customer's life, arrival, frustrations, and jargon, and Dib's avatar sit with Levels; Blank's first deadly sin, that the founders assume they know what the customer wants, and List's finding that a perfect customer cannot be imagined sit with Ulwick and Thomas.
- **What an interview is worth.** Thomas's "users lie" undercuts the interviews Walling and Copyhackers rely on; Ulwick answers with a structured survey that a pre-launch company cannot run. Thiel takes the strongest version of the doubt, praising LinkedIn for not polling recruiters for pain points at all; Godin agrees the answers will not contain the breakthrough and calls seeking feedback asking to be proven wrong, substituting advice for it; Christensen and his coauthors say one interview yields hypotheses and better questions rather than a conclusion. Fitzpatrick and Blank answer neither with a survey nor with silence but by changing the question — specifics in the past rather than opinions about the future, and a presentation built to elicit rather than to convince.
- **Evidence.** Ulwick's method is his firm's product; Walling's two-customer rule is his own experience; Thomas cites Netflix's published test. List is the only author on the track whose claims rest on randomized field experiments, 111 of them in the Opower case alone; Christensen and his coauthors offer two decades of case work and say plainly they had no dataset of the kind that produced the theory of disruption; Miller and Peterson cite a survey by an unnamed independent research company and a dissertation; Thiel says statistics does not work at a sample size of one and offers none; Priestley, Jarvis, Martell, Dunford, Dib, and Walling rest on the author's own practice.
- **Whether a persona is a legitimate instrument.** Ulwick calls demographic and psychographic personas phantom targets. Godin names worldviews and personas as the shortcut for choosing people by what they dream and believe. Christensen and his coauthors keep personas, ethnography, focus groups, and panels valid as a starting point under a different lens, while saying the theory itself is not about who a customer is. Dib's avatar and Walling's imagined customer profile are constructed rather than gathered. Ries and Blank and Dorf keep the instrument and demote it, to a hypothesis that stays provisional and an archetype updated from what customers say.
- **How narrow, and what bounds it.** Godin's smallest viable market and his fifty or a hundred people who would miss the work, Thiel's small concentrated group, Jarvis's proudly excluded audience, Walling's warm niche, and Dib's instruction that a reader who thinks the niching is done probably has not, all push the definition inward with no stated floor. Dunford puts a floor on it from the business side — narrowness is bounded by the near-term sales objective, and a best-fit segment that cannot hit the number is widened instead — and List puts one on it from the evidence side, since the narrower the initial population the less it represents the population at scale. Thiel supplies the failure case for going too narrow himself: the PalmPilot's users were scattered and episodic, so nobody needed the product.
