# Theme synthesis: reaching the market

## What this file is for

This is the second stage of ADR-0041 for one theme on the values track. It lays out what each of the twenty-nine values books claims about how a product like this one finds its first buyers, what a founder spends, where the founder shows up, and what a landing page says; where the books agree; where they conflict; and what the repo has already decided. It contains no ruling. Page citations follow each read-back's own page rule. The read-backs are in `docs/research/books/`. Where an agreement or conflict bullet names Walling without a title, it means the two 2023 books, *The SaaS Playbook* and *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*, which were the only Walling books on the track when the bullet was written; *Start Small, Stay Small* is named by title wherever it is meant. A bare Blank means *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* and Blank and Dorf means *The Startup Owner's Manual*; a bare Miller means *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* and Miller and Peterson means *Marketing Made Simple*; Dib is named by title or as both of his books wherever the two differ.

## The question

Where does the founder go, what does the money buy, and does the January site have to be found or only arrived at?

## What each book claims

**Make: The Bootstrapper's Handbook** (Levels). Launch on Product Hunt, Hacker News, and Reddit, against the better point that a launch belongs where the customers are; reach the niche's trade magazine; build a personal press list (Launch, pp. 83, 99–101, 107). Keep launching; side-project marketing launches mini-apps as separate products (pp. 108–110; Grow, pp. 117–118). Grow organically; paid traffic only after traction at 100,000 monthly active users; do not hire growth hackers (Grow, pp. 115–117). Tell your own story and build in public; showing mistakes makes a founder look human (pp. 118–121; This book, p. 17). Fill Open Graph and Twitter Card tags and generate a share image per page (pp. 121–123).

**The Almanack of Naval Ravikant** (Jorgenson, 2020). Business networking is a complete waste of time; a maker of something interesting is found by the right people (Building Wealth, pp. 85–86). Learn both to sell and to build; selling includes marketing, recruiting, and PR (pp. 32, 63–64). Media is permissionless leverage; a brand is built on Twitter and YouTube and by giving away free work (pp. 35, 57, 60).

**The SaaS Playbook** (Walling, 2023). High-touch funnels need $500 a month or more in price; low-touch funnels are a volume game (Marketing, pp. 90–93). In-person events pay only if the return covers the cost: one enterprise deal may, twenty new small customers means raise prices or skip events (pp. 99–100). Score approaches on speed, cost, and scalability, prioritize with ICE, run one fast and one slow channel, keep a marketing changelog, measure every channel (pp. 102–106).

**Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding** (Walling, 2023 ed.). Traffic quality decides conversion; a mailing list converts up to ten times website traffic; measure every source and drop the ones that do not convert ("Nine Levels", pp. 18–21). An automated follow-up sequence converts a subscriber ("#1 Goal", pp. 27–29).

**Jobs To Be Done** (Ulwick, 2016). Buyers searching online rarely start with a product name; they enter a job step or a desired outcome, so campaigns and search are built on those words (ch. 4 §IX, pp. 115–116).

**Copyhackers: Uplift.** The One Reader and her stage of awareness decide the design and the copy's length; the more aware the reader, the less copy (Section 1, p. 11; Section 5, p. 48). A trusted-by row and social proof made a claim more believable; the losing page had no persuasive elements to build trust (Section 2, pp. 21–22). Voice-of-customer research, about the customer's needs rather than the product, is where the winning copy came from (Section 2, p. 20; Section 4, p. 35).

**Landing Page Hot Tips.** Social proof, founding year, usage figures, and the best testimonial above the fold (Tips #52, #82, #83; PDF pp. 100, 153, 154–155). A free teaser and no obstacles to a demo (Tips #40, #46; PDF pp. 83, 91). The first question a SaaS visitor asks is the price (Tip #85, PDF p. 157).

**Design for Cognitive Bias** (Thomas, 2020). Online advertising is the rat king of poor incentives: a market of something like $280 billion that loses advertisers sixty-three cents on the dollar; eBay believed its brand-keyword ads returned $245.6 million and was losing $20 million, shown when the ads were paused for three months and search traffic held; Bing's ad spend fell 10 percent for a while and then the market grew, which the author reads as sunk cost plus poor incentives (ch. 3, pp. 45–47). Its claims about what a page may do to a reader sit in the persuasion theme.

**The Best Interface Is No Interface** (Krishna, 2015). No position.

**Obviously Awesome** (Dunford, 2019). A subsegment is only worth choosing if it is identifiable enough to build a prospect list from, which she contrasts with a list of companies that value design (Step 8, pp. 107–108). Word-of-mouth marketing happens most naturally in tight market subsegments, which is the Big Fish, Small Pond case (Step 8, pp. 104–106). Head to Head is rarely a good choice for a small business launching a product, and taking the lead in an uncrowded but understood category often needs outside funding, which she says may not be how a bootstrapper wants to enter the market; Create a New Game generally requires deep-pocketed, patient investors (Step 8, pp. 99–101, 115). The segment test is arithmetic: thirty deals needed against a hundred businesses in the segment means the segment is too small (Step 8, p. 106). Messaging follows positioning rather than preceding it, with a sales story between them, and a master messaging document keeps later copy from drifting (After Positioning, pp. 129–132). Asking customers what market a product is in works only sometimes, because they place it in the market nearest their own industry or job (Step 8, pp. 95–96).

**Building a StoryBrand 2.0** (Miller, 2025). The grunt test is his first measure of a site: whether, within five seconds of looking at it, a person can say what is offered, how it will make their life better, and what to do to buy it (ch. 2, PDF p. 38). The header carries a large-text offer above the fold with a direct call to action and a transitional one beside it, and the offer is not buried in a paragraph (ch. 14, PDF pp. 176–178; ch. 15, PDF pp. 192–193). Very few words: a paragraph above the fold is passed over, some of the most effective sites use ten sentences or fewer, and long text goes behind a read-more link (ch. 14, PDF pp. 182–183). The campaign is a lead generator that exchanges a resource for an email address, followed by an automated drip of three nurture emails and then a sales email, repeated month after month, with some brands never stopping (ch. 15, PDF pp. 198–205). He calls a 20 percent open rate the industry standard, says a deleted email still accomplishes the goal because the sender is branding itself into the reader's universe, and calls an unsubscribe a good thing (ch. 15, PDF p. 204). The seventh campaign step is a referral system: a coupon code, a shareable tool, a reward, or an affiliate program at 10 percent commission (ch. 15, PDF pp. 211–214). The wireframe is adjustable, and a reader may add to or subtract from the eight-section landing page (ch. 15, PDF p. 198).

**Lean Marketing** (Dib, 2024). Go undercover: a day at an industry conference beats six months of online research, and long-standing advertisers in a trade publication are worth paying attention to because they are likely getting a return (Chapter 2, pp. 49–50). In-person presence costs money and the trade-show route is one line in a budget, while flagship experiences are an advanced strategy with little connection to any return on investment, so a taster, trial, or sample is a better use of resources early on (Chapter 2, p. 50; Chapter 8, p. 139). On social media you are a tenant who can be deplatformed, shadow-banned, or evicted at any time, so an audience is moved to an owned asset, and the instruction is to choose one platform to start (Chapter 13, pp. 212, 216). Search behavior is specific rather than generic — a searcher did not type "doctor" or "car" into the box (Chapter 2, p. 38). The hero section says what you have got, how it makes the reader's life better, and what to do next, and a products page carries pricing or a price range (Chapter 9, pp. 150, 152–153). A landing page collects only what is essential, because each additional field requested reduces completion, with a multi-step form as the compromise (Chapter 8, p. 141; Chapter 9, pp. 148–149). Email runs once a week at minimum, because a list below that decays into unsubscribes and spam complaints (Chapter 12, pp. 199–200). The attack on agencies is on generalist agencies and on abdication rather than on outside help: specialist agencies are worth their weight in gold, and experts are recommended for setting up digital advertising (Chapter 11, pp. 180–181; Chapter 13, pp. 225–226).

**The 1-Page Marketing Plan** (Dib, 2018). Hold at least five sources of new leads, because one of anything is a single point of failure (Chapter 3, pp. 130–132). Own your marketing assets, and prefer a list you own to an audience on a platform that can change its rules (Chapter 3, pp. 119, 122). Track every channel's return, because not measuring is the mark of an amateur, and response and conversion rates are said to be useless in themselves (Chapter 3, pp. 111, 114, 128). He declines to name a good response or open rate, calling it a nonsense question because sometimes 50 percent is a disaster and sometimes 0.01 percent is a success (Chapter 3, p. 112). There is no advertising budget except in the testing phase, where the instruction is to fail often and fail cheap, and the whole argument applies only to marketing that is measured and working, while unmeasured or failing marketing is told to stop (Chapter 3, pp. 128–129). He is not against social media, states he has used it in multiple businesses, and limits the objection to its being a medium rather than a strategy and to the profile not being an asset you own (Chapter 3, pp. 117–119). Never sell from the ad: capture interested people into a database and nurture them, because only about 3 percent are ready to buy now (Chapter 4, pp. 140–143). Email frequency has no rule — at least monthly, best practice closer to weekly, some marketers daily — and it depends on the target market (Chapter 3, p. 123). Media technique is out of scope and sent to hired specialists (Chapter 3, p. 112). Brand awareness follows sales rather than being bought with awareness campaigns before them (Chapter 9, pp. 280–282).

**The Mom Test** (Fitzpatrick, 2013). The routes to finding people are bringing them to you and going to them: organize the meetup, immerse yourself where they already are, and find an excuse to talk about a topic you both care about (ch. 6, pp. 77–79). Cold approaches are called a different beast and much more of a gamble than the warm-introduction method the book is written for, and calling in old keep-me-in-the-loop offers carries the caveat that he would not make a habit of it because it is annoying and can burn bridges (ch. 6, pp. 82, 85). After arguing against phone calls he records that others in the field like and recommend them, says to use whatever works, and narrows his own claim to starting in person (ch. 6, p. 87). A good customer segment is a who-where pair, and a group nobody knows how to reach is sliced further until they do (ch. 7, pp. 94–97). On landing pages he is skeptical of the quantitative validation: what convinced Buffer's founder was not the metrics or the conversion rate but the conversations that followed from emailing every single person who signed up (ch. 6, p. 78). The number of conversations needed is three to five for a relatively simple industry and a focused segment, and the opportunity cost of meetings is called low for a sales-driven business where the conversations double as leads (ch. 6, p. 89).

**Competing Against Luck** (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan, 2016). A purpose brand is one that has become synonymous with a job; it is built by doing the job rather than by advertising, and a brand that fails to integrate around a job becomes a category placeholder competing on price (ch. 6, PDF pp. 132–134). Nonconsumption is often the largest competitor, so a market defined against it is much bigger than the category suggests (ch. 3, PDF pp. 56–57; ch. 4, PDF pp. 76–78; ch. 6, PDF p. 125). The competitive set is drawn by the job and not by the category — margarine competes with Teflon and olive oil from two other aisles (ch. 1, PDF pp. 21–25; ch. 2, PDF p. 45). Reviews cut both ways, because the same mechanism that helps a good product get hired lets a customer who hired it for the wrong job damage it, which is why conveying who should not hire a product is treated as an obligation rather than an option (ch. 6, PDF p. 131). Products are copyable and experiences integrated into processes are not; Toyota, Pixar, and Southern New Hampshire University all opened their doors and were not matched (ch. 6, PDF p. 121; ch. 7, PDF pp. 140–142).

**Forget the Funnel** (Laudi and Suellentrop, 2023). The authors report their own cases, Unbounce and Calendly, as each growing sharply in two years on a seed round and without paid acquisition (ch. 1, PDF pp. 17, 21). Where there are no customers to learn from, audience research substitutes: mine reviews of competing solutions, listen in forums and communities, and study how the audience describes its pain — and they call those options labor intensive and in need of practice, with junk responses normal and the output hypotheses rather than a path to take (ch. 4, PDF pp. 47–50). Congregating in ways you can target is one of the seven criteria for choosing which Job to work on (ch. 5, PDF p. 67). A website survey of five questions is the quickest way to learn from future customers, and the fourth question, what matters most in a new solution, decides what the site leads with (ch. 4, PDF pp. 49–51). Each milestone gets one KPI, and new unique website visits is chosen over total traffic because the intent is to count awareness among new people (ch. 7, PDF pp. 98–99). A Try-it-free call to action replacing a Schedule-a-demo one is a named way to bridge the Interest milestone (ch. 8, PDF pp. 113–114). The buyer-journey map and pirate metrics are rejected for rewarding transactional moments such as signup over customer value moments (ch. 6, PDF pp. 73–75).

**Marketing Made Simple** (Miller with Peterson, 2020). The marketing plan is five pieces of collateral — a one-liner, a wireframed website, a lead-generating PDF, a nurture email campaign, and a sales email campaign — and advertising is not replaced by them, only re-sequenced behind them (Introduction, pp. 8–9; ch. 3, p. 34). A first page has about ten seconds to say what is offered, how it makes a life better, and what to do to buy it (ch. 5, pp. 61–62). Only the header's position is fixed; the rest of the sections may go in any order, and two of the nine are optional (ch. 5, pp. 59–60, 70). Publish three price options with bullet points of what each includes, or publish no prices at all, with custom pricing and long catalogs given as reasons to leave prices off the site entirely (ch. 5, p. 103). A lead generator trades something genuinely valuable for an email address, and the hit rate is given as roughly six in ten, with one of the company's own thorough PDFs named as a dud (ch. 6, pp. 108–110, 125). Email runs at least weekly, and they decline to give a magic number for frequency (ch. 7, p. 127). Most companies grow without a sales email campaign, which is why they often recommend building the nurture sequence first (ch. 9, p. 148). Pop-ups are conceded to be annoying, defended only on click rate, and hedged twice, because search engines change the rules and can penalize (ch. 6, p. 123). One funnel is a starting point rather than the plan, and a large company may run hundreds (ch. 1, p. 14).

**This Is Marketing** (Godin, 2018). Asking how to get the word out is called precisely the wrong question and the last part rather than the first; goal, strategy, and tactics are separated, and tactics are replaceable while a strategy a competitor learns cannot be used for want of guts and persistence (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 135-136). Advertising is an optional engine rather than the discipline: an advertiser buys attention instead of earning it, most television advertising is called semiotic noise and a tax major companies in competitive markets pay, and online advertising, despite psychographic precision, immediacy, and measurability, is called the most ignored advertising ever created (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 136-138). He keeps the exceptions open — full-page ads, network television, and sponsoring a tournament or a podcast are allowed as possibly the best use of the money, brand ads only for a marketer who can afford to be consistent and patient, and direct ads not at all until a value can be assigned to each step (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 140-141). Direct marketing is measured and brand marketing is not, and confusing them is the named danger (Ch. 15, PDF p. 139). The funnel is unlikely to be a magical fountain, because lifetime value rarely exceeds the cost of the ads needed to acquire a customer, so most brands that matter are primed by advertising and built by evangelism and tribes; the instruction is not to buy ads where the funnel cannot be seen or cannot be afforded (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 166-167). Permission is the privilege of anticipated, personal, and relevant messages, social platforms are called a sharecropper's arrangement because the platform holds the asset, and the metric offered is how many people would complain if the next send did not go out (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 152-154). Permission is earned by showing up with generosity where a tribe's permission already exists, the way Marvel goes to Comic-Con rather than to nationwide television, a year before release, with news rather than a ticket pitch (Ch. 17, PDF p. 156). Frequency is the closing argument: people remember what they rehearse, familiarity becomes trust, and marketers break it because they get bored before the audience gets the message (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 142-143). He rejects generic search engine optimization outright, calling being the first match among a thousand pages of results a delusion and the consultant market a pyramid the math cannot support, and offers two steps instead — make a product worth searching for specifically, then be the one they want to find (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 143-144).

**Start Small, Stay Small** (Walling, 2010). The Top Shelf channels are a mailing list, a blog or podcast, and organic search; social media and pay-per-click are supplemental, and many approaches will not work in a given market (Ch. 5, pp. 145–150). Measure every traffic source with analytics goals and stop pursuing the sources that do not convert (Ch. 5, pp. 147–148). Lifting conversion beats buying traffic: one more sale a month is 100 more visitors or 0.1 points of conversion (Ch. 4, pp. 115–116). The website's first goal, ahead of the sale, is an email address, won with trust, relevance, and a reward such as a contest or a free report, and list management is never hosted in-house because deliverability is the priority and a third party staffs it full time (Ch. 4, pp. 118–119, 135–138). He twice declines to advise magazine advertising, because prospects are hard to track, the content cannot be changed mid-run, and readers rarely switch media — the sub-$5,000 full-page ad is a market-sizing rule rather than a recommendation (Ch. 2, pp. 47, 57). On search he answers his own question both ways: a blog has no inherent advantage, and a ten-page static site and a ten-page blog that never changes look the same to Google (Ch. 5, pp. 153–154). Directories have lost a lot of impact as algorithms improved, automated submission packages are called not worth $5, article-repository pages do not typically rank because the text is duplicated, and buying links carries Google's own penalty (Ch. 5, pp. 159–163). Blogs beat mailing lists in technical markets and the ordering flips everywhere else (Ch. 5, p. 152). A targeted launch mailing can convert 20 percent or more, against 0.5 to 2 percent for a site above $50 or on recurring pricing (Ch. 2, p. 67; Ch. 4, p. 144).

**Software as a Science** (Martell, Verlaque, Page, and Petitpas, 2024). There are four macro channels: earned media and partnerships, SEO, paid ads, and cold outreach, with earned media described as borrowing attention from someone who already built an audience, in modern forms such as podcast guesting, industry events, social collaborations, guest posts, and joint webinars rather than traditional press (ch. 3, PDF pp. 66–70). Earned media's one risk is that it is the only channel not pointing at a property the company owns, so the attention has to be captured with a lead magnet (ch. 3, PDF pp. 69–70). They count YouTube, Reddit, Quora, Product Hunt, and review sites such as G2 and Capterra as search engines, note that bottom-of-funnel keywords are the most competitive and are topped by ads, and put the larger opportunity in awareness and consideration keywords (ch. 3, PDF pp. 71–73). They say they rarely recommend paid ads as a first channel for a self-funded company, that cost efficiency decays with scale, and that the advertiser is at the mercy of the platform (ch. 3, PDF p. 74). Cold outreach suits a founder who is cash-poor, time-rich, and without a network, and is worth running early because it teaches the founder about the customer faster than any other channel, though whether it works depends primarily on the market and on enough money in a deal to pay the people dialing (ch. 3, PDF pp. 75–76). Trade shows are named as an earned-media channel one founder took to $200,000 MRR by going deep on one show's mechanics (ch. 3, PDF p. 78). The selection tool scores each macro channel red, yellow, or green on five questions — a documented process, dedicated people, a scorecard, a testing cadence, and a CAC payback period under ninety days — and the instruction is to pick the column closest to green whether or not it feels right, take it all the way green, and only then stack the next (ch. 3, PDF pp. 79–81). In most markets fewer than 10 percent of people are shopping at any moment, so a company writing only about its own software addresses a tenth of its opportunity (ch. 4, PDF pp. 88–89). Retargeting is called table stakes, and roughly 97 percent of initial website traffic is quoted as leaving and never returning on its own (ch. 4, PDF p. 92).

**The Lean Startup** (Ries, 2011). Sustainable growth is defined by one rule, that new customers come from the actions of past customers, which excludes one-time advertisements and publicity stunts (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 200). The four sources are word of mouth, a side effect of product usage, funded advertising paid for out of revenue rather than investment, and repeat purchase, folded into three engines — sticky, viral, and paid (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 200-201). The paid engine turns on the margin between lifetime value and cost per acquisition, and the chapter's claim about it is that competition bids up the cost of any acquisition source until firms pay away most of their marginal profit, so lasting growth on it requires a differentiated ability to monetize a particular set of customers (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 209-211). Viral products often cannot charge, because price is friction in the loop, so the value hypothesis has to be tested by a nonmonetary exchange (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 207). He recommends focusing on one engine at a time while noting more than one can operate, and says every engine eventually exhausts its set of customers, which can take years and then presents as a sudden crisis (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 211, 214-215). An MVP that would damage a brand is launched under a different name, and long-term reputation is mostly at risk where a company has already generated hype (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 111-112). A website hit fails the accessibility test because nobody can say what it is, and a report should deal in people and their actions rather than piles of data points (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 140-142).

**The Four Steps to the Epiphany** (Blank, 2013). He places no serious marketing spending before a proven and repeatable sales roadmap exists, and says the first year in a new market aims at adoption rather than market share (Chapter 5, pp. 346–347, 363, 384). The launch reaches its audience through messengers rather than directly, and the audience is narrowed to the small group carrying the most influence rather than everyone on the influence map (Chapter 5, pp. 404–412). The media strategy states which media will be used to reach customers and, more importantly, which will not (Chapter 5, pp. 417–418). Launch type is set by Market Type, so the four types take four different launches, and Market Type changes customer adoption rate, positioning, market size, launch, cash needs, and the shape of the sales growth curve (Chapter 2, pp. 87, 90). Branding is expensive and visceral, and in a new market branding spending is described as the sinkhole (Chapter 5, pp. 342–348, 363; Chapter 6, pp. 473, 479–480). He asks customers directly how they hear about new companies and products, and measures the launch against a pre-launch baseline rather than asserting it worked (Chapter 3, pp. 163–165, 195; Chapter 5, pp. 418–420). The Customer Creation chapter explicitly declines to cover adwords, advertising, and public relations, offering strategy in their place (Chapter 5, p. 369).

**The Startup Owner's Manual** (Blank and Dorf, 2012). The channels hypothesis tells web and mobile startups to pick one distribution channel, with the exception that a company using its own website to sell directly to end users tests that in parallel (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 275-283; Physical Channel, Ch. 8, PDF p. 992). The low-fidelity MVP may be a landing page rotated against AdWords copy rather than working software, after which the instruction is to drive traffic and start counting (Web/Mobile, Ch. 4, PDF pp. 383-387, 392). Customer Development and stealth are mutually exclusive, and in the same breath they say this is not the time for press releases, interviews, blogs, or public demos, because the founder does not yet know enough to say what business the company is in, while allowing an invite-only site to control access while the solution is tested (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420). Velocity is not always the goal: niche vertical sites and high-value products are named as the two rare exceptions where a few high-value customers may be enough (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420). Conferences and trade shows are named as the prime places to meet competitors, collect their literature, talk to their salespeople, and spot trends and talent (Web/Mobile, Ch. 4, PDF p. 406). Retention is held to be far cheaper than acquisition, which is their reason for planning it before acquisition succeeds (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 341). The metrics toolset is built before selling, opened with John Wanamaker's complaint that half his advertising was wasted and he did not know which half (Web/Mobile, Ch. 8, PDF pp. 515-516). Growth is monitored by the viral growth factor with the caution that not every product spreads virally, dating and similar sites being the named case (Web/Mobile, Ch. 9, PDF pp. 574-576).

**Zero to One** (Thiel with Masters, 2014). Distribution is his catchall for everything it takes to sell a product, and he says it is systematically underrated, especially by engineers; a product that sells itself is called an impossible claim, and distribution is part of the design of the product, so an invention with no effective way to sell it is a bad business however good the product (ch. 11, PDF pp. 96–98). The methods run as a continuum bounded by lifetime value against acquisition cost: complex sales, personal sales at roughly $10,000 to $100,000 a deal, the distribution doldrums, marketing and advertising for low-priced mass-appeal products, and viral marketing (ch. 11, PDF pp. 99–103). The doldrums are the dead zone: at a product priced near $1,000 sold to small businesses, advertising is too broad or too inefficient and personal sales too expensive, which he offers as the reason so many small and medium-sized businesses do not use tools larger firms take for granted (ch. 11, PDF p. 101). Advertising is defended on the ground that it works and that it exists to leave impressions that drive later sales rather than to make an immediate one, and what he warns against is competing with big companies' campaigns, his own PayPal launch event being the failure against Priceline's success (ch. 11, PDF pp. 97, 102–103). Distribution follows a power law of its own: one method will be far more powerful than the rest, the kitchen-sink approach fails, most businesses get zero channels working, and poor sales rather than bad product is the most common cause of failure — one working channel is a great business, several attempted and none nailed is finished (ch. 11, PDF p. 104). Selling extends to non-customers, since a prospective hire will search for the company and what he finds matters, and the closing line is that if you see no salespeople, you are the salesperson (ch. 11, PDF pp. 104–105). Branding is a monopoly on your own name, the surface techniques are imitable, and no technology company can be built on branding alone (ch. 5, PDF pp. 40–41).

**Company of One** (Jarvis, 2019). Out-sharing rather than out-scaling is the named edge: teaching makes the teacher the perceived domain expert, shows the benefit of what is sold without selling, and builds the authority that answers a buyer's instinct that a larger company is safer, with Casper's sleep publications that do not sell mattresses and Basecamp's mandate to out-teach everyone as the cases (ch. 9, PDF pp. 106–113). Email is the scalable connection channel, carrying the Data and Marketing Association's 3,800 percent return figure and more than 93 percent of his own revenue (ch. 8, PDF pp. 101–102), while direct one-to-one communication with customers is called always required (ch. 8, PDF p. 101). Word of mouth is hard to measure, because a recommendation can happen in a coffee shop or an untrackable private message, and hard to scale quickly, which he concedes is bad for a business focused on exponential growth; a good product and good service are required first, since no incentive creates advocates without them (ch. 10, PDF pp. 118–119). Cash incentives for referrals are called tricky, because they can reduce trust when profit is seen as the whole reason for a recommendation (ch. 10, PDF p. 119). Followers, subscribers, and clicks are vanity metrics that show how many people took the marketing bait rather than measuring engagement or trust (ch. 4, PDF p. 60; ch. 10, PDF pp. 121–122). Social capital is modeled as a bank account from which nothing can be withdrawn that was not deposited, with Buffer's daily research-backed blog and Chris Guillebeau personally emailing his first 10,000 subscribers as the deposits, and Sam Milbrath's thirds — own content, others' content, personal interaction — as the allocation rule (ch. 12, PDF pp. 144–145). He warns against the phrase "our audience", because an audience is never a company's property (ch. 12, PDF pp. 139–140). Publishing what you charge where anyone can compare it is itself the trust move (ch. 10, PDF pp. 116–117).

**Buy Back Your Time** (Martell, 2023). No position on where the founder goes, what the money buys, or whether the site has to be found. Its nearest claim sits in the founder's own calendar: sales is Rung 4 of the Replacement Ladder, second-to-last, because the founder is usually the best seller and that revenue funds the earlier rungs, so it is only practical to hand over when growth requires it (ch. 5, PDF p. 106).

**Entrepreneur Revolution** (Priestley, 2024). The audience is built before the product, with the Tesla CyberTruck reveal as the model — a prototype, a story, a stated wait of years, a $100 holding deposit, and the resulting list used as proof to raise the money for the factory (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 94–95). Four signal campaigns are named: a waiting list, an online discussion group, an introduction event of 30 to 60 minutes, and an online assessment or quiz (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 95–96). Roughly 1 in 20 of an audience buys at once, so an audience has to exceed 200 people for every 10 sales wanted at launch, and the lead-to-sale rate he sees across industries is under 5 percent, so roughly 20 warm leads are needed per sale (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 96, 103–104). Sales is presented as unavoidable and founder-led: he sold ScoreApp face to face to well over 1,000 business owners, and says the meetings produced the language, the objections, and the product changes that observing from a website would not have (Chapter 4, PDF p. 101). A lead capture process sits between the free gift and the first paid step, and a business should have several ways to collect contact details (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 122–123). Sales conversations should not happen at all until the person has experienced a product for prospects (Chapter 5, PDF p. 123). Reputation and personal profile are named the most prized asset, and the first page of a search for your name is the measure (Chapter 8, PDF pp. 173–175). Market size is mostly irrelevant, since Apple had no PC market in 1977 and Airbnb no market for renting a stranger's bedroom (Chapter 4, PDF p. 94).

**The Voltage Effect** (List, 2022). The rule he draws from federal benefit-cost analysis is to look at the positive impact of the last dollar spent rather than the average (Chapter 7, PDF pp. 155–156). Lyft is where it worked: reading a spreadsheet of advertising returns, he found the last bucket of dollars on Facebook ads yielded a fiftieth of the business of the last bucket on Google ads, and the fix was to move spending; the same pattern ran through driver recruiting and refer-a-friend offers, and the company had been reading average returns (Chapter 7, PDF pp. 161–162). The practice is to look where there are many levers, collect data granularly over time and across strategies, run multisite trials, and look per region rather than at a national daily average (Chapter 7, PDF pp. 166–167). Sunk costs are the obstacle, and the bygones principle is that money spent in the past should have no influence on present decisions, with the University of Chicago call center as the case: mothballed on an average cost per call that included the one-time phone-banking system, and rebooted once the calls were recalculated on the margin (Chapter 7, PDF pp. 168–169). Some marginal benefits resist measurement, and asked by the Make-A-Wish Foundation to value gifts to terminally ill children he answered that some things worth doing and scaling transcend monetization (Chapter 7, PDF p. 167). The people who opt in to a pilot are the ones most likely to benefit from it, so the initial audience may not represent the population served at scale (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 55, 57–58).

**Thinking in Bets** (Duke, 2018). No position on where the founder goes, what the money buys, or whether the site has to be found. Its nearest claim is about how a target is read rather than where it comes from: a forecast pitched anywhere between the two extremes is not shown to have been mistaken merely because the likelier of its futures failed to arrive (ch. 1, PDF p. 33), and a number somebody hesitates to guess at is already being guessed at, so the honest move is to state it as a guess (ch. 6, PDF p. 166).

## Where the books agree

- Words before channels: Copyhackers, Hot Tips, and Ulwick all put the reader's own words and stage of awareness ahead of any channel choice; Walling's changelog and Levels's press list both start from a message that already exists. The new books join on the same side: Dunford puts messaging and the channel work that carries it after positioning (After Positioning, pp. 129–132), Miller's campaign is written from a BrandScript that exists first (StoryBrand, ch. 13, PDF p. 172), Miller and Peterson make the one-liner the first of the five pieces (Marketing Made Simple, Part II opener, p. 39), Godin calls asking how to get the word out precisely the wrong question and the last part rather than the first (Ch. 15, PDF p. 136), and Blank and Dorf craft positioning before the acquire-and-activate plan (Web/Mobile, Ch. 8, PDF pp. 479-483).
- Measure every source and drop what does not convert: Walling in both books; Levels names the analytics tools. Walling's *Start Small* adds analytics goals per source (Ch. 5, pp. 147–148), Dib calls not measuring the mark of an amateur (1PMP, Chapter 3, pp. 111, 128), Martell and his coauthors gate a channel on a CAC payback period under ninety days among five measures (ch. 3, PDF pp. 79–80), Blank measures a launch against a pre-launch baseline rather than asserting it worked (Chapter 5, pp. 418–420), and List sharpens the same instinct into reading the last dollar rather than the average (Chapter 7, PDF pp. 155–156, 161–162). Godin splits it: measure everything for direct and refuse to measure for brand (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 139-140).
- Organic before paid: Levels's 100,000-user threshold and Walling's funnel arithmetic both keep paid traffic out at this price and this stage; Thomas's eBay and Bing figures say paid search under-delivers even at scale. Martell and his coauthors say they rarely recommend paid ads as a first channel for a self-funded company (ch. 3, PDF p. 74); Godin says not to buy brand ads without the money to be consistent and patient, and not to run direct ads until a value can be assigned to each step (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 140-141); Blank puts serious marketing spending after a repeatable sales roadmap (Chapter 5, pp. 346–347); Dib allows a budget only in the testing phase (1PMP, Chapter 3, pp. 128–129); and Laudi and Suellentrop offer their own two cases as having grown sharply without paid acquisition (ch. 1, PDF pp. 17, 21).
- The founder is the voice: Levels's build-in-public, Naval's permissionless media, Walling's sell-and-build. Jarvis puts the founder's personality in front of the brand as the one thing a competitor cannot replicate (ch. 6, PDF pp. 77–78); Priestley sold face to face to well over 1,000 business owners himself (Chapter 4, PDF p. 101); Thiel ends on the line that if you see no salespeople, you are the salesperson (ch. 11, PDF p. 105); Fitzpatrick says customer learning cannot be outsourced until there is a repeatable process (ch. 8, p. 104); Blank and Dorf say the work cannot be assigned to junior staff (Strategy Guide, Ch. 4, PDF p. 97); and Martell and his coauthors have the founder's own voice carry the first channel (ch. 3, PDF pp. 79–83).
- Go where the group already gathers, rather than assembling one: Godin's Marvel at Comic-Con a year before release (Ch. 17, PDF p. 156), Dib's day at an industry conference against six months of online research (Lean Marketing, Chapter 2, pp. 49–50), Fitzpatrick's immersion, meetup, and shared-topic excuse (ch. 6, pp. 77–79), Laudi and Suellentrop's forums, communities, and review sites when there are no customers yet (ch. 4, PDF pp. 47–49) with congregating in a targetable way as a criterion for choosing the Job (ch. 5, PDF p. 67), Martell and his coauthors' earned media and one trade show taken deep (ch. 3, PDF pp. 68–70, 78), Blank's launch through messengers rather than directly (Chapter 5, pp. 404–412), and Walling's warm niche where the founder already has an association (Ch. 2, pp. 45–51).
- The list is the asset, and it is owned rather than rented: Godin's permission, with social platforms called a sharecropper's arrangement (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 152-153); Dib in both books, own your marketing assets (1PMP, Chapter 3, pp. 119, 122) and on social media you are a tenant (Lean Marketing, Chapter 13, pp. 212, 216); Walling's rule that the website's first goal is an email address and that list management is never hosted in-house (Ch. 4, pp. 118–119, 137–138); Jarvis's 93 percent of revenue through email (ch. 8, PDF pp. 101–102); Miller's lead generator (StoryBrand, ch. 15, PDF pp. 198–205) and the gated resource in Marketing Made Simple (ch. 6, pp. 108–110); Martell and his coauthors' lead magnet, which is also how earned media's borrowed attention is captured (ch. 3, PDF pp. 69–70; ch. 4, PDF p. 93); and Priestley's waiting list, named first of four signal campaigns (Chapter 4, PDF p. 95).
- Nobody promises a generic search will find the site. Godin calls being the first match among a thousand pages of results a delusion and the consultant market a pyramid the math cannot support, and offers instead making a product worth searching for by name (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 143-144); Walling says a ten-page static site and a ten-page blog that never changes look the same to Google, and records directories, article repositories, and bought links as diminished, duplicated, or penalized (Ch. 5, pp. 153–154, 159–163); Martell and his coauthors put bottom-of-funnel keywords out of reach as the most competitive and topped by ads, and move the opportunity to awareness and consideration keywords (ch. 3, PDF pp. 71–73); Ulwick has buyers entering a job step or a desired outcome rather than a product name; Dib says a searcher did not type the generic word (Lean Marketing, Chapter 2, p. 38); and Priestley and Thiel both measure discoverability by what a search for a name returns (Chapter 8, PDF pp. 173–175; ch. 11, PDF p. 104).

## Where the books conflict

- **Being in the room.** Naval calls networking a waste; Levels says launch where the customers are; Walling measures a show by whether its return covers its cost. Naval's claim is about the founder's own network, the other two about the buyer's venue, and none of the three says which a trade summit is. Dib joins Levels, with a day at an industry conference beating six months of online research (Lean Marketing, Chapter 2, pp. 49–50), and Martell and his coauthors name a founder who reached $200,000 MRR on trade shows alone (ch. 3, PDF p. 78), while Dib elsewhere files the trade-show route as one line in a budget and flagship experiences as an advanced strategy with little connection to any return (Chapter 2, p. 50; Chapter 8, p. 139). Blank and Dorf send the founder to conferences for a different errand again — to meet competitors, collect their literature, and spot trends and talent, not to sell (Web/Mobile, Ch. 4, PDF p. 406).
- **How many channels.** Thiel says distribution follows a power law, that one method will be far more powerful than the rest, and that the kitchen-sink approach fails (ch. 11, PDF p. 104); Martell and his coauthors take one channel all the way green before stacking the next (ch. 3, PDF pp. 80–81); Blank and Dorf tell a web or mobile startup to pick one distribution channel, with the founder's own website tested in parallel as the stated exception (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 275-283; Physical Channel, Ch. 8, PDF p. 992); Ries recommends one engine of growth at a time (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 211). Against them, Dib says hold at least five sources of new leads because one of anything is a single point of failure (1PMP, Chapter 3, pp. 130–132), Walling's *SaaS Playbook* runs one fast and one slow channel, and Walling's *Start Small* names three Top Shelf channels with social and pay-per-click supplemental (Ch. 5, pp. 145–150). Priestley's four signal campaigns run together (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 95–96).
- **Talking in public before the model is found.** Levels tells his own story and builds in public, and Naval calls media permissionless leverage; Blank and Dorf say Customer Development and stealth are mutually exclusive and, in the same breath, that this is not the time for press releases, interviews, blogs, or public demos, because the founder does not yet know enough to say what business the company is in (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420), with Blank putting serious marketing spending after a repeatable sales roadmap (Chapter 5, pp. 346–347). Jarvis, with the Copyblogger case he reports, sits on the other side, where out-sharing is the edge and teaching is what builds authority (ch. 9, PDF pp. 106–113).
- **Launch platforms.** Levels names Product Hunt and Hacker News and then concedes the better point against them in the same chapter. Martell and his coauthors count Product Hunt, Reddit, Quora, YouTube, and the review sites as search engines rather than as launch venues (ch. 3, PDF p. 71).
- **What a list is for.** Walling's list is a funnel with a sequence; Naval's media is a brand with no funnel at all. Miller and Peterson run a six-email sales sequence on a clock, days apart, whether or not the recipient has done anything (Marketing Made Simple, ch. 9, pp. 149–151), and Miller's drip repeats three nurture emails and a sales email month after month with some brands never stopping (StoryBrand, ch. 15, PDF pp. 198–205); Godin's permission is dating rather than a funnel, earned over time, tested by whether people would complain if the next send did not go out (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 153-154). The frequency figures do not line up either: Dib's *Lean Marketing* sets once a week as a minimum and once a week to twice a day as the band (Chapter 12, pp. 199–200), Miller and Peterson say at least weekly and decline a magic number (ch. 7, p. 127), and Dib's *1PMP* says at least monthly and that there are no hard and fast rules (Chapter 3, p. 123).
- **Whether advertising works at this size at all.** Thiel defends advertising on the ground that it works and that it exists to leave impressions rather than to make an immediate sale, and then names the distribution doldrums near a $1,000 product where neither advertising nor personal sales pays (ch. 11, PDF pp. 97, 101); Godin calls online advertising the most ignored ever created while allowing full-page ads, network television, and podcast sponsorship as possibly the best use of the money (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 138, 141); Thomas's eBay and Bing figures read paid search as a loss even at scale; Dib says long-standing advertisers in a trade publication are worth paying attention to because they are likely getting a return (Lean Marketing, Chapter 2, pp. 49–50), while Walling twice declines to advise magazine advertising at all (Ch. 2, pp. 47, 57).
- **Evidence.** Walling's ten-times figure and Levels's thresholds are the authors' own experience, uncited; Copyhackers's results are one page each; Hot Tips is uncited throughout. Dib disowns his own follow-up statistics in two footnotes, saying he traced them no further than major national research firms and that they should be taken with a grain of salt (1PMP, Chapter 5, p. 171; Chapter 8, p. 263); Priestley's 1-in-20 and under-5-percent figures are what he sees across his own client base (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 96, 104); Miller and Peterson's correlation between checklist implementation and growth comes from an unnamed independent research company (ch. 3, pp. 36–37). At the other end, List's advertising claim is a field economist's read of one company's spreadsheet (Chapter 7, PDF pp. 161–162), and Godin's own funnel case is the project of his that failed, recorded with four reasons of his own naming (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 175-176).
