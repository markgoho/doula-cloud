# Theme synthesis: persuasion and its limits

## What this file is for

This is the second stage of ADR-0041 for one theme on the values track. It lays out what each of the twenty-nine values books claims about the techniques a page or a product may use to move a person, and where those techniques become manipulation; where the books agree; where they conflict; and what the repo has already decided. It contains no ruling. Page citations follow each read-back's own page rule. The read-backs are in `docs/research/books/`. Where an agreement or conflict bullet names Walling without a title, it means the two 2023 books, *The SaaS Playbook* and *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*, which were the only Walling books on the track when the bullet was written; *Start Small, Stay Small* is named by title wherever it is meant. A bare Blank means *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* and Blank and Dorf means *The Startup Owner's Manual*; a bare Miller means *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* and Miller and Peterson means *Marketing Made Simple*; Dib is named by title or as both of his books wherever the two differ.

## The question

Which ways of moving a person are open to the product and to the site that sells it, and what test tells a nudge from a trick?

## What each book claims

**Design for Cognitive Bias** (Thomas, 2020). Reactance: a polite sign draws less graffiti than one shouting in capitals, and telling people what to do invites the opposite (ch. 1, pp. 4–5). The framing effect: 95% lean and 5% fat are the same beef, and the frame supersedes the facts (ch. 2, pp. 34–35). Sometimes friction is good: slowing a person where a decision matters reduces errors (ch. 2, pp. 35–36). Notational bias: a male/female field wipes out identities, and design components represent mental models of what a user can express (ch. 4, pp. 72–74). Honorifics foreground marital status; leaving metadata out is also a choice (ch. 4, pp. 75–76). Every request for personal information builds a potentially unhealthy relationship and deserves pause (ch. 4, pp. 74–75). An optional email field placed between a terms checkbox and a CAPTCHA reads as required, a dark pattern (ch. 4, p. 87). The contrast effect steers a buyer to the visually favored plan, and the test is whether the designer would disclose why (ch. 4, pp. 87–88).

**Landing Page Hot Tips.** Create haste with a time-limited discount, increase demand by limiting supply, and soft-launch with a discount to a select audience (Tips #24, #51, #76; PDF pp. 56, 98–99, 142). Add social proof, prove you are established with a founding year and usage figures, and put the best testimonial above the fold (Tips #52, #82, #83; PDF pp. 100, 153, 154–155). Offer tiers and suggest one (Tips #14, #50; PDF pp. 39–41, 95–97).

**Copyhackers: Uplift.** A trusted-by logo row and social proof made the user-friendly claim more believable; the original page had no persuasive elements to build trust (Section 2, pp. 21–22). Copy written from the customer's own words outperformed copy written about the product (Section 2, p. 20; Section 4, p. 35).

**The Best Interface Is No Interface** (Krishna, 2015). Chapter 5 argues that a product built to maximize screen time is optimizing against the person using it.

**Make: The Bootstrapper's Handbook** (Levels). The complainers about price are not the customers (Monetize, pp. 133–135); build in public and show mistakes, because it makes a founder look human (Grow, pp. 118–121). The read-back's section 8 notes the book's own controversy over tactics it recommends.

**The SaaS Playbook** (Walling, 2023). Grandfathering: never promise it for life; announce a price increase two to four months ahead (Pricing, pp. 83–85). A founder email within ten minutes of a cancellation (80/20 SaaS Metrics, p. 161).

**Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding** (Walling, 2023 ed.). The hook: a five-to-seven-word home-page headline saying what the product does and for whom ("How to Find Your 4-Second Startup Pitch", pp. 73–75). No position on the ethics of persuasion; its funnel advice assumes the techniques work.

**Jobs To Be Done** (Ulwick, 2016). A winning value proposition is tied to unmet outcomes, as in Coloplast's claim to prevent complications and Arm & Hammer's move to talking about outcomes (ch. 4 §V, pp. 100–102; §IX, p. 115; ch. 5, p. 138). Its claim that buyers search by outcome rather than product name (ch. 4 §IX, pp. 115–116) is about being found, not moved.

**The Almanack of Naval Ravikant** (Jorgenson, 2020). No direct position. Its nearest claim is that a person escapes competition through authenticity (Building Wealth, pp. 41, 44).

**Obviously Awesome** (Dunford, 2019). Context is the lever: the Joshua Bell subway experiment and the cake pop are offered to show that the same product, framed differently, is worth a different amount to the same person (Positioning as Context, pp. 26–27, 35–37). A claim that a product is easy to use or has outstanding service is a value claim rather than a feature, and an opinion of one's own value "does not count as proof", third-party validation being what counts (Components, pp. 47–48; Step 5, pp. 81–82). Declaring a market category triggers assumptions about competitors, features and price that the product then has to meet, and a poor choice sends marketing and sales to battle assumptions the product does not satisfy, as with the email product that had no calendar (Components, pp. 49–51). The process assumes enough happy customers to read a pattern from, and where no pattern is visible the positioning is kept loose rather than tightened (Step 1, pp. 61–62). A trend is optional, and the book prefers a product "successful and boring" to one "fashionable and bewildering" (Step 9, pp. 120–121; Conclusion, p. 138).

**Building a StoryBrand 2.0** (Miller, 2025). The mechanism claimed is metabolic: processing information burns calories, so a message that is neither about survival nor simple gets tuned out (ch. 1, PDF pp. 20–22). Miller answers the manipulation objection in the text, conceding that brands must be careful to refrain from manipulating people with half-truths and exaggerations even while learning from the entertainment industry, and he puts a truth condition on drama — do not be afraid of a little drama, "especially if that drama is true" (ch. 5, PDF pp. 72, 75). A villain must be real, the reader is told never to go down the path of being a fearmonger, and in the same list a villain does not have to be serious (ch. 5, PDF pp. 75–76). Fear is salt in the recipe: too many warnings about imminent doom risk turning customers off, and the book block-quotes its own source saying high fear is blocked out while only moderate amounts change behavior — then adds that almost nobody reading the book will overuse it, answers its own question about too many stakes with "Almost never", and calls fearmongering not the problem 99.9 percent of business leaders struggle with (ch. 9, PDF pp. 131–134). Competency is shown by statistics, by awards that are "hardly necessary", and by about three short testimonials, with rambling praise avoided because a long one will not be read (ch. 6, PDF p. 99). The Buy Now button is a different and preferably brighter color from every other button on the page, all such buttons identical and repeated as the page scrolls (ch. 8, PDF pp. 116–117; ch. 14, PDF pp. 178–180); a sales email's call to action carries scarcity, the printed example's strength being that it is a onetime offer (ch. 15, PDF pp. 207–208); and the guide may talk about itself all day long, provided it tells only the parts that support its care and competency (ch. 6, PDF p. 98).

**Lean Marketing** (Dib, 2024). The test set for the marketing itself is that it be valuable enough that the target market "would pay you to receive it" (Chapter 1, p. 21). A soap opera email sequence "should be built on truth", and no lean marketing tactic is to be used "to deceive" (Chapter 12, p. 204). Where a business has no social proof yet, the author's first piece of advice is "not to be dishonest" and invent it, and the two honest routes are giving the product "away for free to a select group" in the target market in exchange for "an honest review" (Chapter 14, pp. 237–238). A quick win is labeled as one, because there is a danger that customers get hooked on quick wins and turn short-term (Chapter 14, p. 248). Cash incentives for informal referrals are argued against on psychological grounds, while in a formal referral relationship disclosure is often legally required and incentivized referrals are sometimes illegal (Chapter 14, p. 243). Being "inappropriately funny" detracts, and "your job isn't to be a comedian" (Chapter 5, p. 95). Price is a signal of quality and of status, and premium positioning is "by no means" an excuse for poor customer service (Chapter 3, pp. 69–70, 72).

**The 1-Page Marketing Plan** (Dib, 2018). Emotional direct-response copy is the engine, and the author raises the manipulation objection himself, conceding that the tool can be used for harm and setting an ethical boundary against using it that way (Chapter 2, p. 100). People answer surveys with logic and buy with emotion, so asking has to be supplemented by observing (Chapter 2, p. 87). Marketing to the database until people buy or die is immediately qualified — no pressure tactics, a stream of value, welcome guest rather than pest, and an occasional offer rather than a constant pitch (Chapter 5, pp. 155, 156). Risk is reversed with a specific guarantee rather than a vague satisfaction promise, and an outrageous guarantee is called risky only where the business consistently does a poor job (Chapter 6, pp. 187–189). Two or three variants is the happy medium between too few and brain overload, and a single pricing option is separately named an error (Chapter 6, p. 191; Chapter 8, p. 244). A price rise carries a stated reason why, costs some customers, and grandfathering is the option for an owner who fears the reaction (Chapter 8, pp. 240–241). The technology that helps a small business look larger is explicitly said not to replace great products and service (Chapter 6, p. 186), and the free trial is called the puppy dog close, putting the onus on the buyer to reverse the sale (Chapter 6, pp. 195–197).

**The Mom Test** (Fitzpatrick, 2013). The book's subject is the other direction of influence, the founder moving the person he is trying to learn from, and its rules exist to stop that. Ask about specifics in the past rather than generics or opinions about the future, the worst fluff-inducing form being "Would you ever" (ch. 1, p. 13; ch. 2, pp. 28–29). A leading question is allowed only where the conversation is about to be abandoned anyway, with extra care taken over a positive answer (ch. 3, p. 47). The idea stays out of the conversation, and the mistake is almost always mentioning it too soon rather than too late (ch. 1, p. 13; ch. 4, p. 62). Commitment is the measure of a meeting and is bounded in the same passage: do not be annoying, this is not strong-arming, and a founder has no claim on anyone's contacts (ch. 5, pp. 69, 71). A trial counts as commitment only in proportion to what it costs the person, so a cheap trial has to be made more expensive before it means anything (ch. 5, p. 70).

**Competing Against Luck** (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan, 2016). The theory is stated to explain why customers make the choices they make and not whether a company ought to solve a given job; cigarettes are named as a product hired for an array of jobs and bad for the customer, and ethical choices are called equally important to get right (ch. 1 endnote, PDF p. 30). Not every premium price is the good kind: printer ink, chargers and phone cases are premiums extracted through interdependence, which create anxiety rather than resolving it, and the customer pays while resenting the company (ch. 6, PDF p. 123). Because the same review mechanism that helps a good product get hired lets a customer who hired it for the wrong job damage it, conveying who should not hire a product is treated as a new obligation rather than an option (ch. 6, PDF p. 131). Jobs are not created by a marketer, and a company attached to its present solution is vulnerable to a rival who understands the same job better, Snapchat being the named case (ch. 2, PDF p. 44). A job's social and emotional dimensions can outweigh its functional ones, and examination-room paper designed for function manufactures anxiety (ch. 2, PDF p. 38; ch. 4, PDF pp. 83–84).

**Forget the Funnel** (Laudi and Suellentrop, 2023). The book's one stated limit on moving a person is a measurement argument: measuring engagement by logins would push a team to nudge customers back into a product bought to save them the task (ch. 7, PDF pp. 96–97), and more generally how a team is measured decides how it behaves, a metric that rewards the wrong action producing the wrong action (ch. 7, PDF pp. 95–96). The words a customer arrives with are the words to use, and open-ended questions are the only way to collect them (ch. 3, PDF pp. 41–42; ch. 4, PDF p. 52), while customer talk taken literally is unreliable and the method is confined to reading the psychology under the answer (ch. 3, PDF p. 41). The projects it names for bridging a gap — an in-app checklist, an onboarding email series written from customer responses, win-back emails, nurture sequences, and solicited reviews and referrals (ch. 8, PDF pp. 111, 113–114), and a Try-it-free call to action replacing a Schedule-a-demo one (ch. 8, PDF pp. 113–114) — carry no test for when one of them becomes pressure.

**Marketing Made Simple** (Miller with Peterson, 2020). A direct request for the sale is presented as honest about a relationship that is transactional by nature, with the funnel controlling the pace so the request is never premature (ch. 2, pp. 26–28). Negative stakes are capped by the salt-in-a-cake limit, and the ASPCA commercials are named as a campaign the author himself turned off, with the guess that a lighter version would have done better; their whole purpose is contrast with a happy ending rather than weight of their own (ch. 5, p. 74). Too much authority against too little empathy confuses the customer about whose story it is, authority unrelated to the customer's problem is left off, and a reader with no testimonials and no awards is told not to worry and to move on (ch. 5, pp. 84–85, 89). A testimonial may be drafted for the customer, which the authors state plainly is not making something up, and the text goes back for approval (ch. 5, pp. 86–87). Pop-ups are conceded to be annoying, defended only on click rate, and the no-X close is admitted to feel like clickbait (ch. 6, p. 123). Three prices with bullet points are published or no prices at all, and the middle-item finding is attributed to what StoryBrand's certified guides have discovered rather than to a study (ch. 5, p. 103). The limited-time offer is conditional, phrased as something to do if it can be done rather than in every email (ch. 9, p. 149), and a sales campaign converts a minority, with rejection the expected outcome of asking (ch. 9, p. 147).

**This Is Marketing** (Godin, 2018). This book states the widest test of the twenty: marketing works for society when marketer and consumer both know what is happening and are both satisfied with the outcome, selling cosmetics passing because the process rather than the beauty brings the joy and swindling someone out of a house for a commission failing, with doing your job refused as a defense and the author noting that he is not the one who decides (Ch. 23, PDF pp. 196-197). Against that he sets the mechanism he teaches: marketers intentionally create tension, the canyon of being left out, left behind, or uninformed, and none of those feelings existed before a marketer created them (Ch. 10, PDF pp. 100-101). Tension is distinguished from fear, which is called a dream killer that paralyzes (Ch. 10, PDF p. 99), and the going-out-of-business sale is named as manufactured tension whose logic the author calls elusive, since the store will not honor a warranty (Ch. 10, PDF p. 100). Status is the engine, and shame is named the status killer, used as a lever because it works (Ch. 11, PDF p. 108). Permission is the privilege of anticipated, personal and relevant messages, and is separated from the presumed and legalistic kind: neither holding an address nor fine print in a privacy policy is permission, and the working test given is that people notice and ask where you went when you stop showing up (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 152-153). Three paths are named for a marketer in a trust vacuum — ignored, sneaking around, and trusted — and sneaking around is said to steal attention and a faux trust that does not last (Ch. 18, PDF pp. 159-160). Lowering the cost of the first click by overpromising is said to backfire (Ch. 19, PDF p. 164), lowering a price does not make a seller more trusted but does the opposite (Ch. 16, PDF p. 149), and saying it is not for you is offered as respect rather than rejection (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 36, 38-39).

**Start Small, Stay Small** (Walling, 2010). The book raises the ethical objection to its own Mini Sales Site — a page selling a product that does not yet exist, built to measure demand — and answers it with a "Coming soon" emblem on the page (Ch. 2, p. 85), while conceding that the technique cannot give 100% certainty either way and does not work for anything resting on a network effect (Ch. 2, pp. 86, 90). Selling something poor to a hungry market makes money and is explicitly "not a sustainable strategy", and the product still "has to be good" (Ch. 1, p. 5; Ch. 2, p. 40). Three pricing tiers exist because the author admits there is no single best price, and the rule that every price ends in 7, 8 or 9 is introduced as "stupid" and kept because it works (Ch. 3, p. 99). A 30-day, no-questions-asked money-back guarantee is recommended on the ground that the buyers it wins outnumber the people who abuse it (Ch. 4, p. 133). Buying links is presented with its penalty attached, Google penalizing or dropping a site found doing it, and the approach is called controversial and questionable to some (Ch. 5, p. 163). The sales website's first goal, ahead of the sale, is an email address, won with trust, relevance and a reward such as a contest or a free report (Ch. 4, pp. 118–119, 135–137).

**Software as a Science** (Martell, Verlaque, Page, and Petitpas, 2024). Of the twenty new books this is the one that recommends pressure techniques and states no limit on them. Self-service cancellation is removed and a call required, on the ground that a free-text reason box teaches nothing (ch. 7, PDF p. 163). The close asks for a credit card on the sales call itself, with Martell described as waiting on the phone while a prospect pulls over to find one, because every drop-off point is a chance to lose the deal (ch. 5, PDF p. 114). Stretching the gap is asking questions that push on a pain until it is unignorable before anything is shown (ch. 5, PDF p. 106), and the lead magnet's claimed move is that it teaches the customer to think about the problem in a way that positions the platform as the only viable solution (ch. 4, PDF p. 93). A customer health index exists so that a company can reach out to an account whose usage is slipping before the customer knows anything is wrong (ch. 7, PDF pp. 150, 161), and the authors note in the same chapter that calling a few dozen red accounts will surface people who had been meaning to cancel (ch. 7, PDF p. 163). Retargeting ads are called table stakes for any company taking its funnel seriously, the pixel placed on the content to tag visitors into an advertising audience (ch. 4, PDF p. 92). The Win/Ask method times each request to a moment the company's own data shows the customer winning, on Isen and Levin's payphone study; a testimonial is pre-written from the customer's own data for approval and directed to whichever review site needs authority; a referral is asked right after the customer says something nice, on Cialdini's commitment and consistency principle; and an anointed advocate is said to be the least likely customer to churn, because the status was announced publicly (ch. 8, PDF pp. 174–181).

**The Lean Startup** (Ries, 2011). No direct position on where a technique for moving a person becomes a trick. Its nearest claim is that split-testing belongs inside product development, a feature not being done until a split test shows changed customer behavior, which is how the lazy-registration test found an industry best practice to be waste (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 134-139); beside it sits the rule that an MVP which would damage a brand is launched under a different name, long-term reputation being mostly at risk where a company has already generated hype (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 111-112).

**The Four Steps to the Epiphany** (Blank, 2013). The problem presentation is built to elicit information rather than to convince, and the instruction is not to try to convince customers they have the problem described (Chapter 3, pp. 187–189). The word customer is reserved for people who pay, giveaways do not prove customers will buy, the aim is near list price from visionary customers with toughness on discounts and flexibility on terms, and no objection at all to a price is read as a sign the price is too low (Chapter 4, pp. 299, 301, 310–311, 327). Against that sits the book's own exception: promising unique extensions is called a trap, and the author allows it is "sometimes essential" to make such promises to get an order or two (Chapter 4, p. 309). An incomplete release is not to be distributed so widely that its limited feature set becomes etched in customers' minds as the finished product, and the gap between a good-enough minimum feature set and a product customers call junk is called a fine line (Chapter 4, pp. 292, 294). Branding is separated from positioning and described as expensive and visceral, and in a new market branding spending is called the sinkhole (Chapter 5, pp. 342–348, 363).

**The Startup Owner's Manual** (Blank and Dorf, 2012). Customer Development and stealth are called mutually exclusive, and a founder who thinks the idea too fragile to show without a non-disclosure agreement is told not to be reading the book; in the same passage an invite-only site is allowed as a way to control access while the solution is tested, and press releases, interviews, blogs and public demos are ruled out for that period, because the founder does not yet know enough to say what business the company is in (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420). The authors also acknowledge investors who encourage a startup to break all the rules and launch a product that does not exist, say there are times when tossing the book out makes sense — a market bubble, or an extraordinary customer reaction — and add that an investor holds a portfolio of startups while the founder holds a portfolio of one (Web/Mobile, Ch. 4, PDF p. 394). Free-to-paid conversion is called sexy but dangerous (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 280), and the early pass/fail experiments are described as looking for a strong enough signal rather than a rigorous one (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF p. 79).

**Zero to One** (Thiel with Masters, 2014). Advertising is defended on the ground that it works, and that it exists not to make an immediate sale but to leave impressions that drive later ones, so the person who thinks himself immune is doubly deceived (ch. 11, PDF p. 97). Sales is said to work best hidden, which the author reads in the job titles — account executive, business development, investment banker, politician — and in Tom Sawyer getting his friends to pay for the privilege of whitewashing the fence; a product that sells itself is called an impossible claim, and distribution is called part of the design of the product (ch. 11, PDF pp. 97–98). Branding is a monopoly on your own name, but the surface techniques are imitable and do not work without underlying substance, Yahoo! is the counter-case, and no technology company can be built on branding alone (ch. 5, PDF pp. 40–41). The word monopoly is narrowed to exclude the firm that eliminates rivals deviously and the one holding a state license (ch. 3, PDF p. 21), and definite planning is said to mean not designing by focus-group feedback (ch. 6, PDF p. 60).

**Company of One** (Jarvis, 2019). Trust is the argued lever, on Glen Urban's twenty years of research finding that it correlates with willingness to consider, try or buy, with confidence, competence and benevolence as its three aspects (ch. 10, PDF pp. 116, 118). Fixed, published prices are themselves the trust move where negotiated ones are not, Mazda being the case and the car dealership the worked example of transparency arriving from outside (ch. 10, PDF p. 117). Cash incentives for a referral are said to reduce trust, because profit is then seen as the whole reason for the recommendation, while discounts, swag and double-sided offers are not (ch. 10, PDF p. 119), and no incentive creates advocates without a good product and good service first (ch. 10, PDF p. 119). Vanity metrics — followers, subscribers, clicks — are said to show how many people took the marketing bait rather than to measure engagement or trust, with 100 passionate fans set against 100,000 followers chasing a free iPad (ch. 10, PDF pp. 121–122). Polarization is said to have an effect that is not always bad rather than always good, and the first of its three named strategies, placation, is a move toward the people who dislike the product (ch. 6, PDF p. 82). Content that goes viral is quoted, through Alex Beauchamp, as usually meaning the audience was never identified (ch. 10, PDF pp. 123–124).

**Buy Back Your Time** (Martell, 2023). No direct position. Its nearest claim is the R in the CLEAR feedback rule, under which the receiver always listens but whether the feedback is implemented is theirs to decide, and the author says not to force the practice on direct reports but only to suggest it (ch. 12, PDF pp. 210, 212).

**Entrepreneur Revolution** (Priestley, 2024). The instruction is to build a brochure before anything expensive and to run campaigns that test whether an audience exists before the thing does, a waiting list named first among four (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 95, 99). That instruction carries its own rule about money taken for something not yet secured: set the money aside so it can be returned (Chapter 4, PDF p. 100). A gift is free and asks nothing back, while a product for prospects is a low-cost first step giving a quick win inside seven days without cannibalizing the core offer, which is never sold at break-even or a loss (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 116–118, 120). Reputation and personal profile are named the most prized asset, with the first page of a search for your name as the measure (Chapter 8, PDF pp. 173–175), and a first approach to a gatekeeper going unanswered is labeled a not-this-time rather than a refusal (Chapter 8, PDF p. 176).

**The Voltage Effect** (List, 2022). Public shaming is this book's named refusal: having reported that telling Dominican taxpayers their penalties would become public record raised revenue by threatening a loss of social standing, the author says he is not suggesting public shaming and threats to reputation belong in a business incentive structure, calls it a recipe for a toxic workplace culture, and says it would likely disincentivize (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 134–136). The clawback is the technique he does recommend, giving the money before it is earned so that it is the worker's to lose, the loss framing beating the identical bonus offered conventionally (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 143–144, 147–148), and it carries stated obligations: a company scaling it is told not to set impossible or unrealistic benchmarks that produce toxic stress, to pay out however many employees earn it, not to make people constantly feel what they are at risk of losing, and that upholding organizational ethics matters more than any voltage gain (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 145–146, 150). Loss aversion has a boundary condition, since people with vast experience in trading assets show very little sign of it and repeated exposure desensitizes (Chapter 6, PDF p. 145), and the extrinsic-versus-intrinsic critique of incentives is granted as valid, with his own finding limited to situations where intrinsic motivation is already very low (Chapter 6, PDF p. 149). An apology is to be used strategically and ideally only after unexpectedly bad outcomes unlikely to repeat, three or more in a short period being worse than none (Chapter 9, PDF p. 212).

**Thinking in Bets** (Duke, 2018). No direct position on moving a customer. Its nearest claim is about not moving the person whose judgment is being asked for: beliefs are contagious, so a person seeking a group's opinion withholds their own conclusion, since telling listeners what the asker believes creates an ideological conflict of interest (ch. 5, PDF p. 134; ch. 6, PDF p. 163), and a child, who cannot consent to a truthseeking exchange, can only be nudged (ch. 5, PDF p. 140).

## Where the books agree

- Proof that is real works: Copyhackers's logo row, Hot Tips's founding year and testimonial, and Thomas's disclosure test all admit a true claim displayed plainly. *Obviously Awesome* joins from the other end, ruling an opinion of one's own value out as proof and leaving third-party validation; *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* wants three short testimonials and a usage statistic; *Lean Marketing* names the honest routes to social proof where there is none; and *This Is Marketing* reads a busy booth, a blurb, and who else is at the launch party as affiliation signals.
- The customer's own words beat the maker's: Copyhackers and Thomas's reactance chapter both describe the shouting page losing. *Forget the Funnel* says the words a customer arrives with are the words to use; *The Mom Test* and *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* both build their interview rules so the founder's words do not become the customer's; *Competing Against Luck* reaches the same place from the job rather than the sentence.
- Framing is unavoidable: Thomas says every frame is a choice; Hot Tips and Copyhackers spend their pages choosing frames. *Obviously Awesome* makes it the whole argument, with the same product framed differently worth a different amount to the same person, and *This Is Marketing* says the price is itself a story.
- Where a new book states a test at all, it puts it on truth or on disclosure rather than on effect: *This Is Marketing* asks that marketer and consumer both know what is happening and both be satisfied; *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* allows drama on the condition that it is true and refuses fearmongering; *Lean Marketing* forbids deceiving and forbids inventing social proof; *Marketing Made Simple* allows a drafted testimonial because it is not making something up and sends it back for approval; *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* separates eliciting from convincing. Thomas's disclosure test and *Zero to One*'s claim that sales works best hidden are the two poles this set sits between.
- A technique is not a substitute for the thing sold: *Zero to One* says branding's surface techniques do not work without underlying substance; *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* says trust-building technology does not replace great products and service; *Company of One* says no incentive creates advocates without a good product first; *Start Small, Stay Small* says selling something poor to a hungry market is not sustainable; *Lean Marketing* says premium positioning is no excuse for poor service.
- Naming who a thing is not for is treated as a legitimate move: *This Is Marketing* offers it as respect rather than rejection, *Competing Against Luck* makes conveying who should not hire a product an obligation, and *Obviously Awesome* says a poor category choice leaves marketing battling assumptions the product does not meet.

## Where the books conflict

- **Urgency and scarcity.** Hot Tips recommends both as tips; Thomas names the mechanisms they exploit as biases and offers the disclosure test, which a manufactured deadline fails. *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* puts scarcity in the sales email's call to action and praises a onetime offer; *Marketing Made Simple* keeps the limited-time offer conditional and puts an expiring bonus in the last email's postscript; *This Is Marketing* agrees the mechanism is manufactured and says so, calling the going-out-of-business sale's logic elusive while still teaching tension as the craft; *The Voltage Effect* reports that a manufactured loss moves people and then declines the version of it that trades on reputation.
- **The favored plan.** Hot Tips's suggested tier and Thomas's contrast effect are the same layout, recommended by one and questioned by the other. *Marketing Made Simple* recommends three prices with the middle one chosen, on practitioner report rather than a study; *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* calls a single pricing option an error and wants a standard and a premium; *Start Small, Stay Small* wants three tiers and every price ending in 7, 8 or 9, a rule it calls stupid and keeps because it works; *Software as a Science* fences features across three plan levels to move accounts up them; *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* makes the Buy Now button a brighter color than every other button on the page. On the other side, *Company of One* argues that fixed, published prices are the trust move and that three mattresses beat 108.
- **Friction.** Thomas argues friction is sometimes good; Hot Tips removes every obstacle to a demo. *Software as a Science* takes the strongest version of Hot Tips's side — a credit card asked for on the call itself because every drop-off point can lose the deal, and self-service cancellation removed so the customer has to phone — while *This Is Marketing*'s permission test, *Competing Against Luck*'s obligation to say who should not hire the product, and *The Mom Test*'s ban on strong-arming all put a deliberate cost back in the path.
- **Who writes the testimonial.** *Marketing Made Simple* drafts one for the customer and sends it back for approval, saying plainly that this is not making something up; *Software as a Science* pre-writes it from the customer's own usage data and coaches the story through situation, struggle and solution; *Lean Marketing*'s first rule is not to be dishonest and invent social proof at all; *Obviously Awesome* rules the seller's own account of its value out as proof. The four draw the same line in four places.
- **Whether the persuasion is visible to the person being persuaded.** *Zero to One* says sales works best hidden and that a person who thinks himself immune to advertising is doubly deceived; *This Is Marketing* makes both sides knowing what is happening the condition of the practice being legitimate; Thomas's test is whether the designer would disclose the reason for the layout. The mechanism is agreed and the visibility requirement is not.
- **Shame and fear.** *This Is Marketing* names shame the status killer and says it is used as a lever because it works, holding the marketer responsible for the impact; *The Voltage Effect* reports the same mechanism working on taxpayers and then refuses it inside a business; *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* rations fear as salt and then says almost nobody will overuse it. The three agree the mechanism works and set three different limits on it.
- **Selling what does not exist yet.** *Start Small, Stay Small* sells a page for an unbuilt product and attaches a "Coming soon" emblem; *Entrepreneur Revolution* allows money to be taken for something not yet secured and requires it be set aside for return; *The Startup Owner's Manual* records investors encouraging a launch of a product that does not exist and names the market bubble as the case where that makes sense, with no disclosure attached; *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* calls promising unique extensions a trap and then allows it as sometimes essential to get an order or two.
- **Evidence.** Thomas cites named studies; Hot Tips cites nothing; Copyhackers reports single-page tests with no controls beyond the A/B itself. *The Voltage Effect* is the one new book built on field experiments and independent replication, and *Thinking in Bets* asks for a stated confidence before the result; against them, *Marketing Made Simple* rests its middle-price finding on what certified guides have discovered, *Software as a Science* on a thousand audited sales calls, and *Start Small, Stay Small* on a rule it admits is arbitrary and keeps because it works.
