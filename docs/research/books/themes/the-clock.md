# Theme synthesis: the clock

## What this file is for

This is the second stage of ADR-0041 for one theme on the values track. It lays out what each of the twenty-nine values books claims about messages sent because time passed rather than because a person acted, whether to a buyer during a trial or to a user inside the product; where the books agree; where they conflict; and what the repo has already decided. It contains no ruling. Page citations follow each read-back's own page rule. The read-backs are in `docs/research/books/`. Where an agreement or conflict bullet names Walling without a title, it means the two 2023 books, *The SaaS Playbook* and *Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding*, which were the only Walling books on the track when the bullet was written; *Start Small, Stay Small* is named by title wherever it is meant. A bare Blank means *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* and Blank and Dorf means *The Startup Owner's Manual*; a bare Miller means *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* and Miller and Peterson means *Marketing Made Simple*; Dib is named by title or as both of his books wherever the two differ.

## The question

When, if ever, does a message go out because a date arrived rather than because someone did something?

## What each book claims

**The SaaS Playbook** (Walling, 2023). Automated emails on a clock: a referral ask at 60 or 90 days, onboarding sequences over the first sixty days, a founder email within ten minutes of a cancellation (80/20 SaaS Metrics, pp. 151, 159, 161).

**Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding** (Walling, 2023 ed.). Trials that never end, zero emails during a trial, and a fifteen-field signup form leak a funnel; trial emails lifted installs from 25% to 75% in the author's own case ("Small Startup Acquisition", pp. 213, 224, 228). An automated follow-up sequence over weeks or months, sent from a mailing service, converts a subscriber ("#1 Goal", pp. 27–29).

**Make: The Bootstrapper's Handbook** (Levels). Push notifications are valuable if used modestly, and a weekly grouped digest is the model (Launch, p. 80).

**The Best Interface Is No Interface** (Krishna, 2015). Automatic solutions are hard to get right, and the ones that work earned trust over a long, careful track record (ch. 18, pp. 278–280). A system that reaches out proactively on a person's inferred pattern, before being asked, is held up as the better model, illustrated by a thermostat that stops asking and a hospital sensor that alerts hours ahead of a standard monitor (ch. 15, pp. 258, 260–261). The book does not reconcile the two.

**Design for Cognitive Bias** (Thomas, 2020). No position on timed messages as such. Its nearest claim is on framing: the same fact framed two ways has a massive influence that supersedes the fact (ch. 2, pp. 34–35), which bears on what any message says rather than when it is sent.

**Jobs To Be Done** (Ulwick, 2016). No position.

**Landing Page Hot Tips.** No position on timed messages. Its time-limited discount (Tip #24, PDF p. 56) is a page element, carried by the persuasion theme.

**Copyhackers: Uplift.** No position.

**The Almanack of Naval Ravikant** (Jorgenson, 2020). No position.

**Obviously Awesome** (Dunford, 2019). No position on timed messages; the only clock in the book is the instruction to re-check the positioning every six months or after a major event (After Positioning, pp. 133–134).

**Building a StoryBrand 2.0** (Miller, 2025 updated edition). A lead generator is followed by an automated email or text drip campaign: three nurture emails, then a sales email, repeated month after month, with some brands never stopping (ch. 15, PDF pp. 198–205). The origin story is automated monthly thank-you letters to a publisher's top two hundred customers, which the author reads as working even unread because the logo crossed the customer's eyes, and a belt seller's near-daily text messages that ran for months before an order arrived (ch. 15, PDF pp. 201–209). A boxed aside puts the industry-standard open rate at 20 percent, says a deleted email still accomplishes the goal, and calls an unsubscribe a good thing (ch. 15, PDF p. 204). Countdown timers and special offers belong to the commitment stage of the campaign (ch. 15, PDF pp. 186–187), a sales email's call to action carries scarcity because the offer is onetime (ch. 15, PDF pp. 207–208), and a clinic is told to tag a waiting-room checklist into an email system so an automated campaign carries the calls to action (ch. 8, PDF pp. 123–124). A limited-time free trial is a transitional call to action that removes risk (ch. 8, PDF p. 123). The repetition arithmetic is that a message is repeated about eighty times for a person to hear it eight (ch. 13, PDF p. 170), and a client's price list gated behind an email address plus thirty weekly emails is credited with renewed growth (ch. 5, PDF pp. 79–80).

**Lean Marketing** (Dib, 2024). Automations are triggered by behavior, elapsed time, tags, and an almost unlimited number of other variables, with a welcome sequence and an abandoned-cart email as the worked examples (Chapter 4, pp. 84–85). Frequency runs between once a week and twice a day, with once a week the recommended minimum, because below it the list decays and produces unsubscribes and spam complaints, since "all relationships deteriorate" without regular contact (Chapter 12, pp. 199–201). Send timing is named as a sixth factor in whether an email is opened (Chapter 12, pp. 196–197). Three email types make the system: short-term welcome sequences, broadcasts for perishable news, and long-term evergreen sequences (Chapter 12, pp. 201–202), with the soap opera sequence a narrative launch sequence in three movements, each ending on a cliffhanger and built on truth (Chapter 12, pp. 203–205). The marketing system itself is a cron-like What/When/Who table with a named owner even for the tasks the CRM fully automates, because "even automated systems can have malfunctions" (Chapter 11, pp. 187–189), and the author's own dead man's switch publishes queued content automatically after "no response from me after 48 hours" (Chapter 11, pp. 189–190). A telecommunications company's no-news update every few days through a multi-week provisioning delay is credited with satisfaction rising sharply (Chapter 14, pp. 247–249). Tools are said to serve "augmentation rather than complete automation" in most cases (Force Multiplier 1 intro, p. 77).

**The 1-Page Marketing Plan** (Dib, 2018 edition). Follow-up grunt work is automated with a CRM and triggered by prospect action, by tracked inquiries and purchases, or by preset timers (Chapter 5, p. 156); the posture is to market to the database until people "buy or die", which the author immediately distinguishes from pestering, the rule being welcome guest rather than pest (Chapter 5, pp. 155–156). Email goes out at least monthly and ideally closer to weekly, at about three value emails to one offer email, with the author stating there are no hard and fast rules on frequency (Chapter 3, pp. 122–124). The forcing mechanism is a marketing calendar of daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly and annual activities beside event-triggered ones, offered because the person who makes marketing recur is the one small businesses lack (Chapter 5, pp. 165–168). Reminders by post, email or SMS are called fully automatable for products whose usefulness expires, and a shoe store's voucher is valid from the day after issue and expires in six months (Chapter 8, pp. 246–248), while a reactivation campaign goes to lapsed customers under themes such as "We Miss You" (Chapter 8, pp. 249–250). The systems chapter's worked checklist is chasing unpaid invoices at 7–13, 14–27 and 27-plus days (Chapter 7, pp. 229–230).

**The Mom Test** (Fitzpatrick, 2013). No position on timed messages; the nearest is an MTV client who called every Friday for a branded report nobody read, where the author's conclusion is that a scheduled email and a hand-built layout would have done, at a cost he puts at three months (ch. 2, pp. 34–36).

**Competing Against Luck** (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan, 2016). The one place the book treats a timed message is Southern New Hampshire University, where a generic email twenty-four hours after an inquiry became a phone call inside ten minutes, on the reasoning that the first institution to speak to a prospective student usually closes (ch. 3, PDF p. 59); the personal adviser who follows is triggered by a red flag, calling after a bad unit test, rather than by a date (ch. 3, PDF p. 60). The general claim behind both is that a process aligned with a job shifts complexity and nuisance from the customer to the vendor, and that the metric follows the job — minutes to respond, delivery rather than shipment (ch. 7, PDF pp. 143–144, 147).

**Forget the Funnel** (Laudi and Suellentrop, 2023). The two projects named for bridging a First Value gap are an in-app checklist and an onboarding email series written from customer responses, which SparkToro ran and which the authors report doubled free-to-paid conversions two months later (ch. 8, PDF pp. 110–111); the Growth-phase table adds win-back emails, continuous education, and solicited reviews and referrals (ch. 8, PDF pp. 113–114). A rewritten nurture sequence at CareerCake is credited with cutting the average time to book a sales call from a month to three days (ch. 8, PDF pp. 114–115). Against that sits the book's argument about what a metric does to behavior: measuring engagement by daily logins at FeedOtter, a tool whose value is not having to log in, would push the team to nudge customers back into a product they bought to avoid (ch. 7, PDF pp. 96–97).

**Marketing Made Simple** (Miller and Peterson, 2020). Nurture emails are the drip campaign, at least one a week, for as long as it takes: the authors say a customer might receive weekly emails for seven years before buying (ch. 7, p. 127; ch. 8, pp. 131–132). Three sequence shapes are given — weekly announcements, weekly tips, weekly notifications — and the podcast email's real value is named as the excuse to email the list every week (ch. 8, pp. 134–143). The argument for consistency is timing: people buy when they are ready rather than when the seller is ready, so the seller is still present when that happens (ch. 8, p. 131). Unopened email is defended as branding, since a name seen while swiping to delete still registers, and the unsubscribe button is what makes frequent sending guilt-free (ch. 8, p. 132). The sales campaign is six emails days apart whether or not the recipient has done anything, run for about a week and then merged back into the nurture campaign, closing on a plain ask with a limited-time offer in the postscript, because a deadline forces action (ch. 9, pp. 147–151). About eight touchpoints precede an order for most products, and reaching a person eight times may take dozens of sends, with cheaper products needing fewer (ch. 2, pp. 27–28).

**This Is Marketing** (Godin, 2018). Permission is the privilege of "anticipated, personal, and relevant" messages, and it is converted drip by drip, day by day, message by message (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 152-154). What fixes the frequency is the promise made when permission was given, kept until both sides agree to change it, whatever the quarter, product launch or campaign; a retailer's daily sales pitch is permitted because it was promised, permission requires no formality only obviousness, and different people can be given different formats and frequencies (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 153-154). The metric proposed is how many people would reach out or complain if the next send did not go out, and real permission is distinguished from having an address, an absence of complaints, or fine print in a privacy policy (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 153-154). The fifth step of the book's own method is showing up consistently and generously for years (Ch. 2, PDF p. 21), and the Open Heart Project's closing reasons include showing up often (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 40-41). A subscription is named an overt act of permission, which is why a home-delivery reader is worth more than a newsstand buyer (Ch. 17, PDF p. 154).

**Start Small, Stay Small** (Walling, 2010). List content is relevant information every two to four weeks, ideally as an autoresponder series that new subscribers receive from the beginning, with broadcasts kept for time-sensitive news (Ch. 4, pp. 138–140, 143–144). Seven sending rules include testing send times, with Tuesday through Thursday between 7am and 10:30am as the general rule, and one sales message every fourth or fifth email (Ch. 4, pp. 141–143). The Ideal Launch is a clock in five parts: a home page six months out trading a deal for an email address, six months of buzz, an email to the list a week before launch promising a 48-hour price, another on launch day, and a final 12-hour reminder 36 hours in, at a conversion the author puts above 20% (Ch. 4, pp. 143–144). Reminder emails and monthly billing are named among the things left unautomated before launch, done by a person until the product proves itself, because an hour spent writing code is wasted if a person can do the same task (Ch. 6, pp. 168–169).

**Software as a Science** (Martell, Verlaque, Page, and Petitpas, 2024). Guardrail emails fire on events rather than on days elapsed since signup, the message changing by whether the user is on or off track, and phone numbers are collected because configured text outreach is put at roughly triple the deliverability of email (ch. 6, PDF pp. 141–142). A customer health index lets a company reach an account whose usage is slipping before the customer knows anything is wrong, with trigger emails set at a threshold such as a task taking longer than it used to (ch. 7, PDF pp. 150, 158, 161); the authors also record that calling a few dozen red accounts will surface people who had been meaning to cancel (ch. 7, PDF p. 163). The Win/Ask method times an ask to the moment the company's own data shows a customer winning — a review as soon as a merchant makes a first dollar, a referral ask when a list passes a thousand — and UpLaunch emailed every client at their first fifty leads (ch. 8, PDF pp. 176, 182). The lead magnet's delivery email opens an email and phone nurture sequence (ch. 4, PDF pp. 93–94). Where the book does put a message on a calendar it is the price increase, which names how long the customer has been there and gives six months before the new price starts (ch. 10, PDF pp. 223–224).

**The Lean Startup** (Ries, 2011). No position on timed messages to a buyer or a user; the nearest are Grockit mailing every employee the day's experiment results in plain English (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 142-143) and Food on the Table's chief executive and vice-president of product visiting one customer weekly with a printed packet before any software existed (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 100-103).

**The Four Steps to the Epiphany** (Blank, 2013 second edition). No position on timed messages; the nearest is the discovery cadence of ten calls a day until three visits a day are booked, where fifty follow-up calls yield five to ten visits (Chapter 3, pp. 180–186).

**The Startup Owner's Manual** (Blank and Dorf, 2012). No position on timed messages; the nearest is the Get/Keep/Grow funnel, in which retention is held to be far cheaper than acquisition and the Keep and Grow funnels are drawn separately (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 302-349; Physical Channel, PDF pp. 800-808).

**Zero to One** (Thiel with Masters, 2014). No position on timed messages; the nearest is PayPal's fraud system, which stopped working while it decided alone and worked once the software flagged the most suspicious transactions for a human operator to make the final judgment (ch. 12, PDF p. 110).

**Company of One** (Jarvis, 2019). An automated email a week after purchase carrying prewritten text is credited with doubling sharing on one of the author's products, and the referral pitch in that worked automation goes only to customers who rated their enjoyment above a seven (ch. 10, PDF pp. 119–120). For a service business the move offered is a follow-up schedule weeks after a project ends, when results exist to describe (ch. 10, PDF p. 120). Email is the scalable connection channel because the same effort sends to one person or fifty thousand (ch. 8, PDF pp. 101–102), and one designer's automated onboarding cut a lead-to-project cycle from eight or sixteen hours to one (ch. 8, PDF p. 103). The same chapter states two limits: turning a system on and watching profit arrive is likened to planting a money tree, personality is still required inside automated communication, and direct one-to-one communication with customers is called always required (ch. 8, PDF pp. 101–102). Growth hacking is the counter-case, where Glide's default contact-list invitations and The Circle's contact-list blasts were each followed by collapse (ch. 12, PDF pp. 141–142).

**Buy Back Your Time** (Martell, 2023). No position on a message to a buyer or a user; the nearest are a Playbook's Cadence section, which records how often each task runs and may be unnecessary in a one-task Playbook (ch. 7, PDF p. 141), and the sync meeting the author holds every other day on a repeat agenda (ch. 9, PDF pp. 169–171).

**Entrepreneur Revolution** (Priestley, 2024 third edition). The servicing process after the core sale is told to undersell strategically, hold surprises back, and never send a half-hearted newsletter, because a mediocre attempt at delight annoys rather than delights (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 122–125). A waiting list is the first of four campaigns that test whether an audience exists before anything is built (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 95–96), and the founder's own year is written holidays first, on three grounds of which one is that a departure date creates a deadline (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 71–73).

**The Voltage Effect** (List, 2022). The one randomized body of evidence on the track about a recurring message is Opower's Home Energy Report: across thirty-eight field experiments sent to some 250,000 homes, consumers cut electricity use by 2.4 percent on average, and 35 to 55 percent of the reduction persisted after the reports stopped (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 139–140). In the same passage one-time incentives are named as easier to scale than continuous messaging, "which goes stale" (Chapter 6, PDF p. 140). Virgin Atlantic's monthly fuel-efficiency reports to captains improved all three treatment groups and the control group too, which the author attributes to the Hawthorne effect, with targets plus encouragement producing the largest gain and a charity donation adding nothing (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 136–139). A reward delayed by one month had no effect at all (Chapter 6, PDF p. 148). The same Opower letters carry the book's generalization warning: 111 randomized controlled trials across 8.6 million households still failed to generalize, because utilities in environmentalist areas were likelier to take part (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 60–61). Early Head Start's twice-monthly home visits are the program-drift case, where at-risk parents had less time and the visits drifted from the method (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 79–80).

**Thinking in Bets** (Duke, 2018). No position on timed messages; the nearest is the classical result that a variable reinforcement schedule makes a rat's lever-pressing far harder to extinguish than a fixed one, offered as an illustration of how uncertainty slows learning (ch. 3, PDF pp. 75–76).

## Where the books agree

- Every business book on the track that addresses the question treats a timed sequence as the mechanism that converts a person who has not yet bought: Walling in both of his books, Miller in *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* and with Peterson in *Marketing Made Simple*, Dib in both of his, Godin, Jarvis, Laudi and Suellentrop, and Martell and his coauthors for the sequence that follows a lead magnet. Each of their figures comes from a single case — Walling's 25%-to-75%, Jarvis's doubled sharing, SparkToro's doubled free-to-paid conversion, CareerCake's month cut to three days — and List's is the only randomized set on the track.
- Levels, Krishna, Dib, Jarvis and Thiel agree that unattended automation is dangerous in proportion to how much of it there is: Levels's "modestly", Krishna's "long, careful track record", Dib's named owner for every task the CRM fully automates, Jarvis's insistence that personality still belongs inside automated communication and that one-to-one contact is always required, and Thiel's fraud system that worked only once a person judged what the software flagged.
- **The clock is defended for presence rather than for being read.** Miller and Peterson say the seller is still there when the buyer becomes ready, and that unopened mail still brands; Miller says a deleted email accomplishes the goal; Dib says a list decays without regular contact; Godin's proposed metric is how many people would complain if the next send did not go out. None of them rests the case on the message being read.
- **Where an act exists, several books prefer it to a date.** Martell and his coauthors fire guardrail emails on events rather than on days elapsed; Christensen and his coauthors replace a twenty-four-hour email with a call inside ten minutes and trigger the adviser on a red flag; Laudi and Suellentrop warn that a login metric turns a team into a nudger of people who bought the product to stop logging in. Dib lists elapsed time and behavior side by side as triggers without preferring either.

## Where the books conflict

- **Who the message is for.** Walling's sequences reach a buyer in a trial; Krishna's examples reach a person the system already serves; Levels's digest reaches an installed user. The new books add two more cases: a customer already paying, inside the product, where Martell and his coauthors place activation guardrails and health-index outreach and Laudi and Suellentrop place Continued Value; and a subscriber who has bought nothing and may never, where Godin's permission list, Walling's autoresponder and Miller and Peterson's seven years of weekly email sit. No book distinguishes the five cases, and their advice differs by case.
- **Date or act.** Miller and Peterson's six sales emails go out days apart whether or not the recipient has done anything; Dib's welcome sequence runs an email immediately, a task at twenty-four hours and an educational email at forty-eight; Walling's autoresponder puts every subscriber through the same two-to-four-week sequence from signup; and Dib's own dead man's switch reads forty-eight hours of his silence as approval to publish. Martell and his coauthors state the opposite rule outright, changing the message by whether the user is on or off track rather than by days since signup, and Christensen and his coauthors and Laudi and Suellentrop argue the same way from the other end.
- **What sets the interval.** Godin says the promise made when permission was given, whatever the campaign; Dib names a floor of once a week, because below it the list decays; Miller and Peterson say at least weekly and make the number of touches a function of the product's price; Walling says every two to four weeks; Priestley says only when there is something worth sending, since a half-hearted newsletter annoys rather than delights. The numbers disagree and so do the reasons behind them.
- **Whether an unnecessary send still pays.** Miller's monthly letters are said to work even unread and his boxed aside calls a deleted email a success; List finds that continuous messaging goes stale and that one-time incentives are easier to scale, and that a reward delayed by a month did nothing at all; Priestley treats a mediocre send as worse than none.
- **Proactive versus earned.** Krishna argues for a system that acts before being asked (ch. 15) and, thirteen chapters later, for one that earns the right to act over years (ch. 18). Martell and his coauthors join the first side, reaching an account before the customer knows anything is wrong, while recording that calling red accounts surfaces people who had been meaning to cancel.
- **Evidence.** Walling's figure is one product of his own with no control; Levels offers none; Krishna's examples are a thermostat and a hospital monitor, neither a SaaS. Miller's, Dib's, Jarvis's, and Laudi and Suellentrop's figures are each a single seller's own campaign, likewise with no control. List's Opower and Virgin Atlantic sets are the only randomized evidence about a recurring message anywhere on the track, and his own reading of the larger one is that 111 trials across 8.6 million households still did not generalize, while the Virgin Atlantic control group improved without receiving the reports at all.

## What the repo already decided

- ADR-0038: every outbox is nudged by an act, and a due date passing is not an act; an Invoice's overdue state is derived at read time, stored nowhere, and notifies nobody; the Client-facing label stays "Not yet paid".
- ADR-0035: a scheduled sweep for an unconnected Stripe account was rejected in favor of a Staff member's own act.
- ADR-0031, decided on #871: an Owner deletes the Practice herself through a block-over-warn confirmation, the window runs thirty days with a restore screen and a reminder at seven days, and finalization is automatic "with no second confirmation".
- ADR-0028: the shell has no notification bell; unfinished work appears in a list and never as a badge that pulses.
- ADR-0002: a Web Push notification carries no content; it wakes the service worker, which fetches the message from the BFF.
- ADR-0014: the waitlist lives in Buttondown, outside the stack; it sends one confirmation carrying the pilot-group offer and broadcasts once in January 2027; Kit and Drip were weighed and rejected as funnel automation.
- The **Credit** entry in `CONTEXT.md`: Credits do not expire. `api/internal/billing/dormancy.go` holds a two-year dormancy notice whose comment cites New York's APL 1315(1-b) escheat deadline as the source of the interval.
- #1266, which asked what a Practice with untouched signup Credits hears after two years of silence, was closed as not planned on 2026-09-10; a not-planned ticket decides nothing.
- Open: #361, the confirmation email and how a person says yes to the pilot; #1003, the channel plan through January.
