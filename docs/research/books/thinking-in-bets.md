## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that exactly two things determine how a life turns out, decision quality and luck, and that learning to tell them apart is what thinking in bets is about (Introduction, PDF p. 11). A bet, on the broad dictionary definition the author adopts, is a choice among alternatives made under uncertainty with something at risk, so every decision is a bet, usually not against another person but against the other future versions of oneself that were not chosen (ch. 2, PDF pp. 43–45). Because a bet's quality is set before the cards fall, an unwanted result does not make the decision wrong, and the author's name for the error of reading decision quality off outcome quality is "resulting" (ch. 1, PDF pp. 13–14). The remedies the book offers are four: get comfortable saying one is not sure and express belief as a percentage or a range (ch. 2, PDF pp. 62–63); sort each outcome deliberately into a luck bucket or a skill bucket rather than by what flatters (ch. 3, PDF p. 73); recruit a small group that has agreed in advance to accuracy, accountability, and dissent (ch. 4, PDF pp. 105–106); and recruit past and future versions of oneself through scenario planning, precommitment, and premortems (ch. 6, PDF pp. 141–142). The stated promise is modest: catching a few extra learning opportunities compounds, the way a one-degree navigation error compounds across an ocean (ch. 3, PDF pp. 96–97).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a poker book, not about poker strategy or gambling, though it is about what poker taught her regarding learning and decision-making (Introduction, PDF p. 11). It is not a claim that the method made her rational: she says thinking in bets did not turn her into a permanently rational, unemotional decision-maker, and that errors, feelings, and losses come with being human (Introduction, PDF p. 11). It is not a cure: the author writes that the method leaves self-serving bias and motivated reasoning in place and only makes them a little better (ch. 3, PDF p. 96). It is not a survey of behavioral economics, which it summarizes in a few pages and then sends the reader to the bibliography for (ch. 1, PDF pp. 16–17). And it is not a defense of certainty in any form: the author's own position is that nearly nothing is 0% or 100% (ch. 2, PDF p. 61).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction: Why This Isn't a Poker Book (PDF pp. 9–11)

The author left a doctoral program in cognitive psychology at the University of Pennsylvania, started playing poker in Billings, Montana, and stayed twenty years, retiring in 2012 with a World Series of Poker bracelet, the WSOP Tournament of Champions, the NBC National Heads-Up Championship, and more than $4 million in tournament earnings; her brother Howard had by then won over $6.4 million (Introduction, PDF pp. 9–10). The argument for poker as the evidence base is its density and its feedback: a hand takes about two minutes and can carry up to twenty decisions, each ending in money won or lost, though she cautions that the feedback is awkward, since who won a hand indicates decision quality only loosely (Introduction, PDF p. 10). A 2002 speaking invitation that Erik Seidel turned down took her to a group of traders, and from there to professional audiences in finance, strategic planning, human resources, law, and entrepreneurship (Introduction, PDF pp. 10–11).

### Chapter 1: Life Is Poker, Not Chess (PDF pp. 12–37)

The opening case is Pete Carroll's second-down pass call at the New England one-yard line in Super Bowl XLIX, with twenty-six seconds left and the Seahawks trailing by four; the pass was intercepted, and the book reproduces five hostile headlines from USA Today, the Washington Post, FoxSports.com, the Seattle Times, and the New Yorker (ch. 1, PDF p. 12). Two dissenting analyses, Benjamin Morris's on FiveThirtyEight and Brian Burke's on Slate, argued the call was defensible on clock management, and noted that of sixty-six passes thrown from an opponent's one-yard line that season, none had been intercepted, with a rate of about 2% over the previous fifteen seasons (ch. 1, PDF p. 13). The author's explanation for the reaction is one word, "resulting" — equating decision quality with outcome quality — and she notes Carroll conceding on the Today show four days later: "worst result of a call ever" (ch. 1, PDF pp. 13–14). Her consulting exercise supplies the second case: asked for their best and worst decisions of the year, executives name their best and worst results, and the CEO who called firing his company president his worst decision turns out, on the details, to have run a careful process and drawn a bad outcome; his companion error is hindsight bias, which the book defines as reading an outcome, once known, as something that could not have gone otherwise (ch. 1, PDF pp. 14–16).

The mechanism offered is evolutionary and cognitive. Brains evolved to create order, and Michael Shermer's account of prehistoric pattern-finding makes a false positive far cheaper than a false negative, so seeking certainty was adaptive (ch. 1, PDF p. 17). Kahneman's System 1 and System 2 are cited, alongside Gary Marcus's preferred labels of reflexive and deliberative mind, with automatic processing seated in older structures and deliberation in the prefrontal cortex; Colin Camerer told the author in an interview that the deliberative layer is already overtaxed, so the fix cannot be to route more decisions through it (ch. 1, PDF pp. 17–18). The Müller-Lyer illusion is the analogy: knowing about a bias does not dissolve it, and the practical answer is to carry a ruler (ch. 1, PDF pp. 19–20). Poker is offered as the laboratory because its decisions are dense, timed, and costly — about thirty hands an hour, and a player who slows down can have the clock called and get seventy seconds (ch. 1, PDF p. 21).

The chess comparison carries the chapter's title. John von Neumann built game theory on a stripped-down poker rather than on chess, and Jacob Bronowski's account of a London taxi ride records von Neumann's insistence that chess is a well-defined computation while real games involve bluffing and deception (ch. 1, PDF pp. 22–24). Chess has no hidden information and little luck, so outcomes track decision quality closely; poker has both, so resulting is a reasonable learning strategy in chess and a bad one in poker or life (ch. 1, PDF pp. 25–26). The Princess Bride's poisoned goblets and a four-flip coin question make the same point about incomplete information: the arithmetic answer of 6.25% assumes facts about the coin nobody has, and the correct answer is that one is not sure (ch. 1, PDF pp. 26–28). The chapter closes on redefining wrong. A prediction that is not 0% or 100% cannot be wrong merely because the likelier future failed to arrive; the author's charity-tournament audience learned this across two hands, at 76–24 and 82–18, and she extends it to the Brexit bookmakers, to Alan Dershowitz's September 2016 claim that the bookies got it wrong, and to Nate Silver, whom she says had Trump between 30% and 40% in the week before the 2016 election (ch. 1, PDF pp. 32–34). Giving up the feeling of being right is the price, and she argues it is worth paying: even a strong player loses more than 40% of eight-hour sessions, and Kahneman and Tversky's loss aversion means a loss feels about twice as bad as an equivalent gain feels good (ch. 1, PDF p. 36).

### Chapter 2: Wanna Bet? (PDF pp. 38–65)

The chapter opens with John Hennigan, a Las Vegas professional who accepted a $30,000 wager to live for a month on one street in Des Moines with a golf course as his only concession, called a friend after two days, and paid $15,000 to get out (ch. 2, PDF pp. 38–40). The author's point is that the underlying analysis was ordinary: two mutually exclusive futures, each with quantifiable and unquantifiable value, and opportunity cost in whichever was rejected — the same structure as a job offer, a relocation, or an employer's compensation package (ch. 2, PDF pp. 41–42). She then widens the definition of a bet to Merriam-Webster's, emphasizing choice, probability, risk, decision, and belief, and concludes that not placing a bet is itself a bet (ch. 2, PDF p. 43). Most bets are against the future versions of ourselves we did not choose (ch. 2, PDF pp. 44–45).

Bets are only as good as the beliefs under them, and the chapter's second half argues that belief formation is haphazard. Daniel Gilbert's 1991 review and his 1993 experiments with color-coded true and false statements found that subjects under time pressure or cognitive load erred in one direction only, treating statements as true regardless of their labels, so the real sequence is hear, believe, and only sometimes vet (ch. 2, PDF pp. 48–49). Examples of unvetted belief include the maternal-grandfather baldness myth and the dog-years multiplier, and, at stake, the suited-connector mantra among her poker students and the low-fat diet, whose cost David Ludwig summarized in JAMA (ch. 2, PDF pp. 47–51). Beliefs then resist correction: Johnson and Seifert's 1994 warehouse-fire experiments found subjects still blaming burning paint after being told the closet was empty (ch. 2, PDF pp. 51–52). Hastorf and Cantril's 1954 study of the Dartmouth–Princeton football game of November 23, 1951, found Princeton students seeing Dartmouth commit twice as many flagrant and three times as many mild infractions while Dartmouth students saw the teams as even; Dan Kahan and colleagues reproduced the pattern in 2012 with an edited video of a protest labeled differently for two groups (ch. 2, PDF pp. 52–54). The author names the loop motivated reasoning, connects it to fake news and disinformation and to Eli Pariser's 2011 filter bubble, and reports two findings that intelligence does not rescue: West, Meserve, and Stanovich's 2012 work found bias blind spots larger in more cognitively sophisticated participants in six of seven biases tested, and Kahan found that on a politically charged version of the same data task, the more numerate subjects made more mistakes than less numerate subjects sharing their politics (ch. 2, PDF pp. 55–58).

The remedy is the chapter's title. A challenge to bet triggers the vetting step, and the book prints a list of questions it prompts — where the information came from, how good the source is, how current it is, what the challenger knows (ch. 2, PDF pp. 59–60). The author does not suggest actually wagering with people; the point is to adopt the frame (ch. 2, PDF pp. 60–61). Confidence is then redefined as a quantity rather than a state: rate a belief zero to ten, or give a range of plausible alternatives, which tightens as knowledge grows and as luck recedes (ch. 2, PDF pp. 62–63). Samuel Arbesman's *The Half-Life of Facts* supplies the warrant — the coelacanth, and a Queensland pair's list of 187 mammals declared extinct in five hundred years, more than a third since rediscovered (ch. 2, PDF p. 61). The claimed benefits are four: smaller, less painful updates instead of reversals; less self-judgment; more credibility, because a stated 80% signals thoughtfulness; and an invitation to others to contribute what they know. Science is the model, with p-values standing in for the zero-to-ten scale and confidence intervals for the range (ch. 2, PDF pp. 63–65).

### Chapter 3: Bet to Learn: Fielding the Unfolding Future (PDF pp. 66–97)

Nick the Greek, a Billings hotel manager in the 1992 Crystal Lounge game, believed a pair of aces was the worst hand because everyone expects it and seven-deuce the best because nobody does; he lost steadily and never revised (ch. 3, PDF pp. 66–68). The author's puzzle is that poker should be an ideal learning environment by the textbook criterion of fast, tightly coupled feedback, and is not: experience is necessary to expertise but not sufficient (ch. 3, PDF p. 68). Her model is a loop. A decision is a bet on a future; the future unfolds as outcomes; and deciding why an outcome happened is itself a bet, which she calls fielding, after an outfielder deciding where to throw (ch. 3, PDF pp. 69–73). Fielding is hard because a single outcome has multiple possible causes — the diagnostic cough, the two salespeople with different order sizes, the poker hand that ends with no cards shown (ch. 3, PDF pp. 70–71). The SnackWell's case is the worked example: weight gain on a low-fat cookie could be fielded as evidence the belief was wrong or as bad metabolic luck, and only twenty years of later research makes the answer look obvious (ch. 3, PDF pp. 74–75). Uncertainty itself slows learning, as in the classical result that a variable reinforcement schedule makes a rat's lever-pressing far harder to extinguish than a fixed one (ch. 3, PDF pp. 75–76).

Fielding errors are patterned, not random. Self-serving bias credits good outcomes to skill and blames bad ones on luck; Fritz Heider described us as studying our outcomes like "naïve scientists", looking for a reason that also flatters (ch. 3, PDF p. 77). The evidence offered is Robert MacCoun's study of auto-accident accounts, in which 75% of victims blamed someone else, 91% did in multi-vehicle accidents, and 37% still did in single-vehicle accidents, plus a set of insurance-form excuses and von Neumann's own account of a tree stepping into his path (ch. 3, PDF p. 77). Phil Hellmuth's on-camera line about winning every tournament if not for luck is the poker instance, and Chris Christie's January 2016 debate answer — accusing Hillary Clinton of the pattern, then displaying it — is the political one (ch. 3, PDF pp. 78–79). Learning by watching others fails for a mirror reason: we blame their bad outcomes on them and credit their good ones to luck, as Jean Cocteau's line about explaining the success of those we dislike has it, and as 40,000 people at Wrigley Field did to Steve Bartman in 2003 while ignoring the shortstop's error two batters later (ch. 3, PDF pp. 82–85). The author ties this to comparison: Sonja Lyubomirsky's summary of the literature puts objective circumstances, demographics, and life events together at 8% to 15% of the variation in how happy people are, at most, and the party-game question about earning $70,000 in 1900 rather than now — against an average income of about $450 and a life expectancy of forty-seven against over seventy-six — shows how much of the rest is relative standing (ch. 3, PDF pp. 87–88).

The fix is habit substitution rather than willpower. Charles Duhigg's golden rule of habit change — keep the cue and the reward, change the routine — is applied so that the good feeling comes from being the best credit-giver and mistake-admitter rather than from being right (ch. 3, PDF pp. 89–90). Phil Ivey is the exemplar, spending the dinner after a half-million-dollar win deconstructing his own possible errors, and Mia Hamm's and Phil Mickelson's practice habits are the sporting parallel, against the PGA ritual of staring at the green after a missed putt (ch. 3, PDF pp. 88–91). Competitiveness is not fought but redirected: be better than peers at giving credit and admitting mistakes (ch. 3, PDF p. 91). Treating each fielding call as a bet is the trigger, because it forces alternative hypotheses and perspective taking — ask what you would conclude if a peer's outcome had happened to you, and land in the middle (ch. 3, PDF pp. 93–94). The claimed payoff is compounding: catching ten of a hundred learning opportunities still misses ninety and still beats an opponent catching none, and a one-degree navigation error on a crossing to London is the image (ch. 3, PDF pp. 96–97).


### Chapter 4: The Buddy System (PDF pp. 98–122)

David Letterman's October 2008 question to Lauren Conrad — whether she might be the problem — is treated as a correct insight offered in the wrong forum, to someone who had not agreed to that kind of exchange; the author coins "Lettermanning" for it and reports doing the same thing to a player who wanted sympathy rather than analysis (ch. 4, PDF pp. 98–100). Truthseeking is therefore framed as a choice, on the model of the red pill in *The Matrix*, that has to be freely made to hold (ch. 4, PDF pp. 100–101). Her own group was her brother's circle of New York players, and Erik Seidel laid out its rules in three sentences during a tournament break: no interest in a hard-luck story, unlimited interest in a strategy question (ch. 4, PDF pp. 106–107). A group of three is enough — two to disagree and one to referee — and not every friendship has to convert (ch. 4, PDF p. 103).

The charter is the chapter's contribution. Philip Tetlock and Jennifer Lerner's 2002 paper distinguishes confirmatory thought, a one-sided attempt to rationalize, from exploratory thought, even-handed consideration of alternatives, and states the conditions under which open-minded thought is activated: accountability, known in advance, to an audience whose views are unknown, who is interested in accuracy, who is reasonably well informed, and who has a legitimate reason to ask for reasons (ch. 4, PDF pp. 104–105). Jonathan Haidt's *The Righteous Mind* adds diversity as the missing ingredient, and the author reduces the three to a charter: accuracy over confirmation, accountability with advance notice, and openness to diverse ideas (ch. 4, PDF pp. 105–106). The rewards are social. Groups like Alcoholics Anonymous hand out tokens for intervals as short as twenty-four hours, and the author's own group paid her in engagement for admitting mistakes and withheld it when she complained about luck (ch. 4, PDF pp. 104, 108). Accountability is illustrated by a $200 side bet on Ira the Whale eating a hundred White Castle burgers, and, more usefully, by a preset $600 loss limit she was much likelier to respect because she would have to explain breaking it (ch. 4, PDF pp. 110–111).

Diversity is argued from John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* and then from institutions and data. The State Department's Dissent Channel, created after Vietnam, drew fifty-one signatures on a Syria memo in June 2016 and roughly a thousand on a cable about the January 2017 immigration order; the American Foreign Service Association gives four annual awards for constructive dissent; the CIA built red teams after September 11, and red-team analysis was cited in the bin Laden raid (ch. 4, PDF pp. 113–114). Cass Sunstein's study, covering more than 6,000 appeals in the federal circuits and close to 20,000 separate votes, found large disciplining effects from a single panelist appointed by the other party: in environmental cases, Democratic appointees who voted for plaintiffs 43% of the time overall did so 10% of the time on an otherwise Republican panel, and Republican appointees who voted for plaintiffs 20% of the time overall did so 42% of the time on an otherwise Democratic panel (ch. 4, PDF p. 115). Supreme Court clerk hiring is the counter-trend, with Justice Powell's practice of hiring liberal clerks giving way to Justice Thomas hiring 84 clerks in a row from Republican-appointed judges (ch. 4, PDF pp. 116–117). Social psychology is the third case: 85% to 96% of surveyed members left of center, a ratio that moved from 4-to-1 in the 1990s to more than 10-to-1, and about 10% of faculty against 2% of graduate students identifying as conservative, which is the drift Heterodox Academy was founded to fight and documented in its 2015 *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* paper (ch. 4, PDF pp. 118–120). The chapter's last exhibit is a betting market: Anna Dreber and colleagues had experts judge whether forty-four psychology studies would replicate, and the same experts trading in a prediction market were right 71% of the time against 58% through traditional peer review; General Electric, Google, Microsoft, Eli Lilly, Pfizer, and Siemens are the firms named as running prediction markets (ch. 4, PDF pp. 121–122).

### Chapter 5: Dissent to Win (PDF pp. 123–140)

Robert K. Merton, born in 1910 and briefly a teenage magician, supplies the group's rules of engagement through the CUDOS norms, which the Heterodox Academy authors had cited: communism in the sense of communal data, universalism, disinterestedness, and organized skepticism (ch. 5, PDF pp. 123–124). Communism becomes a rule of more is more: share every detail, especially the ones there is an urge to leave out, because hesitation is itself a signal; Richard Feynman's 1974 lecture on leaning over backwards is the ideal, and the Rashomon Effect is the warning that no single account is complete (ch. 5, PDF pp. 125–126). The author's own consultation with the CEO is offered as the demonstration, since the conclusion changed only after every detail came out, and John Madden's eight hours listening to Vince Lombardi on a single running play is the illustration of how much detail an expert will supply (ch. 5, PDF pp. 127–128).

Universalism is stated as "don't shoot the message": a claim's merit is independent of who carries it, in both directions, so on the book's account a disliked source does not discount an idea and a liked one does not float it (ch. 5, PDF pp. 128–129). Her own violation was treating her brother's starting-hand list as complete and dismissing anyone who played off it, and her exercise was to find something each dismissed player did well; the political version is reading the other side's sources looking for points of agreement (ch. 5, PDF pp. 129–130). A further technique is to withhold the source when putting an idea to the group (ch. 5, PDF p. 131). Disinterestedness is argued from the 1967 *New England Journal of Medicine* review by three Harvard scientists that blamed fat and exonerated sugar, which an article in *JAMA Internal Medicine* in September 2016 showed had been paid for by a sugar trade group; the journal did not require disclosure until 1984 (ch. 5, PDF pp. 131–132). The remedy generalizes as outcome blindness: Robert MacCoun and Saul Perlmutter reported in *Nature* in 2015 that blind analysis has spread through particle physics and cosmology, and the author's group habit was to describe a hand up to the decision point and stop (ch. 5, PDF pp. 132–134). Beliefs are contagious as well as outcomes, so she argues for withholding one's own conclusion when asking for an opinion, and for a referee who makes two disagreeing members argue each other's position (ch. 5, PDF pp. 134–135). Organized skepticism is presented as compatible with good manners: dissent expressed as a probability rather than a contradiction, red teams and anonymous dissent channels as the institutional form, and the devil's advocate as its origin in the canonization process (ch. 5, PDF pp. 135–137). For people outside the group, four techniques are offered — express uncertainty, lead with assent and continue with "and" rather than "but" on the improv model, ask whether the person wants to vent or wants advice, and focus on the future, since it is harder to get defensive about what has not happened yet (ch. 5, PDF pp. 137–140).

### Chapter 6: Adventures in Mental Time Travel (PDF pp. 141–182)

The chapter inverts the *Back to the Future* rule against meeting yourself: in decision-making, colliding with past and future versions of oneself is the goal (ch. 6, PDF pp. 141–142). Jerry Seinfeld's Night Guy and Morning Guy are the frame for temporal discounting, whose adult exhibit is the 1990s military drawdown, in which tens of thousands of service members took lump-sum payments totaling $2.5 billion at a 40% discount to the present value of the annuities (ch. 6, PDF pp. 143–144). Imagining the future recruits the same pathways as remembering the past, including the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex (ch. 6, PDF pp. 144–145). Age-progression tools are the applied case — Prudential Retirement, AARP, and Bank of America Merrill Lynch's Merrill Edge "Face Retirement", released for the web in 2012 and for mobile in 2014 — resting on work by Jeremy Bailenson and Laura Carstensen in which subjects shown a present-day avatar allocated an average of $73.90 of a hypothetical $1,000 to retirement, while subjects shown an age-progressed avatar allocated $178.10 (ch. 6, PDF pp. 145–146). The Center for Retirement Research at Boston College is cited for roughly half of working households being unable to maintain their standard of living, against a shortfall the author gives as $6.8 to $14 trillion (ch. 6, PDF p. 145).

The chapter's organizing move is to relocate regret. Nietzsche's view of remorse as a second act of stupidity and Thoreau's praise of regret are set against each other, and the resolution offered is to move regret in front of the decision so it can change one (ch. 6, PDF p. 147). Suzy Welch's 10-10-10 — the consequences in ten minutes, ten months, and ten years — is the tool, and the author extends it backward as well, asking how a decision made ten months ago would feel today; her poker application was a six-to-eight-hour session limit alongside the loss limit (ch. 6, PDF pp. 148–149). Perspective is the second theme: a flat tire in the rain feels like the worst moment of a life and would not register on a year's happiness, and Berkshire Hathaway's chart since 1964 looks nothing like a random day in January 2017 or the banking crisis window of September 2008 to March 2009 (ch. 6, PDF pp. 149–153). Fielding is also path dependent — a night of blackjack that ends $100 ahead after an early $1,000 win feels worse than one that ends $100 down after an early $1,000 loss (ch. 6, PDF pp. 154–155). Tilt, borrowed from the pinball sensor, is the name for decisions made while the limbic system has shut down the prefrontal cortex, and it has verbal and physiological tells a person and a group can learn to watch for (ch. 6, PDF pp. 156–158).

Precommitment comes next, under the name Ulysses contract, with examples running from a ride-sharing app to an automatic retirement allocation to an investment advisor's pre-agreed conditions; the author stresses that few of these are tamper-proof and that the point is the decision-interrupt rather than the barrier (ch. 6, PDF pp. 158–161). The decision swear jar is a list of phrases that signal irrationality — expressions of certainty, absolutes, conclusions of luck or blame, sweeping characterizations of people, markers of motivated reasoning and echo chambers, and the word "wrong", which she calls a conclusion rather than a rationale (ch. 6, PDF pp. 161–163). Reconnaissance is the forward half of the work: enumerate the possible futures, then estimate their probabilities, on the grounds that a decision already embeds a prediction, so the choice is between an explicit guess and an implicit one (ch. 6, PDF pp. 164–166). D-Day and the bin Laden raid are the military instances and Nate Silver's February 2017 piece the civilian one (ch. 6, PDF pp. 164–166). The worked example is After-School All-Stars, a nonprofit whose grant applications she reweighted by expected value, so that a $50,000 grant at 70% outranked a $200,000 grant at 10%, with reported gains in work order, budgeting, post-outcome review, and resistance to resulting and hindsight bias (ch. 6, PDF pp. 168–170). Carroll's call is then rebuilt as a decision tree in which a pass yields three plays rather than two, against an interception probability of 2% to 3% and a fumble probability of 1% to 2% (ch. 6, PDF pp. 170–171).

Backcasting and premortems are the backward half. Gary Klein's *Harvard Business Review* summary of a 1989 experiment run by three researchers — Deborah Mitchell, J. Edward Russo, and Nancy Pennington — reports that prospective hindsight increases the ability to identify reasons for future outcomes by 30% (ch. 6, PDF p. 173). Backcasting starts from a headline announcing the goal reached and works back to what produced it, with Frederick Law Olmsted's Central Park — opened in 1858, substantially built by 1873, and designed for a landscape decades away — as the illustration (ch. 6, PDF pp. 172–173). A premortem starts from a headline announcing failure, and its warrant is Gabriele Oettingen's twenty years of work on mental contrasting, whose first study, of women in a weight-loss program, found that those who indulged vivid optimistic daydreams about becoming slim ended up twenty-four pounds behind those who pictured the thing going badly, with the pattern repeated among students with crushes, job seekers, examinees, and hip-replacement patients (ch. 6, PDF pp. 175–176). The author frames a premortem as an implementation of organized skepticism that licenses dissent, since the exercise makes the naysayer the team player (ch. 6, PDF pp. 176–177). The closing image is dendrological: the past is one trunk, the futures are branches, and the advancing present is a chainsaw that removes every branch that did not happen, which is hindsight bias. Judge Frank Easterbrook's opinion in *Jentz v. ConAgra Foods*, setting aside part of a $180 million verdict, is quoted for the proposition that a jury's hindsight cannot by itself carry a punitive-damages award, and the 2016 election is the last instance of a 30% to 40% branch being cut off and forgotten (ch. 6, PDF pp. 178–182).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits her own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** The book is not about poker strategy or gambling, and the author states plainly that the method never made her a reliably rational actor free of feeling (Introduction, PDF p. 11). The method is explicitly short of a cure — the author says it removes neither self-serving bias nor motivated reasoning and only improves them, and that a little better is all she claims (ch. 3, PDF p. 96).
- **Objective truth is not denied.** Admitting to being unsure is not a claim that no objective truth exists; the author, following Stuart Firestein, treats acknowledging uncertainty as the first step toward getting closer to it (ch. 1, PDF p. 30).
- **Experience still counts.** An experienced player makes a better guess than a novice, and an expert has an advantage over a rookie; what neither gets is knowledge of how the next flip lands (ch. 1, PDF pp. 30–31).
- **Some outcomes really are luck, and some really are skill.** The author states that not all bad outcomes are luck and not all good ones are skill — her objection is to treating either as true all the time (ch. 3, PDF p. 79).
- **Self-serving bias is a tendency, not a law.** A footnote records that some people show the reverse pattern, blaming themselves for everything bad and crediting luck for everything good, that the reverse is rarer and more likely in women, and that it is no better because it is equally inaccurate (ch. 3, footnote, PDF p. 231).
- **Truthseeking is not always appropriate.** Not all situations suit it and not all people want it, which is what Letterman got wrong; outside a chartered group the author advises against going around saying "Wanna bet?" (ch. 4, PDF pp. 100, 102).
- **Venting is allowed.** Her own group made an explicit, temporary exception for a player who needed to complain about bad luck after an elimination, on the understanding that it was a break from the work and not a change to it (ch. 4, PDF p. 103).
- **Truthseeking is not a cult.** Friends who have not taken the red pill do not have to be cut off, different friends fill different needs, and the breaks they provide replenish willpower (ch. 4, PDF p. 103).
- **Data sharing is by agreement.** Without an agreement, nobody can be compelled to share; the author names privacy, trade secrets, and intellectual property as limits on the Mertonian norm of communism (ch. 5, PDF p. 126).
- **Feynman's ideal is unreachable.** The author says it is unrealistic to think anyone can perfectly achieve the standard of reporting everything that might invalidate a result, and that even scientists struggle with it (ch. 5, PDF p. 125).
- **Outcome blindness has a limit in poker.** Analyzing hands before the outcome is known is not practical there, because results arrive within seconds of the decision; the workaround is to omit the ending when asking for advice (ch. 5, PDF pp. 133–134).
- **People who ask for advice may not want it.** She warns to tread lightly even after someone says they want advice, because what they may want is to be affirmed (ch. 5, PDF p. 139).
- **Children are a special case.** A child cannot consent to a truthseeking exchange and can only be nudged, which is why her own approach with her son was validation first and a question about the future second (ch. 5, PDF p. 140).
- **Deliberative mind is not a guarantee.** A footnote states that System 2 is not immune to bias, citing Kahan's numerate subjects reasoning to a preferred answer and Kahneman's own recognition of the point (ch. 6, footnote, PDF p. 240).
- **Precommitment is rarely airtight.** In most situations a contract cannot be made tamper-proof, and even Ulysses depended on his crew ignoring his signal; the value claimed is the interrupt rather than the barrier (ch. 6, PDF pp. 159–160).
- **A wide probability range still beats none.** The author says that a guess between 20% and 80% is better than not guessing at all, since anything off the extremes of 0% and 100% is a more reasonable assessment (ch. 6, PDF p. 166).
- **Backcasting is not neutral.** She notes that backcasting overrepresents the positive space rather than ignoring the negative one, which is why a premortem is needed beside it (ch. 6, PDF p. 177).
- **Better decisions do not guarantee better results.** Improving decision quality increases the chances of good outcomes without guaranteeing them, and no strategy in the book turns anyone into a perfectly rational actor (ch. 6, PDF p. 143).
- **The mindfulness alternative is conceded, not adopted.** Observing thoughts without judging them is called a great goal and worth pursuing, and is set aside as a tall order that works against how brains evolved (ch. 3, PDF p. 92).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, study, tool, or person the book leans on, checked in September 2026. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure; this session's web-search budget was exhausted partway through this section, and several journal hosts refused a direct fetch, so the unchecked list below is long, and each entry says which of the two happened.

- **Nate Silver's 2016 forecast: Trump at between 30% and 40% in the week before the election (ch. 1, PDF p. 34), and Clinton at somewhere between 60% and 70% (ch. 6, PDF p. 181).** Partly off at the end. FiveThirtyEight's election-morning forecast gave Clinton 71%, so Trump about 29% (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FiveThirtyEight) — just outside the Clinton band the book prints and just under the Trump band. The daily archive that would settle the week-before figures was not reachable, so those are unchecked. The institution is gone: Silver left in April 2023 amid ABC News layoffs, and the site was closed by Disney on March 5, 2025, after which Silver started Silver Bulletin.
- **Pete Carroll as the Seahawks' head coach (ch. 1, PDF p. 12).** Historical now. He and Seattle agreed he would step down as head coach on January 10, 2024, he became head coach of the Las Vegas Raiders on January 25, 2025, and he was fired on January 5, 2026, after a 3–14 season (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Carroll). The play-call itself is unchanged; the book's figure of zero interceptions in sixty-six one-yard-line passes that season (ch. 1, PDF p. 13) is unchecked, because the Morris and Burke pieces it comes from were not fetched.
- **Dan Ariely, cited as "predictably irrational" and named in a footnote as a leading researcher in behavioral economics (ch. 3, PDF p. 77 and footnote, PDF p. 228).** Materially damaged since publication. A 2012 dishonesty paper he co-authored was retracted in 2021 after its data were found to be falsified; Ariely said in February 2024 that a three-year Duke investigation concluded data were falsified but found no evidence he knowingly used fake data; and his 2002 procrastination paper with Klaus Wertenbroch was retracted in September 2026 after Data Colada reported duplicated observations and other patterns inconsistent with genuine data (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Ariely). Nothing the book cites him for is one of the retracted results, and the phrase it borrows is a book title.
- **The Marshmallow Test, cited in a footnote for correlations with SAT scores, social and cognitive functioning, body mass index, addiction, self-worth, and goal pursuit (ch. 6, footnote, PDF p. 241).** Weakened. Watts, Duncan, and Quan's 2018 conceptual replication found the associations with behavioral problems mostly not statistically significant once early cognitive ability, behavior, family background, and home environment were controlled, with a smaller surviving association to achievement tests at age 15 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_marshmallow_experiment). The footnote's list of correlates predates that.
- **Heterodox Academy, which the book says Haidt founded with Philip Tetlock and four others (ch. 4, PDF p. 119).** The organization holds and has grown: still operating in 2025 with about 7,000 members in 22 countries, John Tomasi as president since 2022 and Haidt as board chair (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterodox_Academy). The founding attribution is contradicted by the organization's own account, which says it was founded in 2015 by Jonathan Haidt, Chris Martin, and Nicholas Rosenkranz (heterodoxacademy.org/about-us); the six people the book lists are the authors of the 2015 *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* paper. See section 8.
- **The replication controversy the betting market was run against (ch. 4, PDF p. 121).** The Reproducibility Project: Psychology reported that 36% of 97 original studies with significant effects replicated at p below 0.05, with effect sizes averaging half the original (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis), which is consistent with the book's description of a large number of unreplicable published studies. Two of the book's three figures check out against the paper itself: Dreber and colleagues (PNAS, December 15, 2015, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1516179112) state in their significance summary that the markets covered 44 studies from the Reproducibility Project and outperformed a survey of individual forecasts, which is the study count and the direction the book gives (abstract retrieved through the Crossref API). The two percentages, 71% against 58%, sit in the body rather than the abstract and are **unchecked**, because PNAS returned 403 to two fetches and no other full text was reachable.
- **The American Foreign Service Association's "four separate awards" for constructive dissent (ch. 4, PDF p. 113).** Holds. AFSA still gives four — the Harris, Harriman, Rivkin, and Herter awards — each with a $4,000 prize, with nominations open to April 12, 2027 (afsa.org/constructive-dissent-awards).
- **Gabriele Oettingen's WOOP, recommended in the notes (ch. 6, Notes, PDF p. 196).** Live: woopmylife.org still publishes the Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan method and its tools.
- **Poker career figures.** All three named players have moved past the book's numbers. Phil Hellmuth, credited with fourteen bracelets and counting (ch. 3, PDF p. 78), reached 17 on July 2, 2023 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phil_Hellmuth). Erik Seidel, credited in a footnote with eight bracelets and over $30 million (ch. 4, footnote, PDF p. 235), won a tenth on December 9, 2023, with winnings over $45.5 million (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Seidel). ## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **The anchor case is defended with the book's own numbers.** Carroll's call opens chapter 1 and is settled in chapter 6 by a decision tree in which the pass buys a third play against a 2% to 3% interception risk and a 1% to 2% fumble risk (ch. 6, PDF pp. 170–171). The footnote supplying those figures says an ordinary fan can plug in general probabilities, that they include no adjustment for New England's defense or for short-yardage situations, and that beyond a point one can only guess from small samples (ch. 6, footnote, PDF p. 247). The exoneration that frames the whole book therefore rests on estimates the book itself labels as approximations.
- **Correlation presented as cause.** The After-School All-Stars consultation is the one organizational case study, and it credits six distinct improvements — work ordering, budget confidence, better probability estimates, actions taken to raise grant odds, and resistance to both hindsight bias and resulting — to the introduction of expected value, with no control, no before-and-after figures, and no source beyond the author's account (ch. 6, PDF pp. 169–170). A footnote discloses that she joined the organization's board in 2009 and that the consulting was part of that board work (ch. 6, footnote, PDF p. 245), which is the kind of interest the book's own disinterestedness section says to declare and discount for; it is declared and not discounted for.
- **Generalization from very small samples, in a book about small samples.** Phil Ivey's status as the exception to self-serving bias rests on two dinners, one of which the author heard about secondhand (ch. 3, PDF pp. 88–89). Nick the Greek carries the entire opening argument of chapter 3 from one player in one Montana card room in 1992, and the author says she cannot attribute his deportation to his poker beliefs and then says she has her suspicions (ch. 3, PDF p. 67). The Hennigan bet, the Ira the Whale bet, and the Erik Seidel exchange are each a single anecdote doing the work of evidence for a general claim.
- **Figures stated from experience with no source anywhere.** That a strong player still loses more than 40% of eight-hour sessions (ch. 1, PDF p. 36), that an experienced player folds about 80% of hands dealt (ch. 3, PDF p. 82), that a hand can carry up to twenty decisions and a casino game runs about thirty hands an hour (ch. 1, PDF p. 21), and that feature of the poker table which makes it "a naturally diverse setting" (ch. 4, PDF p. 113) — none of these carry a note, and the Notes section for chapter 1 cites only the tournament-results database and the game's own statistics (Notes, PDF pp. 189–190).
- **An attribution its own sources do not support.** The book says Haidt founded Heterodox Academy with Philip Tetlock and four others, naming the authors of the 2015 *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* paper (ch. 4, PDF p. 119); Heterodox Academy's own history page names three different founders — Haidt, Chris Martin, and Nicholas Rosenkranz — in 2015. Authoring the paper that motivated an organization is not the same as founding it, and the book's notes for that chapter carry no source for the founding at all (Notes, PDF pp. 193–194).
- **The evidence base overlaps heavily with the author's own network.** Erik Seidel, Howard Lederer, Phil Ivey, Colin Camerer, Dan Ariely, Charles Duhigg, and Joe Kable all appear as evidence in the text and again in the notes or acknowledgments as people who helped with the book; the insurance-form excuses attributed to Robert MacCoun are disclosed in a footnote as having come from a book written by the author's father (ch. 3, footnote, PDF p. 229), and the acknowledgments record that Ariely introduced her to her literary agent and that Duhigg shared his book proposal (Acknowledgments, PDF p. 184). Every one of these is disclosed; none of the disclosures changes the weight the claims are given.
- **The book's own standard is not applied to the book.** Chapter 2 asks a reader to attach a number from zero to ten, or a range of plausible alternatives, to every belief expressed (ch. 2, PDF pp. 62–63). No claim in the book carries one, including the central claim that adopting the betting frame improves decision quality, for which no effect size is offered anywhere.
- **Two chapters rest on literatures the book itself shows to be unstable, without reconciling them.** Chapter 1 builds its account of irrationality on behavioral economics and social psychology published before 2018 (ch. 1, PDF pp. 16–18), and chapter 4 reports the replication controversy in psychology and the betting market run against it (ch. 4, PDF p. 121) without asking which of the earlier chapter's results are in that pool. Section 7 above found that at least two of the cited supports have since moved: the marshmallow-test correlates shrank under controls in 2018, and papers by a researcher the book names have been retracted for falsified data.
