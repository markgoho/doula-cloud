## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's own frame, is that a company of one is "a business that questions growth" — one that resists some forms of traditional growth not on principle but because growth is often neither the most beneficial nor the most financially viable move, and that treats staying small as an end goal and a long-term strategy rather than as a stepping stone or a sign of failure (ch. 1, PDF pp. 10, 15; ch. 2, PDF p. 35). Jarvis argues that four traits define such a business — resilience, autonomy, speed and simplicity — and that a company built on them can raise revenue, enjoyment, focus and experience while deliberately refusing to raise payroll, expenses and stress (Prologue, PDF p. 10; ch. 1, PDF pp. 18–27). The author reduces his own model to three moves, in the manner of Michael Pollan's food rules: start small, define what growth means, and keep learning (Prologue, PDF p. 11). The Epilogue states the whole thing as a single rule, that a founder stays alert to the opportunities that require growth and questions them before taking them (Epilogue, PDF p. 170).

Jarvis says at length what the claim is not. It is not anti-growth and not anti-revenue (ch. 1, PDF p. 18). It is not necessarily a one-person business, though it can be, and it is not a freelancer, because a freelancer trades time for money one-to-one while a company of one profits outside the hours worked (Prologue, PDF p. 10). It is not a startup in the usual sense, since startups are described as temporary organizations that spend against anticipated revenue (ch. 2, PDF p. 31). It is not confined to people who work for themselves: the author holds that an employee inside a large corporation can be a company of one, and borrows "intrapreneur" as the nearest existing word while saying the two are not the same (ch. 1, PDF pp. 16–17). It is not an either-or commitment, and the reader is invited to take some of the ideas and leave the rest (Epilogue, PDF p. 169). And it is not offered as the one correct path — the author writes that every business is a lifestyle business in that each is a choice about how to live, that this book presents one such choice, and that no choice is better than another (Epilogue, PDF p. 170).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Prologue (PDF pp. 7–11)

Jarvis opens with his own move in February 2010 from downtown Vancouver to Tofino, a town of fewer than 2,000 people on Vancouver Island, sold and donated of almost all possessions, made to escape both city life and the constant pressure to grow a working business into a bigger one (Prologue, PDF p. 7). The argument he draws from it is that decluttering a life made the same question legible in the work: what was necessary and what was not (Prologue, PDF p. 8). A surfing conversation with an accountant who had calculated his own "enough" number and planned to stop working for the year once he hit it supplies the book's organizing idea (Prologue, PDF pp. 8–9). The author sets out the counter-move to every growth reflex — more customers by more efficiency rather than more employees, more revenue by spending less, fewer support requests by teaching customers better — and asserts that blind growth is the main cause of business problems (Prologue, PDF p. 9). The definition is drawn against two neighbors: a company of one is not just a small business, whose end game is usually expansion, and not a freelancer, whose every paid relationship is one-to-one (Prologue, PDF p. 10).

### 1. Defining a Company of One (PDF pp. 13–27)

Tom Fishburne, who left a consumer-foods marketing vice-presidency in 2010 to draw cartoons and now earns two to three times his executive income at a firm that is himself, his wife and occasional freelancers, is the opening case (ch. 1, PDF pp. 13–15). The definition follows: a business that questions growth, available to a small owner, a group of founders, or an employee inside a corporation (ch. 1, PDF p. 15). Jarvis dates his own realization to an October 2016 blog post saying he was not interested in exponential growth, and to the replies it drew (ch. 1, PDF p. 15). The corporate case rests on intrapreneurship — Gifford Pinchot's term — and on Post-it notes at 3M, Elixir guitar strings at W. L. Gore, Google's twenty-percent time, Facebook's hackathons and the Like button, and a Govindarajan study putting 250 true innovators and 25 intrapreneurs in every 5,000 employees (ch. 1, PDF pp. 16–17).

The four traits are then set out. **Resilience** is illustrated by Danielle LaPorte, who took $400,000 from private investors on condition she hire a chief executive and was pushed out of her own eponymous business six months later, then rebuilt alone (ch. 1, PDF p. 18); Dean Becker's Adaptiv Learning research supplies three learnable characteristics — accepting reality, a sense of purpose, and adapting to change — alongside automation figures of 42 percent of Canadian jobs and 62 percent of American ones at risk (ch. 1, PDF pp. 19–20). **Autonomy** requires mastery of a core skill first, and is illustrated by the freelance strategist Kaitlin Maud, by results-only work environments, and by Sol Orwell of Examine.com refusing venture capital for a seven-figure business (ch. 1, PDF pp. 21–23). **Speed** is doing the work by better methods rather than faster ones, with Basecamp's four-day summer week and Stewart Butterfield's pivots out of failed games into Flickr and Slack as the examples (ch. 1, PDF pp. 23–25). **Simplicity** is carried by Pinboard, a one-person bookmarking service at $3 a year rising to $11, buying the venture-funded Delicious for $35,000 in June 2017 (ch. 1, PDF p. 25). Jarvis closes by warning that simple and easy are not the same word, and that a business idea needing a lot of money, time or resources is probably being thought too big (ch. 1, PDF pp. 26–27).

### 2. Staying Small as an End Goal (PDF pp. 28–42)

Sean D'Souza of Psychotactics set a profit target of $500,000 a year and decided the business should not exceed it, shaping the company around a life that includes a three-month annual holiday; he retains customers by attention to the ones he has and by mailing them chocolate that costs about $20 (ch. 2, PDF pp. 28–30). Ricardo Semler is cited as finding an organic size for each business he owns and stopping there (ch. 2, PDF p. 30). Two studies carry the chapter's central evidence: the Startup Genome Project's analysis of more than 3,200 high-growth tech startups, in which 74 percent failed from scaling too quickly rather than from competition or bad plans, and a Kauffman Foundation and *Inc.* follow-up finding more than two-thirds of the 5,000 fastest-growing companies out of business, laying off, or sold below market value five to eight years later, and almost 86 percent of long-term successes having taken no venture money (ch. 2, PDF pp. 30–31). Paul Graham is quoted to the effect that venture firms invest what their own portfolios need rather than what a company needs, and Salim Ismail that startups are fragile by design (ch. 2, PDF p. 31). Buffer is the counter-example within the category: it hired ahead of a funding round, laid off 11 percent, and lost a chief operating officer and chief technology officer who wanted the high-growth game, keeping a profit-based plan and seventy-two employees (ch. 2, PDF pp. 31–32).

LaPorte's term for the machine growth builds is "the Beast" — in her case a million-dollar website needing a standing team to run it — which Jarvis glosses with the Buddhist "hungry ghost" (ch. 2, PDF pp. 32–33). Starbucks closing 900 stores after diluting itself with sandwiches and CDs, Krispy Kreme's 18 percent sales fall from 2004 to 2006 and $75 million Securities and Exchange Commission settlement, and Pets.com spending more than $17 million on advertising in one quarter against $8.8 million of revenue, are the large-company cases (ch. 2, PDF pp. 33–34). Leah Andrews of Queen of Snow Globes is the small one: facing demand she could not meet, she raised prices until demand leveled rather than scaling production, and Jarvis says he did the same by repeatedly doubling his design rates (ch. 2, PDF pp. 34–35). Pat Riley's phrase for what happens to winning teams, "the disease of more", is borrowed for startups (ch. 2, PDF p. 34). Technology is then argued to have made this cheap: WordPress, which the book says powers 26 percent of all websites, closed a 15,000-square-foot San Francisco office used by about five people a day; Pieter Levels runs Nomad List for $400,000 a year with no employees and no office; Brennan Dunn sells through email automation costing a few hundred dollars a month (ch. 2, PDF pp. 35–36). The risk argument is inverted through Miranda Hixon of MilkWood Designs, whose father ran a project-management business from the garage under a sticky note reading "OVERHEAD = DEATH", and through US Census data showing 38,029 non-employer establishments at seven-figure revenue, up nearly 6 percent in 2015 (ch. 2, PDF pp. 37–39).

The chapter's distinctive move is the **upper bound**: most businesses set a floor on a goal and none on the ceiling, and Jarvis proposes stating both. Southwest Airlines is the case, turning down more than 95 percent of over 100 cities asking for service in 1996 and adding four, and James Clear is the personal one, hitting 10,000 and then 100,000 newsletter subscribers and finding nothing changed (ch. 2, PDF pp. 39–40). A closing section treats envy as the engine of the growth wish, notes that public numbers are usually gross revenue or monthly recurring revenue rather than profit, and offers the Pali word *mudita*, delight in another's good fortune, as the counterweight (ch. 2, PDF pp. 41–42).

### 3. What's Required to Lead (PDF pp. 43–53)

Charisma is presented as trainable rather than innate, on University of Lausanne research, with Gandhi and Sharp's Katsuhiko Machida as the high-goal examples (ch. 3, PDF pp. 43–44). Harvard Business School work by Adam Grant, Francesca Gino and David Hofmann is cited for introverted leaders outperforming with proactive teams, against a stereotype in which more than 96 percent of managers and executives are extroverted and 65 percent of senior executives in a 2006 study saw introversion as a barrier (ch. 3, PDF pp. 44–45). Servant leadership, traced to the Tao Te Ching, is the alternative model (ch. 3, PDF p. 45). Jarvis describes structuring his own business around written and one-to-many teaching because he finds groups hard, and hiring only contractors who need no management (ch. 3, PDF p. 45).

Autonomy is then qualified: removing all rules produces anarchy, 79 percent of Fortune 1,000 companies run self-directed teams that are still led, and Henrik Kniberg's autonomy-versus-alignment dichotomy is called false (ch. 3, PDF p. 46). Kyle Murphy at Hudl is the worked case — a shared style guide and a streamlined revision process let fewer designers do more, so process rather than hiring was the answer (ch. 3, PDF p. 46). The leader of a company of one is told to be a generalist with a specialization underneath, on Carter Phipps and Vikram Mansharamani, and five qualities are named: psychology, communication, resilience, focus (a leader has to become expert at saying no) and decisiveness (ch. 3, PDF pp. 47–49). The chapter closes against hustle culture, citing David Heinemeier Hansson, Wayne Oates's 1971 coinage of workaholism and his finding that hustlers do not outperform, and Crew's refusal to set hours (ch. 3, PDF pp. 49–50). Sukhvinder Obhi's "power paradox" and Dacher Keltner's twenty years of research supply the claim that the traits that win leadership are the ones it erodes, and Rand Fishkin, who grew Moz from $300,000 of revenue in 2006 to over $48 million in 2014 and stepped down as chief executive when he became depressed, is the case for self-awareness, transparency, empathy and gratitude as the remedy (ch. 3, PDF pp. 51–53).

### 4. Growing a Company That Doesn't Grow (PDF pp. 54–63)

Growth hacking is set against meaningful growth through Kate O'Neill, who found at Magazines.com that reducing cancellations beat acquiring subscribers and moved the home page and support effort toward existing customers (ch. 4, PDF pp. 54–55). Jarvis then names **four reasons companies grow**: inflation, investors, churn and ego. Inflation is met by raising rates yearly; investors want a multiple of what they put in; churn is usually answered by adding customers rather than by finding out why people leave, though an Econsultancy and Responsys report is cited for a new customer costing five times as much as keeping one; and ego is the hardest because size buys social standing (ch. 4, PDF pp. 55–56). Gary Sutton is quoted on the impossibility of selling a way out of an unprofitable business (ch. 4, PDF p. 57).

Starting is treated as a matter of subtraction: find the smallest version of the idea and a way to do it now, with Steve Martin's answer to aspiring comedians — get good at comedy rather than find an agent — as the illustration, and Alexandra Franzen, who quit radio and emailed sixty people she already knew in her first week, as the case (ch. 4, PDF pp. 57–58). Obscurity at the start is recast as an advantage, since few people watch a failure (ch. 4, PDF p. 58). A true need-to-have is defined as whatever makes the idea fall apart in its absence, and Crew's launch as a single form on a one-page website is the example (ch. 4, PDF p. 59). Growth is then distinguished by direction: growth as a goal produces shortsighted decisions and churn, growth as a result of profit from a valuable product does not (ch. 4, PDF p. 60). Peldi Guilizzoni's Balsamiq is the extended case — privately owned, no funding taken, with the founder drawing $1 million a year, an eighteen-month runway held in the company, the remainder paid to a twenty-five-person team growing by two or three a year (ch. 4, PDF p. 60). Patagonia's Yvon Chouinard, the lifetime guarantee, 1% for the Planet and the "Don't buy this jacket" campaign close the argument that stripping a business to its essence reveals its strength (ch. 4, PDF p. 61). The chapter ends inside the corporation, with Buffer's career model of scope of influence and ownership, designed by Katie Womersley so that a programmer never has to choose between stagnation and managing people (ch. 4, PDF pp. 61–63).

### 5. Determining the Right Mind-Set (PDF pp. 65–76)

Purpose is defined as values in action and as the filter every business decision passes through, distinguished sharply from a mission statement (ch. 5, PDF pp. 65–67). Patagonia is again the case, with the claim that the top five companies in 1% for the Planet had record sales in the 2008–2009 recession; Seventh Generation, a B Corporation at about $250 million of revenue that encourages line-drying at the expense of its own dryer sheets, is the second; CVS dropping tobacco is the third (ch. 5, PDF pp. 65–67). John Kotter and James Heskett are cited for purpose-driven companies outperforming on stock price by a factor of twelve, and Michael Porter and Mark Kramer for shared value producing economic returns (ch. 5, PDF pp. 67–68).

Passion is then separated from purpose and attacked. Robert Vallerand's University of Quebec study found students more passionate about sport, art and music than their studies, while only 3 percent of jobs sit in those industries (ch. 5, PDF p. 68). Barbara Corcoran is quoted as having discovered passion through work rather than followed it; Cal Newport's craftsman mindset and William MacAskill's four components of engaging work — clear assignments, tasks one excels at, feedback and autonomy — carry the claim that passion follows mastery (ch. 5, PDF pp. 68–69). Jarvis makes the argument autobiographical: he started on his own only after clients followed him out of an agency, moved into courses only after years of transition, and failed twice — at consulting in his twenties and at two software companies — precisely where the skills were not built and the demand was never tested (ch. 5, PDF pp. 69–71). The chapter closes on the cost of opportunity: opportunities are obligations in disguise, "priority" was singular until the 1950s, Microsoft Research put the productivity cost of more than one priority at up to 40 percent, Hewlett-Packard measured a ten-point IQ drop under interruption, and Gloria Mark found 23 minutes and 15 seconds to return to a task (ch. 5, PDF p. 72). Jocelyn Glei's productivity audit and stop-doing list, Jason Fried's rule that a manager protects the team's attention and Basecamp's refusal to share calendars, and John Pencavel's finding that focus falls away past 55 hours a week, are the practices offered (ch. 5, PDF pp. 73–75).

### 6. Personality Matters (PDF pp. 77–84)

Jarvis reports surveying more than 10,000 of his own customers and finding that his personality was the first reason they bought from him rather than from someone else, and argues that skills and expertise are replicable while a personality is not (ch. 6, PDF p. 77). Marie Forleo's eight-figure training business and Charlie Bickford of Excalibur Screwbolts, who still answers the phone at seventy-four, are the cases (ch. 6, PDF p. 78). Attention is then framed as the scarce currency, on Steve Rubel, with the Urban Outfitters episode — a tweet amplified to 1.3 million followers costing 17,000 followers in hours — as the downside, and Killingsworth and Gilbert's finding that minds wander 46.9 percent of the time as the backdrop (ch. 6, PDF p. 79). Sally Hogshead's fascination research, over 125,000 participants across ten years, supplies the prescription to unlearn being boring, and the book's most-used image: a large company is the vanilla ice cream of its market, and a company of one has to be pistachio (ch. 6, PDF pp. 79–80).

Neutrality is then argued to be costly. Derek Sivers is cited for proudly excluding people, Tom Fishburne for the "power in polarization", Marmite for twenty years of a love-it-or-hate-it line, and Guy Kawasaki for making an identified group very happy and ignoring everyone else (ch. 6, PDF p. 81). Just Mayo and Hampton Creek's fight with Unilever, the Food and Drug Administration and the American Egg Board is the extended case, ending with Unilever dropping its suit and launching an eggless product of its own (ch. 6, PDF p. 82). Three polarization strategies are named — placation, prodding, amplification — with General Mills's gluten-free mixes, Marmite XO and WestJet's campaign against United's overbooking as the respective examples, and Apple's 1984 advertisement as the free-media case (ch. 6, PDF pp. 82–83). Jarvis adds that critical replies to his newsletter come almost entirely from people who never bought anything, so the audience vets itself, and that polarization shortens a sales cycle by forcing a binary choice (ch. 6, PDF pp. 83–84).

### 7. The One Customer (PDF pp. 85–97)

The chapter argues that treating each customer as the only one pays measurably. A Harris Interactive survey is cited for nine in ten Americans willing to spend more for great service and 79 percent abandoning a transaction after a poor experience; a White House Office of Consumer Affairs study for loyal customers being worth up to ten times a first purchase; Ruby Newell-Legner for only 4 percent of dissatisfied customers complaining and 91 percent simply never returning (ch. 7, PDF pp. 85–86). The structural advantage claimed for a company of one is that its service does not scale and does not have to: CDBaby answering every call within two rings between 7am and 10pm with no voicemail, Basecamp answering support within fifteen minutes, Bickford picking up the phone (ch. 7, PDF pp. 86–87). A McKinsey study putting 70 percent of buying experience on how customers feel they were treated supports what Jarvis calls the second wave of customer service, built on emotion and ease rather than on deflection (ch. 7, PDF p. 87).

Referrals are the mechanism by which service becomes marketing: SmallBizTrends is cited for 83 percent of new business coming from word of mouth, the Rackspace pizza story and a Trader Joe's snowbound delivery are the set-piece anecdotes, and Joel Klettke reports 80 to 90 percent of his good leads arriving that way (ch. 7, PDF pp. 88–89). Trello is the product case, grown to more than ten million users on 100 percent organic growth (ch. 7, PDF p. 89). Empathy is then argued as a profit tool rather than a soft one, on Kate Leggett of Forrester and on Lady Geek's Harvard Business Review empathy index, where the five most profitable companies rank at the top (ch. 7, PDF pp. 89–90). Eric von Hippel's research putting more than 60 percent of profitable innovations with customers, and 3M's Medical-Surgical division reaching $146 million of average revenue from lead-user ideas against $18 million from internal ones, carry the case for listening (ch. 7, PDF p. 90). Customer success is presented as its own discipline through Adam Waid at Salesforce Pardot and Cindy Carson at UserIQ, with the argument that a customer paying $10 a month for ten years outvalues one paying $100 who leaves in a few months (ch. 7, PDF pp. 92–93).

The chapter's last third is about failure. Jarvis describes a payment vendor's bug double-charging dozens of his customers $600 for a $300 product, and taking responsibility because his name was on the store: he emailed everyone affected including those who had not noticed, described the fix, and lost two refunds (ch. 7, PDF p. 94). A 2015 *New York Times* report that apologizing doctors are sued less, the University of Illinois halving malpractice filings after adopting transparency, and a University of Nottingham study finding apology often costs nothing and beats compensation, are the supporting evidence (ch. 7, PDF p. 95). Netflix's 2011 split of its DVD and streaming businesses is the counter-case, and Liel Leibovitz's finding that 88 percent of consumers are less likely to buy from a company that ignores social-media complaints closes it (ch. 7, PDF pp. 95–96). Nicholas Epley's work on warmth and competence supports the rule that a promise kept is the whole mechanism, Luigi Zingales's research that stated values are irrelevant without matching action, and Kouchaki, Doty and Gino's "commitment drift" supplies three remedies: fewer and better commitments, tracking them, and a process for meeting them (ch. 7, PDF pp. 96–97).

### 8. Scalable Systems (PDF pp. 98–105)

The chapter concedes that growth is sometimes required and asks how to get it without adding people. Need/Want, a physical-products company at nearly $10 million of revenue with fewer than ten employees, is the case: Shopify for the store, outsourced manufacturing and fulfillment, no big-box retail and so no field sales, no trade shows, and three people running online channels (ch. 8, PDF pp. 98–99). James Clear is the second: a list above 400,000 growing by 1,000 a week, with two rules — products that need no ongoing management, and a one-time fee with no retainers or ongoing consulting — chosen by working backwards from the life he wanted (ch. 8, PDF pp. 99–100). **Creation** as a scalable system is illustrated by Naomi Klein on globalization's costs and by Arthur & Henry's slow fashion and Girlfriend Collective's recycled-bottle manufacturing at 125 percent of minimum wage (ch. 8, PDF pp. 100–101). **Connection** is email: the same effort sends to one person or 50,000, the Data & Marketing Association's 3,800 percent return on investment is cited, Campaign Monitor's 26 percent lift from personalized subject lines and the Epsilon Email Institute's 70.5 percent open and 152 percent click advantages for segmented automation are the levers, and Jarvis reports email accounting for more than 93 percent of his own annual revenue (ch. 8, PDF pp. 101–102). Jamie Leigh Hoogendoorn's automated onboarding, which cut a lead-to-project cycle from eight or sixteen hours to one, is the small case (ch. 8, PDF p. 103). **Collaboration** is the exception: Samuel Hulick's word for tools like Slack is "asyncronish", real-time messaging becomes an all-day meeting with no agenda, and Basecamp and Buffer expect replies in days rather than minutes — so collaboration is the one thing a company of one is told to scale down, with the hackathon as the model of focused collaboration (ch. 8, PDF pp. 103–105).

### 9. Teach Everything You Know (PDF pp. 106–114)

Brian Clark's path from practicing attorney to real-estate brokerages to Copyblogger is the opening case, and the lesson Jarvis draws is that Clark's edge was out-sharing rather than out-scaling his competition (ch. 9, PDF pp. 106–107). Copyblogger, renamed RainMaker Digital, is reported at more than $12 million of annual revenue and more than 200,000 customers (ch. 9, PDF p. 108). Four benefits of teaching are named: it makes the teacher the perceived domain expert, it shows the benefit of what is sold without selling, it makes new customers successful and so long-term, and, except for unexecuted ideas and patentable technology, most processes do not need to be secret (ch. 9, PDF p. 108). The point is sharpened into a claim that ideas are worthless without execution, with the copyright on this very book described as protecting the months of research and writing rather than the idea itself, and the UFC — an idea from 1993 that only worked once rule changes and casino money arrived — as the case (ch. 9, PDF p. 109).

Jessica Abel's teaching, which improves her own material through student questions, carries the claim that sharing costs nothing (ch. 9, PDF p. 110). Eisingerich and Bell's survey of 1,200 clients of an investment firm, in which education raised trust, loyalty and appreciation, is the study; Casper's sleep publications, which do not sell mattresses, are the commercial case (ch. 9, PDF pp. 110–111). Authority is then argued to be the countermeasure to a buyer's instinct that a larger company is safer, and is built by teaching rather than by asserting; a 2009 Emory study by Greg Berns found that the decision-making centers of the brain quiet down when wanted expert advice arrives (ch. 9, PDF pp. 112–113). Basecamp is the closing case: no internal conversion or growth quotas, a mandate to out-teach everyone, and $1,000 workshops that sell out in minutes (ch. 9, PDF p. 113).

### 10. Properly Utilizing Trust and Scale (PDF pp. 116–125)

Glen Urban's twenty years of research supplies the frame: trust correlates with willingness to consider, try or buy, and the internet scales the relationship without scaling the business (ch. 10, PDF p. 116). Verified-purchase reviews, robo-advisers such as WealthSimple paying bonuses on client feedback rather than commission, and Ellevest's fiduciary duty are the trust cases; airlines and cell-phone providers are the broken ones (ch. 10, PDF pp. 116–117). The car dealership is the worked example of transparency arriving from outside — dealer invoices, safety ratings and vehicle histories now public — and Mazda's fixed rather than negotiated prices are the response (ch. 10, PDF p. 117). Urban's three aspects of trust are confidence, competence and benevolence (ch. 10, PDF p. 118).

Word of mouth is then treated as trust by proxy, with Nielsen cited for 92 percent of consumers trusting recommendations from family or friends above any advertising, the Word of Mouth Marketing Association for word of mouth driving five times the sales of paid online media and $6 trillion of annual spending, and a Verizon and Small Business Trends survey putting referrals first among small-business acquisition channels (ch. 10, PDF p. 118). Jarvis explains its neglect: businesses expect it to happen unbidden, it resists measurement, and it does not scale quickly — which he says matters to a growth company and not to a company of one (ch. 10, PDF p. 118). A Texas Tech study found 83 percent of customers willing to give referrals and 29 percent actually doing so, and Jarvis reports doubling sharing on one product with an automated email a week after purchase carrying prewritten text (ch. 10, PDF p. 119). A Harris Poll for Ambassador Software found 88 percent of American consumers wanting an incentive to share, rising to 95 percent among eighteen- to thirty-five-year-olds; cash incentives are said to reduce trust while discounts, swag and double-sided offers do not; MailChimp's unbranded shirts and Freddie figures are the loyalty case (ch. 10, PDF p. 119). For service businesses, a Drip survey putting half of new customers on word of mouth is cited, and the practical move is a follow-up schedule weeks after a project ends, when results exist to describe (ch. 10, PDF p. 120).

Marketing is then defined as building trust and empathy with a specific group by consistent communication, which is why cold mail needs volume and targeted email does not (ch. 10, PDF p. 121). Vanity metrics — followers, subscribers, clicks — are said to measure who took the bait rather than engagement or trust, and 100 passionate fans are set against 100,000 followers chasing a free iPad (ch. 10, PDF pp. 121–122). Niche focus is the lever: Kurt Elster's decision to serve Shopify store owners alone rather than general ecommerce grew his revenue eightfold and raised his prices (ch. 10, PDF p. 122). The chapter closes on cost: Alex Beauchamp, formerly of Airbnb, says content that goes viral usually means the audience was never identified; Jason Fried reports Basecamp spending about $1 million on social advertising, stopping, and returning to educational content and referral bonuses; BoatUS's free hazard-and-tide app is the education-as-marketing case; and the Pointe Restaurant in Tofino is the personal-touch one (ch. 10, PDF pp. 123–124).

### 11. Launching and Iterating in Tiny Steps (PDF pp. 126–137)

Jeff Sheldon's Ugmonk is the opening case at full length: a $2,000 loan from his father, four designs, a run of 200 shirts, profitability almost immediately, and inventory increased only as each run sold out, with two years of nights and weekends beside a full-time job (ch. 11, PDF pp. 126–127). From it comes the chapter's coinage, **minimum viable profit** (MVPr): the point at which the business is in the black, which Jarvis argues is the determinant of sustainability rather than customer count, measured growth or gross revenue, and which arrives sooner the lower it is set (ch. 11, PDF p. 127). Profit is defined as enough to cover a salary for the owner, viability as that continuing or rising; growth and profit are described as nearly impossible to focus on at once, because traditional growth means spending now against a future payoff (ch. 11, PDF pp. 127–128).

Dan Norris supplies the claim that nothing is learned before launch (ch. 11, PDF p. 129). Groupon typing deals by hand into emailed PDFs, Pebble's explainer video and Kickstarter campaign, and Virgin's single leased aircraft are the minimum-launch examples (ch. 11, PDF p. 129). George Whitesides's three elements of simplicity — predictability, accessibility and serving as a building block — are applied to Casper, which is credited with three mattress models rather than 108, honest claims backed by reviews from more than 400,000 customers, an existing product category it did not have to explain, and a 100-night return window (ch. 11, PDF p. 130). Funding is treated next: Sheldon's Kickstarter for the Gather desk organizer raised over $430,000, 2,394 percent of its goal, from an audience built over a decade; Pebble's campaign remained the most-funded on the platform at more than $20 million from 78,471 backers and still failed; only about 35 percent of Kickstarter campaigns are funded; crowdfunding raised about $6 billion in 2016; Olav Sorenson holds it suits consumer products rather than business ones; and Harvard Business School research on investor preference for founders like themselves, against PwC and Crowdfunding Center data showing women 32 percent more successful at hitting crowdfunding goals, is the meritocracy argument (ch. 11, PDF pp. 131–132). Katherine Krug's BetterBack, over $3 million raised and a Shark Tank deal refused, is the case (ch. 11, PDF p. 132). Derek Sivers's CDBaby — started for $500, $300 in the first month, sold in 2008 for $22 million at about $250,000 a month of net profit, with weekly investment offers refused — carries the claim that capital is often not required at all (ch. 11, PDF pp. 132–133). Halley Gray's advice to offer the product idea as a service first, and LaPorte's Fire Starter Sessions, are the practical version (ch. 11, PDF p. 134).

The chapter's last argument is that launching is not one event. WD-40 is named for its thirty-nine failures, GM's EV-1 gave way to the Bolt twenty years later, and Reid Hoffman's line about being embarrassed by a first version is quoted at second hand (ch. 11, PDF pp. 134–135). Jim Collins's study of 1,435 companies over forty years supplies the finding that great companies started merely good enough to launch, and his fox-and-hedgehog image the argument for a single focus (ch. 11, PDF p. 135). Anil Dash is quoted that every company is now a technology company, and the practical consequence given is to use Stripe, Square, PayPal, WordPress or YouTube rather than build them (ch. 11, PDF p. 135). Blockbuster dismissing Netflix and Redbox, Sears's catalogs, Motorola's Ed Zander on the iPod Nano and Darryl Zanuck's 1946 verdict on television are the failures to iterate (ch. 11, PDF p. 136). Quitting is then defended against Napoleon Hill: Scott Belsky's test is whether the initial assumption still looks true, and whether the founder would start again knowing what they now know, with the endowment effect named as the trap; Akio Morita's burnt rice and Ev Williams's abandoned Odeo are the cases (ch. 11, PDF pp. 136–137).

### 12. The Hidden Value of Relationships (PDF pp. 139–151)

Chris Brogan opens the chapter: selling to someone with whom a relationship exists is not pushy, and he builds those relationships by connecting people without being asked (ch. 12, PDF p. 139). Personalization is the small company's structural advantage — a list of 1,000 replying subscribers can be answered personally where a corporation cannot — and Brogan warns against the phrase "our audience", because an audience is never a company's property and the implied ownership licenses selling to it (ch. 12, PDF pp. 139–140). Growth hacking is then the foil: its true north is growth, its relationships exist to offset churn, and Glide's default contact-list invitations and The Circle's contact-list blasts are the cases, both followed by collapse in the app-store rankings or a change of heart by the chief executive (ch. 12, PDF pp. 141–142). Andy Johns is cited that aggressive growth focus expedites failure, and Des Traynor that credibility can be swapped for attention at any time (ch. 12, PDF p. 142). Kiva is the opposite case: a microlending platform built on stories, 1.6 million lenders and 2.5 million borrowers, more than $1 billion in loans and a 97 percent repayment rate (ch. 12, PDF p. 142). Brogan's fortune-cookie thought experiment — a newsletter that features one praised employee a week rather than pitching the cookies — is the worked illustration of a message repeated through action (ch. 12, PDF p. 143).

**Social capital** is the chapter's organizing idea: beside financial and human capital, a third kind, credited to Lyda Judson Hanifan in 1916, and modeled as a bank account from which nothing can be withdrawn that was not deposited (ch. 12, PDF p. 144). Buffer's daily research-backed blog, grown to more than 1.2 million users and 700,000 monthly blog readers, and Chris Guillebeau personally emailing the first 10,000 subscribers on his list, are the deposits (ch. 12, PDF p. 145). Sam Milbrath's thirds — own content, others' content, personal interaction — is the allocation rule, and Bolander and Satornino's finding that as much as 26.6 percent of variance in sales performance comes from social capital is the evidence (ch. 12, PDF p. 145). HighRise filming a personal welcome video for each new customer is the deliberately unscalable gesture (ch. 12, PDF p. 146). Matthew Lieberman's argument that belonging sits below safety in Maslow's hierarchy is the theoretical support (ch. 12, PDF pp. 146–147).

Existing customers are then argued to be the ones most easily forgotten. Daiya Foods' 2017 sale to Otsuka, which tests on animals, drew boycotts, delistings and 6,000 petition signatures within hours; Apple's maps apology, United's dragged passenger and roughly $1 billion of market value, and Nivea's campaign are the supporting cases (ch. 12, PDF pp. 147–148). Jim Dougherty's four steps — being liked, being respected, being admired as a whole person, and maintaining the relationship when there is no immediate business reason — close the argument, with an IBM study of more than 1,500 leaders in which 88 percent named deeper customer relationships their most important dimension (ch. 12, PDF pp. 148–149). A final section argues against working alone: Angela Devlen's Wakefield Brunswick takes hospital-scale contracts through a trusted network of independent owners with one full-time employee, Ghostly Ferns wins agency-sized work as a loose group, and James Niehues inherited a monopoly on hand-painted ski maps from Bill Brown after a few projects together (ch. 12, PDF pp. 149–150).

### 13. Starting a Company of One—My Story (PDF pp. 152–164)

Jarvis tells his own origin — dropping out of computer science in Toronto, an agency job he quit over its treatment of clients, and clients phoning the day after — and then states the caveats at length: working for yourself is not for everyone, there is no payroll, accounting or sales department, and the people he knows with their own company spend about half their time or less on the craft they are paid for (ch. 13, PDF pp. 152–154). Austin Kleon's line about wanting the noun without doing the verb is quoted against people in love with the idea (ch. 13, PDF p. 154). Ego and purpose are said to be required in equal measure, ego in the sense of believing one can do it better, and his own purpose is named as freedom of choice (ch. 13, PDF pp. 154–155).

The build, if he started again with nothing, is described concretely: listen to people hiring web designers, answer their questions free and without pitching, notice where the trends are, and only then write or sell — which is also how he says he kept a four-to-five-month waiting list for over a decade (ch. 13, PDF pp. 155–157). Creative Class is the worked example of the launch rule: thirty planned lessons and custom course software would have taken a year, so he launched seven lessons on existing software in a month, added seven more from student feedback, and by the sixth version it supported him (ch. 13, PDF p. 157). **The setup** is then walked through as a checklist. *Money*: Jarvis argues that expenses matter as much as revenue, and lays out the arithmetic of how many clients or units profit actually requires (ch. 13, PDF pp. 158–159). *Legal*: he tells the reader a business belongs in its own entity, a corporation in most countries or an LLC in the United States, so liability stops at the business; money goes into the business and out as salary or dividends; contracts cover service work and terms of service cover products; and a lawyer sits on a small retainer as prevention rather than for suing (ch. 13, PDF pp. 159–160). *Accounting*: he picks an accountant for familiarity with the size and kind of business, consults them every few months rather than once a year, and hires them as a consultant (ch. 13, PDF pp. 160–161). *Salary*: he keeps a separate bank account, sets his base salary at the last twelve months' average profit less 25 to 30 percent for tax, and holds a runway buffer of six months of liquid assets, with extra saved for any vacation, since a business without recurring revenue pays nothing while its owner is not working (ch. 13, PDF pp. 161–162). *Savings*: he puts what is left into index funds through a robo-adviser, arguing that anything returning less than roughly 3 percent loses to inflation (ch. 13, PDF pp. 162–163). *Health coverage*: he cites Jonnie Hallman of Cushion for the cost of health care being the first reason Americans do not start their own companies, against Canada's public system (ch. 13, PDF p. 163). *Lifestyle* closes it: once revenue covers costs, buffer and investments, Jarvis says the founder is presented with choices (ch. 13, PDF pp. 163–164).

### Afterword: Never Grow Up, and Epilogue (PDF pp. 165–170)

The Afterword is built on two Japanese businesses. Nishiyama Onsen Keiunkan, a hotel opened in AD 705 and run by fifty-two generations of one family, has thirty-five rooms and six hot-spring baths, no wifi and no nearby attractions, and has survived by putting service ahead of expansion (Afterword, PDF p. 165). Kongō Gumi, a Buddhist temple-construction company, is its foil: 1,428 years of building temples, then an expansion into real estate during Japan's 1980s bubble, about $343 million of debt, a sale and a liquidation (Afterword, PDF pp. 165–166). Jarvis notes that about 90 percent of businesses worldwide older than 100 years are Japanese, that all have fewer than 300 employees, and that the average lifespan of an S&P 500 company is about fifteen years on Richard Foster's figures (Afterword, PDF p. 166).

The Epilogue generalizes: rather than too big to fail, build something too small to fail, and measure success by Natasha Lampard's "exist strategy" — sticking around, profiting, serving customers — rather than by quarterly increases or an exit (Epilogue, PDF p. 167). Adding more is likened to covering a cut without asking why it happened, and Semler is quoted again that profit past the minimum is not essential to survival (Epilogue, PDF p. 167). "Enough" is named the antithesis of growth and the true north of a company of one (Epilogue, PDF p. 168). The book closes by restating that everyone is a company of one whether or not they own the business, that entrepreneurs are in the author's experience the most risk-averse people he knows, that a business proved to work at its smallest will work if it grows, and that the whole model reduces to one rule about questioning growth before accepting it (Epilogue, PDF pp. 169–170).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Growth itself.** The model is stated as not anti-growth and not anti-revenue, and a company of one is described as questioning growth first and resisting it only where a better way exists (ch. 1, PDF p. 18). Chapter 8 opens by conceding that the answer is sometimes that growth is required, where it aligns with the purpose (ch. 8, PDF p. 98). Companies of one are called not anti-scale but obliged to decide which parts of the business scale and when (ch. 4, PDF pp. 59–60).
- **Where scale really is needed.** Economies of scale are allowed to be necessary in certain markets and for some products, and the objection is to growth forced by ego where it is not needed (ch. 2, PDF p. 34). Airbnb is named as a business that genuinely had to amass inventory before it could matter (ch. 4, PDF p. 55).
- **Whether to do it at all.** Jarvis writes that after nearly twenty years he still does not think working for yourself is the best option for everyone, and that it depends on what a person wants to do and how (ch. 13, PDF p. 153). The people he knows running one spend about half their time or less on the craft they are paid for (ch. 13, PDF p. 154). Working inside an existing organization is offered as a legitimate alternative path (ch. 13, PDF p. 155).
- **Autonomy.** A section headed to that effect says autonomy is not a magic bullet: removing all rules produces anarchy, self-directed teams still require a specific kind of direction, and the balance between guidance and trust is described as delicate rather than binary — too much guidance makes leadership a bottleneck, too little collapses into anarchy (ch. 3, PDF pp. 46–47). Even a company with no employees is said to need constraints (ch. 3, PDF p. 47).
- **Collaboration.** The book names it explicitly as the one area a company of one is told to scale *down* rather than up, against the rest of the chapter it sits in (ch. 8, PDF p. 105). Real-time collaboration is still allowed where a whole team is needed to brainstorm or solve a problem together, and the hackathon is the named form (ch. 8, PDF p. 104).
- **Scalable systems are not automatic.** Turning a system on and watching profit arrive is likened to planting a money tree; work is required at the outset and through iteration, and personality is still required inside automated communication (ch. 8, PDF p. 102). Direct one-to-one communication with customers is called always required, to learn, empathize, adapt and revise (ch. 8, PDF p. 101).
- **Venture capital.** The chapter that recommends crowdfunding says the choice is not as clear-cut as venture capital being bad and crowdfunding good: capital can bring mentorship, connections and the business experience needed to run a company, and the real difficulty is finding investors who share the vision (ch. 11, PDF p. 131).
- **Crowdfunding.** Not a sure thing: only about 35 percent of Kickstarter campaigns are funded, and Olav Sorenson is cited for it suiting consumer-facing products rather than business-focused ones (ch. 11, PDF pp. 131–132). Pebble raised more than $20 million and still did not survive (ch. 11, PDF p. 131).
- **Teaching everything.** The rule carries a carve-out for proprietary information — unexecuted ideas, business strategies and patentable technology — and the author notes that the copyright on this book protects the execution rather than the idea (ch. 9, PDF pp. 108–109).
- **Passion.** After several pages against following it, Jarvis writes that nobody should tell a person not to pursue a passion; the limit is only the entitlement to be paid for it (ch. 5, PDF p. 71).
- **Polarization.** Said to have a polarizing effect that is not always bad, rather than always good, and the first of the three named strategies, placation, is a move toward the people who dislike the product rather than away from them (ch. 6, PDF p. 82).
- **The larger employer.** Working at a big company is credited with real benefits — not having to worry about insurance, administration or expenses — and freelancers are reminded that a higher gross carries office rent, equipment, insurance and unbillable sales cycles (ch. 4, PDF pp. 61–62).
- **Professional help is not optional.** The setup chapter says a lawyer and sometimes an accountant are probably needed to structure the business correctly, and recommends a lawyer on a small retainer (ch. 13, PDF pp. 160–161). Chapter 7 states outright that the book is not offering legal advice before citing the malpractice studies (ch. 7, PDF p. 95).
- **Health coverage.** Insurance is allowed to cost more outside an employer or group plan, and the advice is to shop around; the author notes that his own case is Canadian and the American one is harder (ch. 13, PDF p. 163).
- **Word of mouth.** Named as hard to measure — a recommendation can happen in a coffee shop or an untrackable private message — and as hard to scale quickly, which Jarvis concedes is bad for a business focused on exponential growth (ch. 10, PDF p. 118). A good product and good service are required first, since no incentive creates advocates without them (ch. 10, PDF p. 119).
- **Incentives.** Called tricky: cash incentives can reduce trust when profit is seen as the whole reason for a recommendation (ch. 10, PDF p. 119). The referral pitch in the worked automation goes only to customers who rated their enjoyment above a seven (ch. 10, PDF p. 120).
- **Mistakes.** Avoiding them at all costs, or pretending they do not happen, is called not a viable strategy; having a plan for when they happen is the realistic position (ch. 7, PDF pp. 93–94).
- **Quitting.** Napoleon Hill's line is rejected outright, and the test offered is whether the initial assumption still looks valid and whether the founder would start again knowing what they now know (ch. 11, PDF pp. 136–137).
- **The whole model.** The Epilogue says accepting the mindset does not have to be an either-or decision, invites the reader to adopt some ideas and leave the rest, and states that every business is a lifestyle business and that one choice is not better than another (Epilogue, PDF pp. 169–170).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter. The book argues about how a founder is organized, funded and measured, so most of what it touches sits on the business and go-to-market maps rather than in the product's schema.

1. **A company of one questions growth and stays small on purpose; the two studies the book leans hardest on put premature scaling at 74 percent of high-growth startup failures and no venture money behind almost 86 percent of long-term successes, and investors are named the biggest single reason businesses grow at all** (ch. 1, PDF p. 15; ch. 2, PDF pp. 30–31; ch. 4, PDF p. 56). Record: [#375](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/375), settled 2026-08-28 — "No co-founder taking equity, no employee on payroll, and no outside investment before January", a single-member New York LLC that is a disregarded entity for federal tax, with the S-corp election "ruled out, not deferred", and whether the LLC ever becomes a C-corp answered "Only if the funding position changes."

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool or company the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source, first-party where one exists. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes it. Every check below is a direct fetch of a named page rather than a search result, because search was unavailable from this environment when the section was written, so the unchecked list is longer than it would otherwise be.

- **WordPress powers 26 percent of all websites (ch. 2, PDF p. 35).** Low now. W3Techs reports WordPress at 40.3 percent of all websites and 58.8 percent of those whose content management system is known (w3techs.com/technologies/details/cm-wordpress). The direction of the argument holds; the number is well out of date.
- **Shopify is used by more than 400,000 businesses (ch. 10, PDF p. 122).** Superseded. Shopify's own about page says "millions trust Shopify to power their business", with more than $1 trillion of sales processed and 675 million unique shoppers in 2023 (shopify.com/about). It publishes no merchant count, so the current figure is unchecked, but 400,000 is plainly historical.
- **Pinboard is a one-person service at $3 rising to $11 a year that bought Delicious for $35,000 (ch. 1, PDF p. 25).** Partly holds. Pinboard's tour page still describes it as a small site owned and operated by its developer, and its archival tier now costs $39 a year (pinboard.in/tour). Its API is still described as modeled on version 1 of the Delicious API. The $35,000 purchase price and the Yahoo sale figure of $15–30 million are unchecked.
- **Pieter Levels earns $400,000 a year from Nomad List with no employees and no office (ch. 2, PDF p. 36).** Changed in every particular except the solo part. Nomad List is now Nomads.com, with a free tier and 46,263 members (nomads.com); Levels's own site reports Photo AI at $105,000 a month of revenue and $80,000 a month of profit and a later project reaching $1 million of annual recurring revenue in seventeen days, across a portfolio that did not exist in 2019, still built alone (levels.io).
- **Casper sells three mattresses, backs them with reviews from over 400,000 customers, and gives 100 nights (ch. 11, PDF p. 130).** The simplicity example no longer holds. Casper's mattress page lists nine models across foam, hybrid and max ranges (casper.com/mattresses). The 100-night trial does hold, with a 30-night adjustment period and free return pickup. The 400,000-customer figure is unchecked.
- **Kongō Gumi was sold and liquidated, ending its run as a company (Afterword, PDF p. 166).** Wrong as stated, and it is the book's closing argument. Kongō Gumi's own site describes it as still operating as a temple-construction specialist with roughly 100 artisans, founded in 578 CE, within the Takamatsu Group (kongogumi.co.jp). The 1980s real-estate expansion, the debt and the sale are not contradicted here; the end of the company is. The $343 million debt figure is unchecked.
- **Nishiyama Onsen Keiunkan, opened AD 705, thirty-five rooms, six hot-spring baths, fifty-two generations (Afterword, PDF p. 165).** Mostly holds. The hotel's own English site gives 705 AD, 1,300 years, and six baths — four open-air and two indoor (keiunkan.co.jp). It does not state a room count or a generation count on that page, so those two are unchecked.
- **HighRise films a personal welcome video for every new customer (ch. 12, PDF p. 146).** Gone. Highrise stopped accepting new signups on 20 August 2018, before the book was published, and now serves existing accounts only under 37signals (highrisehq.com). There are no new customers to film.
- **CopyBlogger, renamed RainMaker Digital, at more than $12 million of annual revenue and 200,000 customers (ch. 9, PDF p. 108).** The name went back and the owner changed. The site is Copyblogger again, and its about page says Darrell Vesterfelt took majority ownership in 2026; it sells a $49-a-month academy, a $3,500 sprint and $3,000-a-month coaching, and does not mention Rainmaker Digital at all (copyblogger.com/about). The revenue and customer figures are unchecked.
- **Rand Fishkin, the onetime chief executive of MOZ (ch. 3, PDF p. 51; ch. 6, PDF p. 78).** Moved on. He is chief executive and co-founder of SparkToro, and is described there as formerly co-founder and chief executive of Moz (sparktoro.com/about). The $300,000-to-$48-million revenue figures for 2006 to 2014 are unchecked.
- **Ugmonk is a boutique clothing line whose stylish shirts get photographed and shared, and whose Gather desk organizer raised over $430,000 on Kickstarter (ch. 7, PDF p. 92; ch. 11, PDF pp. 126, 131).** The business has moved category. Ugmonk's storefront now sells Analog card systems, journals, notebooks, pens and desk accessories under a focus line about analog tools; neither T-shirts nor Gather appear on the home page or the Gather URL the book's story points at (ugmonk.com). The Kickstarter total is unchecked.
- **Balsamiq is privately owned, has never taken funding, and pays out beyond the founder's $1 million to a twenty-five-person team (ch. 4, PDF p. 60).** The ownership claim holds and the headcount does not resolve. Balsamiq's company page says it has never taken outside funding, has never optimized for growth at the expense of the product, and has been bootstrapped and independent for seventeen years; it describes the team only as small and remote (balsamiq.com/company). The team size, the $1 million draw and the eighteen-month runway are unchecked.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Two incompatible numbers for the same claim.** Chapter 4 cites an Econsultancy and Responsys report for a new customer costing five times as much as keeping an existing one (ch. 4, PDF p. 56). Chapter 7 makes the same argument and puts the gap at "6 to 7 percent more", attributed to the White House study cited a page earlier (ch. 7, PDF p. 86). Five times and seven percent cannot both be the cost of acquisition over retention, and the second is far too small to carry the sentence it is in.
- **A statistic restated as a different statistic.** Bolander and Satornino are cited for as much as 26.6 percent of the *variance* in sales performance coming from social capital, and the next sentence converts that into sales "sometimes as much as one-third higher" (ch. 12, PDF p. 145). Variance explained is not an increase in sales, and the conversion is not one the cited work supports.
- **Correlation presented as cause.** The empathy index's five most profitable companies ranking top on empathy is read as empathy paying off by increasing LinkedIn's bottom line (ch. 7, PDF pp. 89–90). Kotter and Heskett's factor of twelve on stock price is given with no period and no control (ch. 5, PDF p. 67). Purpose-driven firms doing well in the 2008–2009 recession is offered without any comparison group (ch. 5, PDF p. 65).
- **The closing case is not what the book says it is.** The Afterword's whole structure rests on Kongō Gumi's run ending in liquidation after it chased growth (Afterword, PDF p. 166). Section 7 finds the company still building temples inside a larger group. The pairing with a surviving hotel is also survivorship on both sides: one hotel that lasted and one company that did not are two observations, and the failure rate of small businesses that never grew does not appear anywhere in the book.
- **The samples are selected on the outcome.** The two studies the growth argument rests on are of high-growth tech startups and of the fastest-growing 5,000 companies (ch. 2, PDF pp. 30–31). Both samples are chosen *because* the companies grew, so neither can say anything about the failure rate of companies that stayed small — which is the comparison the argument needs. The book's own note for the Kauffman claim points at an entrepreneurship-policy digest on the importance of young firms rather than at a five-to-eight-year follow-up study (Notes, PDF p. 175).
- **Attribution that does not match the note.** The 42 percent automation figure is credited in the text to Ryerson University while the note names the Brookfield Institute (ch. 1, PDF p. 20; Notes, PDF p. 174). The 62 percent American figure is credited to the Council of Economic Advisers with the whole Economic Report of the President cited and no page (Notes, PDF p. 174). Oates is credited with finding that hustlers do not outperform non-hustlers and with specific results on job stress, work-life conflict and self-efficacy, while the note gives only his 1971 book, which is where the word was coined (ch. 3, PDF p. 50; Notes, PDF p. 176). The origin of "social capital" is cited to Wikipedia (Notes, PDF p. 183).
- **Figures with a thin source or none.** More than half of Google's product releases coming out of twenty-percent time (ch. 1, PDF p. 22), 79 percent of the Fortune 1,000 and 81 percent of manufacturing organizations running self-directed teams beyond a single hospitality-journal citation (ch. 3, PDF p. 46), more than 96 percent of managers being extroverted (ch. 3, PDF p. 45), and email accounting for more than 93 percent of the author's revenue (ch. 8, PDF p. 102) are all stated flat.
- **A definition nothing could fail.** A company of one is a single freelancer's successor, a twenty-five-person software company, a seventy-two-person one, an employee inside a corporation, and — the Epilogue says — technically everyone (ch. 1, PDF pp. 10, 15–17; ch. 2, PDF p. 31; ch. 4, PDF p. 60; Epilogue, PDF p. 169). With that reach, no observed business could contradict the thesis, which is also why the book can claim both Buffer and Balsamiq as evidence while they differ threefold in size.
- **Dates missing from moving numbers.** Buffer is given more than three million users in chapter 2 and more than 1.2 million in chapter 12, neither dated, which reads as a contradiction whatever the two passages meant (ch. 2, PDF p. 31; ch. 12, PDF p. 145). Chapter 12 also reports 1.6 million Kiva lenders against 2.5 million borrowers with no date (ch. 12, PDF p. 142).
- **The named cases are the author's own contacts.** The Acknowledgments list the interviewees, and they are most of the book's protagonists: Brogan, O'Neill, Womersley, Haas, Hixon, Fishburne, Beauchamp, Devlen, Clark, LaPorte, Urban, Clear, Fried, Sheldon, Abel, D'Souza, Glei, Murphy, Maud, Fishkin and Orwell (Acknowledgments, PDF p. 172). No company of one that tried the model and failed is interviewed, and the counter-examples in the book are all large companies that grew.
- **The upper bound rests on two observations.** The idea the book is most original about carries one corporate case, Southwest declining more than 95 percent of the cities asking for service in 1996, and one personal one, a blogger's subscriber targets (ch. 2, PDF pp. 39–40). No company that set an upper bound and was hurt by it appears, and the airline's later decades are not revisited.
- **The word that carries the argument is never defined.** "Enough" is the Epilogue's true north and the Prologue's organizing idea, and the book's own account of it is that it differs for everyone and is found by asking why more is wanted (Prologue, PDF pp. 8–9; Epilogue, PDF pp. 168–169). That leaves the central instruction with no procedure attached, which is a gap the chapter on minimum viable profit partly fills for money and nothing fills for time, scope or customer count.
