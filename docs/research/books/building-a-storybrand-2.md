## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's own frame, is that a business grows when it stops explaining itself and instead states its offer in the seven categories of a story formula, with the customer as the hero and the brand as the guide. Miller states the thesis in one place: defining the elements of a story as they relate to a brand creates, in his words, a "mental map" a customer can follow to engage the product (ch. 2, PDF p. 24). The mechanism he gives is not aesthetic but metabolic. He reports a conversation with Mike McHargue, who told him the brain's overriding job is to help a person survive and thrive and that processing information burns calories, so a message that is neither about survival nor simple gets tuned out (ch. 1, PDF pp. 20–22). Story, on this account, is a sense-making device that lets a reader follow without burning many calories (ch. 1, PDF p. 24). The seven categories are the SB7 framework: a character who wants something, has a problem, meets a guide, who gives them a plan, calls them to action, that helps them avoid failure, and ends in a success (ch. 2, PDF p. 33; ch. 3, PDF pp. 42–51). The output is a one-page document called the BrandScript, and the 2.0 edition adds two modules to it, an identity transformation and a controlling idea (ch. 11, PDF p. 160; ch. 12, PDF p. 163). The claimed scope is universal: Miller writes that the framework works regardless of industry and has been used to sell, in his list, everything from plungers to foreign policy to airplane engines (Introduction, PDF p. 16; Note from the Author, PDF p. 14).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a book about telling the company's story, which he calls a waste of time because customers care about their own (Introduction, PDF p. 16). It is not an appeal to art or fancy: he says business leaders hear the word story and file him with artists, and he means a "concrete formula" for getting attention instead (ch. 2, PDF p. 24). It is not a claim that the product or the design is the lever; his position is that pretty websites do not sell and words do, and that an inferior product wins if its offer is communicated more clearly (ch. 1, PDF pp. 18–19). It is not a claim to originality: he presents story formulas as a summation of best practices tested by the entertainment industry since antiquity, next to Six Sigma and Situational Leadership (ch. 2, PDF p. 25). And it is not offered as a full screenwriting method — he says a whole screenplay would need more than seven plot points, and that seven are what a brand needs (ch. 2, PDF p. 33).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Note from the Author on This Updated Edition (PDF pp. 14–15)

Miller reports that since the 2017 first edition the book has sold more than a million copies, and he estimates, without a source, that it has helped companies realize billions in increased revenue and millions of new customers (PDF p. 14). What is new in 2.0: the examples and stories are, he says, nearly doubled; the free online BrandScript tool has been used by nearly a million businesses and has been rebuilt; and his own teachings, books, videos, podcasts and recorded coaching sessions have been used to train a product he calls the StoryBrand Brain, which he describes as the greatest artificial-intelligence tool for messaging and marketing on the internet (PDF p. 14). The Brain's output, listed identically here, at the end of ch. 14, at the end of ch. 15, and on a back-matter page, is a tagline, one-liner, wireframed website, lead generator, follow-up emails, sales script, narrative scripts for video and social media, podcast prompts, urgency ideas, upsell ideas, social posts, and industry trends (PDF pp. 14, 184, 216, 218–219). The audience is widened in this edition to include a person starting a brand, growing one, positioning as a leader, working as a sales rep, or growing a nonprofit (PDF p. 15).

### Introduction (PDF p. 16)

One PDF page. The customer should be the hero, not the brand, which Miller calls the secret every phenomenally successful business understands. He states that his company helps more than ten thousand businesses a year and asks the reader to do three things: read and understand SB7, filter the message through it, and execute.

### Chapter 1: The Key to Being Seen, Heard, and Understood (PDF pp. 18–28)

The diagnosis: companies waste money on marketing because the problem is not the product but the way the product is talked about, and the designers being paid have not read a book on sales copy (PDF p. 18). Evidence is autobiographical — his first book sold about ten thousand copies, and after he learned story structure the next was on the bestseller list for nearly a year; after he applied the framework inside his own company it doubled in revenue for four consecutive years (PDF pp. 19, 24). The mechanism comes from McHargue: Maslow's hierarchy as a scanning order, the brain discarding millions of facts a day, and the ballroom analogy in which a person never counts the chairs but always knows where the exits are (PDF pp. 20–21). Two mistakes follow: failing to focus on what helps a person survive and thrive, and requiring the customer to burn too many calories to understand the offer (PDF pp. 20–22). The worked example is Spectrum Brands, where Miller says adding three words to aquarium packaging and signage produced a 99 percent increase in overall sales in a test market (PDF p. 23).

The rest of the chapter argues clarity. Formulas are a summation of best practices and story formulas have been tested since antiquity (PDF p. 25). An audience must always be able to say who the hero is, what the hero wants, who must be defeated, and what happens either way, and a company's customers have the same burning questions (PDF pp. 25–26). The StoryBrand mantra is that confusion loses (PDF p. 26). The named enemy of a business is noise, which Miller says has killed more ideas than taxes, recessions, lawsuits, interest rates or inferior design, and which businesses create themselves (PDF p. 26). The industrial painter who said his three revenue streams were too diverse to reduce is the example: his site had a painting of his building, a thousand links, FAQs, a company timeline and nonprofit links, and the workshop agreed unanimously that a single blunt line plus a quote button would grow the business (PDF pp. 26–27). The chapter ends on the writer's rule that the more that is cut, the better the result, and on the Pascal letter anecdote, which Miller hedges as often credited rather than sourced (PDF pp. 27–28).

### Chapter 2: The Secret Weapon That Will Grow Your Business (PDF pp. 29–41)

Story as filter. Miller asserts that neuroscientists claim the average person spends more than 30 percent of their time daydreaming unless engaged by a story, because the story does the daydreaming for them (PDF p. 29). Music is noise submitted to rules, and a final cut is called that because storytellers use frameworks to cut what does not serve the plot (PDF p. 30). Apple is the pivot example: the 1983 Lisa launched with a nine-page technical ad in the *New York Times* and bombed; after Pixar, Jobs came back and the campaign was two words on billboards, and Apple stopped featuring computers in most advertising (PDF pp. 31–32). Miller's reading is that Apple plays Q to the customer's Bond, and that people buy not the best product but the one they can understand and apply fastest (PDF p. 32).

The seven plot points are stated as story in a nutshell and mapped onto *The Hunger Games* and *Star Wars: A New Hope* (PDF pp. 33–35). Miller says *Top Gun: Maverick* may be the most formulaic movie in the history of man and grossed $1.46 billion (PDF p. 35), and that indie films that break narrative formulas fail at the box office while critics who like them are "snobs" watching everything (PDF p. 36). *Welcome to Wrexham* is offered as proof that documentary obeys the same rules (PDF p. 36). Two tests close the chapter. The three crucial questions — what the hero wants, who opposes, and what life looks like either way — must be answerable at any pause, and within the first fifteen to twenty minutes (PDF p. 37). The grunt test is the customer-facing version: within five seconds of looking at a website, a person should be able to answer what you offer, how it makes their life better, and what to do to buy it, phrased as whether a caveman could grunt the offer back (PDF p. 38). Two client results: Kyle Shultz rewrote his photography-course sales page the night before a launch, removed 90 percent of the text and the inside language, and sold $103,000 to the same list that had produced $25,000 six months earlier (PDF p. 39); Nicole Burke's Gardenary stopped leading with her own mission to make gardening ordinary and took the domain StartAGarden.com, and Miller says her business is poised to quadruple (PDF pp. 39–40).

### Chapter 3: The Simple SB7 Framework (PDF pp. 42–55)

An overview chapter that states the seven principles as headings and previews each. Principle one, the customer is the hero, not your brand, is called a major paradigm shift and supported by Nancy Duarte's advice to speak as Yoda to an audience of Lukes (PDF pp. 42–43). Principle two, people buy solutions to problems; the hero in a hole is the shape, and there are three levels of problem, external, internal and philosophical, of which companies address only the first (PDF pp. 44–45). Principle three, customers are looking for a guide, not another hero (PDF pp. 45–46). Principle four, customers trust a guide who gives them a plan, and two plans are coming, the agreement plan and the process plan (PDF p. 47). Principle five, customers do not act unless challenged, and phrases such as learn more or get started are called passive-aggressive suggestions rather than calls to action (PDF p. 48). Principle six, every human being is trying to avoid a tragic ending, with Wendy's, Whole Foods and Trader Joe's as stakes examples, and a pre-emptive answer to the objection that stakes sound negative: the deeper the hole, the more powerful the perceived outcome, and all negativity becomes positivity once overcome (PDF pp. 48–50). Principle seven, never assume people understand how the brand changes their lives; tell them, with Reagan, Clinton, Roosevelt, Apple, Weight Watchers and Men's Wearhouse as vision examples (PDF p. 50).

The chapter then introduces the BrandScript as a tool that now carries AI prompts, states that nearly a million people used the old form, and that it is free with the book at StoryBrand.AI (PDF p. 51). Order of work: the overall brand first, then each division, then each product, and optionally each customer segment (PDF p. 52). Miller says he used a BrandScript to outline this book and more of them to write each chapter (PDF p. 53). The closing warning is against skipping ahead: each module has, in his words, "set-in-stone rules", and he estimates most readers stop after the first twenty pages, which he offers as the reader's advantage over a competitor (PDF pp. 54–55).

### Chapter 4: A Character (PDF pp. 58–69)

The first module. A story does not begin until the hero wants something, and screenwriters define that ambition within the first nine or so minutes (PDF p. 58). The high-end resort that had been showing its restaurant, front desk and staff replaced the page with images of rest and one line about luxury and rest, which then became the line every staff member could repeat (PDF pp. 58–59). Other one-line desires from client work: a hassle-free MBA completed after work, a yard better than the neighbor's, mobile fine dining, a plan for your retirement, a story to get lost in (PDF pp. 59–60).

The story gap is the chapter's mechanism: a gap between the hero and what they want, and attention rises and falls with its opening and closing (PDF p. 60). Miller's gift-retailer example opens the gap with a sidewalk sign promising twenty ideas under a hundred dollars and closes it with numbered cards in the store, and he reports a significant increase in sales on the numbered items (PDF pp. 60–61). He extends the mechanism to sonata form, to a Byron rhyme, to a dad joke, and then to all of human behavior — hunger opens a gap and lunch closes it (PDF p. 61). Two mistakes follow. The first is failing to pare the desire to a single focus: a leader who says he provides twenty-seven things is told that listing too many benefits backfires, and that subsequent BrandScripts per division and per product are where the rest goes (PDF pp. 62–63). The second is choosing a desire unrelated to survival: the leadership expert whose tagline was about inhaling knowledge and exhaling success is redirected to a line about becoming everyone's favorite leader, which Miller connects to tribe, status and opportunity (PDF p. 64). Survival is then defined as the physical, economic and social resources to eat, drink, reproduce and fend off foes, and enumerated in seven categories with a brand for each: conserving money (Walmart), conserving time (the Full Focus Planner), building social networks (the Coach Builder Community, and every church, league, AA chapter and Girl Scout troop), gaining status (Mercedes, Rolex, Louis Vuitton), accumulating resources (business-to-business offers, financial advisers), showing generosity (the NFL softening a violent sport), and desiring meaning (Frankl against Freud, and Patagonia) (PDF pp. 64–67). The chapter closes with the hitchhiker image — the one burning question is where you are going, not your mission statement or your grandfather — and the Filson tag as an example of a brand printing the words it wants repeated (PDF p. 68).

### Chapter 5: Has a Problem (PDF pp. 71–85)

The problem is the hook. Miller's carriage-house parlor trick is to have a guest open any of a thousand books to any page, and he says the paragraph is always about conflict, and he has never been wrong (PDF pp. 71–72). He extends the point to politicians, who want constituents to believe the country is failing, and to twenty-four-hour news, which he classes as entertainment, while noting that the world is not nearly as bad as the media suggest and citing the novelist James Scott Bell that readers want to fret (PDF p. 72). The unnamed government agency under a nondisclosure agreement is the chapter's set piece: a day of whiteboards produced a single public-facing threat statement about a global force empowering authoritarian regimes, and Miller reports that the clarity of it changed his own attention (PDF p. 73).

The villain gives conflict a focus (PDF p. 74). Time thieves, personified as snipers, are the worked example for time-management software; animated dust bunnies in leather jackets, hairballs in drains and talking plaque are cited as the technique already running in television advertising (PDF pp. 74–75). The list of villain characteristics is numbered 1 through 5 under a heading that announces four: root source rather than symptom, relatable, singular, real and never fearmongering, and, added fifth, that a villain need not be serious (PDF pp. 75–76). Three levels of conflict follow. External is the tangible barrier — hunger for a restaurant, a leaky pipe for a plumber, termites for a pest controller (PDF p. 77). Internal is the frustration the external problem causes, and the chapter's governing principle is that companies sell solutions to external problems while people buy solutions to internal ones (PDF p. 78). The hero's recurring question is whether they have what it takes (PDF p. 78). Apple's answer to intimidation, National Car Rental's answer to forced small talk, CarMax's answer to the fear of a used-car salesman, and Starbucks' answer to wanting somewhere to sit are the examples, and the CarMax passage carries the claim that solving an internal problem lets a brand charge more, with Starbucks charging four or five dollars for what Americans used to pay fifty cents for (PDF pp. 78–81). The client example is Vitality Aesthetics, where adding two words to a header to say a customer could get their natural beauty back, plus a price list gated behind an email address and thirty weekly emails, is credited with renewed growth (PDF pp. 79–80). Philosophical is the ought and shouldn't level, the reason a story resonates, illustrated by *Tommy Boy*, *Hamlet*, *Bridget Jones* and *The King's Speech*, and in commerce by a consulting firm on deserving a great manager, a pet store on pets deserving healthy food, and Tower Records' tagline about music and life (PDF pp. 82–83). The perfect brand promise resolves all three at once, as the obligatory scene does in *Star Wars* (PDF p. 83), and three brands are laid out in the villain-external-internal-philosophical shape: Tesla, Nespresso and Edward Jones (PDF p. 84). The chapter closes by warning against three villains, seven external problems and four internal ones: choices must be made (PDF p. 85).

### Chapter 6: And Meets a Guide (PDF pp. 88–101)

Guides are introduced through Bell's "doorways of no return" and a block quotation from Christopher Booker's *The Seven Basic Plots* (PDF pp. 88–89). Miller's list of real guides runs from parents to teachers to poets to therapists to brands (PDF p. 89). The fatal mistake is positioning the brand as hero, and the named failure is Tidal: Jay-Z's $56 million personal investment, sixteen musicians taking equity, a press conference in which the artists stood shoulder to shoulder, and a line about water being free that social media turned on him (PDF pp. 90–91). Miller's reading is that Tidal existed to help the artists win the day rather than the customer, that it should have positioned existing streaming services as the villain and sold sound quality, and that he subscribes himself for the larger downloads (PDF p. 91). He adds that it would be a good private-equity play to buy brands making the hero mistake, change the messaging, and sell them at a predictable increase (PDF p. 92).

The second argument is that heroes are weak: in a good story the hero is, in Miller's view, the second-weakest character after the victim, while the guide has already conquered the same challenge in their own backstory (PDF pp. 92–93). A film he does not name is offered as the counter-case that proves the rule — its guide also transformed, and the audience could not tell hero from guide (PDF p. 93). *Mary Poppins* is the model: George Banks is the hero, the measuring tape scene establishes the guide's authority, and Poppins refuses to explain herself (PDF pp. 93–94).

The two characteristics of a guide are empathy and competency. **The 2.0 edition changes the second word.** Miller writes that in the first edition he listed empathy and authority, that authority never sat right with him because nobody likes a know-it-all, and that the better word is competency (PDF p. 97). Empathy is illustrated by Clinton's "I feel your pain" and by a town-hall exchange on the national debt (PDF pp. 95–96), by Oprah Winfrey's formulation that people want to be seen, heard and understood (PDF p. 96), by openers such as "We understand how it feels to" (PDF p. 96), and by the search for commonality — a dinner-party conversation, a politician told to find a shared experience before disagreeing, and a Discover Card campaign in which the customer reaches an exact replica of themselves (PDF pp. 96–97). Competency is illustrated by the nutritionist who pinches her own spare tire, and by Nēmah, a skin-care line founded after the founder could not find products safe in pregnancy, which Miller reads as former heroes turned guides (PDF pp. 98–99). Five ways to show competency are listed: testimonials, of which three is called a great number and which should be short; statistics, with the example of an email platform's claim that 125,000 users trust its software; awards; press mentions; and logos of businesses served (PDF pp. 99–100). A guide, Miller says, can talk about itself all day long, as long as it tells only the parts of its story that support care and competency, and he notes that Mary Poppins gets more screen time than George Banks (PDF p. 98). Amy Cuddy's *Presence* supplies the closing frame: two subconscious questions, can I trust this person and can I respect this person, which empathy and competency answer in turn (PDF pp. 100–101).

### Chapter 7: Who Gives Them a Plan (PDF pp. 104–112)

The plan exists to remove cognitive dissonance. The image is a customer on the bank of a rushing creek who can hear a waterfall downstream, and the plan is stones placed in the creek (PDF pp. 104–106). Miller's own example is a wearable fitness tracker he wanted for years and did not buy until the site showed three steps, after which setup took three minutes (PDF p. 105). He reports that a national mattress brand's sales went up when representatives recited a three-step plan — choose, deliver, remove the old mattress — immediately before asking for the order (PDF p. 106). In stories, the guide gives a plan: Peter Brand to Billy Beane in *Moneyball*, Luke's three steps at the Death Star trench (PDF pp. 106–107).

Two kinds of plan. A **process plan** describes the steps to buy, the steps to use after buying, or a mix; examples are schedule, customize, execute; download, integrate, revolutionize; and test-drive, purchase, enjoy free maintenance (PDF pp. 108–109). The recommended length is at least three and no more than six steps, with anything longer broken into phases, and Miller adds in the next sentences that studies show buying decreases when customers are bombarded and that more than four steps may add to confusion (PDF p. 109). An **agreement plan** is a list of agreements that alleviate fear rather than confusion; CarMax's four-point plan is the commercial example, Newt Gingrich's Contract with America the political one, and Whole Foods' list of values the shared-values one (PDF p. 110). An agreement plan works in the background and need not be on the home page, though it may be on a wall, on packaging, or on shopping bags (PDF pp. 110–111). Either plan should be given a title, such as an easy installation plan or a quality guarantee, which Miller says increases the perceived seriousness of the offer (PDF p. 111).

### Chapter 8: And Calls Them to Action (PDF pp. 114–125)

Characters do not act unless challenged, so customers must be asked (PDF pp. 114–115). Miller asserts that customers are bombarded with more than three thousand commercial messages a day and that soft calls to action go unnoticed (PDF p. 115). The governing advice, from a mentor, is not to put people in the mind-reading business, and the chapter contrasts an opener such as "If you're interested" with a scripted yes/no close (PDF p. 115). The sales-training set piece: he has a room of professionals write a follow-up email live and hit send, and he says the last such session, 250 account executives at a broadband provider called Calix, closed more than $2.5 million off the emails sent during a single hour, with the CFO calling days later (PDF p. 116). He adds that he and his team guarantee to close more in sales during a training than they were paid to be there, and have never failed (PDF p. 116).

Mechanics: a Buy Now button in the top right, uncluttered, in a different font or brighter color than the others, repeated above the fold, in the center of the header, and again down the page (PDF pp. 116–117). Miller says of the thousands of clients he has worked with, not one has oversold, and most think they are overselling when their calls to action fall softer than a whisper (PDF p. 118). Passive selling communicates a lack of belief in the product (PDF p. 118). Two kinds of call to action follow. **Direct** calls ask for the sale — order now, call today, schedule an appointment, register today, buy now — and can go in email signatures and on business cards (PDF pp. 119, 121). **Transitional** calls ask for a smaller commitment, and the running metaphor is dating against marriage, set out as a scripted exchange (PDF p. 119). Miller's own case is that StoryBrand became a multimillion-dollar company in its second year on a transitional call to action alone: a free PDF about what a website should include, with an advertisement for the workshop at the back, after which workshop revenue doubled in twelve months with no marketing spend (PDF p. 122). A transitional call does three things: stakes a claim to territory, creates reciprocity, and positions the brand as guide (PDF p. 122). Types listed: free information, testimonials, samples, and a limited-time free trial as a risk-removal policy (PDF pp. 122–123). The design house he hired is the narrative proof — the prettier site had no clear call to action, the plainer one offered a PDF, then helpful emails, then a bright Schedule a Call button he eventually pressed, and he never went back (PDF pp. 120–121). The clinic example closes the chapter: remove the television and magazines from the waiting room, replace them with a self-assessment checklist reviewed by a nurse, tag the answers into an email marketing system, and let an automated campaign carry clear calls to action (PDF pp. 123–124).

### Chapter 9: That Helps Them Avoid Failure (PDF pp. 127–137)

Stakes. Miller opens on why so many stories begin with an orphan, then on how storytellers stretch a premise to feature length by interjecting conflict and dangling stakes (PDF pp. 127–128). Each scene must answer the stakes question, and a brand that does not paint the stakes fails to answer the "so what" question every customer is secretly asking (PDF p. 129). The extended example is Allstate's Mayhem campaign and its 2015 Project Share Aware stunt with Leo Burnett: a couple was told they had won a prize, their home was secretly photographed and rebuilt on a soundstage, they were given a private box at the Sugar Bowl, and their belongings were auctioned on national television while hidden cameras caught their panic (PDF pp. 129–130). The reported results are six to ten thousand hits per second after each commercial, more than eighteen million hits during the game, the campaign hashtag going to number one worldwide, twenty-four thousand new followers, more than twenty million Facebook impressions and almost seventy thousand likes (PDF p. 130).

The theory is loss aversion. Miller attributes prospect theory to Daniel Kahneman in 1979 and states that people are, in certain situations, two to three times more motivated to avoid a loss than to achieve a gain (PDF p. 132). Lyndon Johnson's conversation with George Wallace over the Civil Rights Act is offered as loss aversion used on a person rather than a market (PDF p. 132). The operational device is a four-step "fear appeal" taken from Infante, Rancer and Womack's *Building Communication Theory*: establish vulnerability, say action reduces it, name the specific action, then challenge the reader to take it, worked through with termite statistics (PDF pp. 132–133). Fear is then explicitly rationed — the heading calls it salt in the recipe — and the book block-quotes the same source to the effect that very high and very low fear both fail and moderate amounts work best (PDF pp. 133–134). Miller's own rule is to add a little more negativity when he is slightly uncomfortable, then stop, because he estimates a reader feels about half the negativity the writer does (PDF p. 134). The bank-robber parable follows: a story with more negative than positive elements reads as a feel-good story because of its ending, so the darker the story, the brighter the light at the end (PDF pp. 134–135). Four client lists of what customers avoid close the chapter — a used-car dealer, a home audio-video firm, an aerospace manufacturer and a children's summer camp — with the note that the failure module gets only a few bullet points on purpose while the success module gets more (PDF pp. 136–137).

### Chapter 10: And Ends in a Success (PDF pp. 139–151)

The vision module. Clarifying a message is framed as leadership, with Reagan's city on a hill and Clinton's bridge as the political examples and Stew Friedman's phrase about a compelling image of an achievable future as the academic one (PDF p. 139). Nike, Starbucks and Men's Wearhouse are the commercial ones (PDF pp. 139–140). The vision must be specific: Kennedy's moon sentence against an imagined vision for a productive space program (PDF p. 140). Ryan Deiss's before-and-after grid is reproduced as the tool — what the customer has, feels, does in an average day, and what their status is, before and after the brand (PDF pp. 140–141). Images matter too, and the advice is to show people happily engaging with the product (PDF pp. 141–142).

The success module should close all three problem levels at once, as the obligatory scene does, and a fictional toothpaste paragraph is broken down into its external, internal and philosophical halves (PDF pp. 142–144). Three dominant resolutions are then named as three psychological desires: **status**, offered through access, scarcity, a premium tier title, or identity association, with Starbucks' card, a Jeep badge, an Emerald Club membership and a suggested Anchor Donor title for nonprofits as examples (PDF pp. 144–146); **union that makes the hero whole**, offered through reduced anxiety, reduced workload, or more time, with dish detergent advertised as anti-anxiety medication and the brand cast as Q (PDF pp. 146–148); and **self-realization or acceptance**, offered through inspiration, acceptance, or transcendence, with Dove's forensic-artist films, American Eagle's Aerie campaign, TOMS Shoes and FUBU as examples (PDF pp. 148–149). The chapter then tells the reader not to overthink it: a sound bite that screams resolution comes first, and the three levels are a refinement (PDF p. 150).

### Chapter 11: People Want Your Brand to Participate in Their Transformation (PDF pp. 153–162)

**A module the first edition did not have.** The final BrandScript element is an identity transformation, stated as a from-X-to-Y line (PDF pp. 153, 160–161). The argument is that the desire to transform is the single motivating factor behind nearly every human decision, and that brands which deliver an aspirational identity create evangelists (PDF p. 153). Taylor Swift's fans are the illustration, along with the claim that the primitive brain has trouble separating a hero from the self, which is why people get defensive when a public figure they identify with is diminished (PDF pp. 153–154). Miller's own example is a Gerber knife he wanted at Home Depot despite being, in his words, a writer who needs a knife for peanut butter; the *Hello Trouble* campaign's narration is quoted at length as poetry, and an Army Ranger later gave him an engraved one (PDF pp. 155–157). He asks whether a forty-dollar knife never used is a waste of money, reports that hundreds of people familiar with the framework have all said no, and agrees (PDF p. 157). The practical question offered is how a customer wants to be described by their friends (PDF p. 157).

Dave Ramsey is the long-form example, from a consulting engagement Miller says gave vocabulary to what the team already did (PDF p. 158). The pieces named: a radio show with more than eight million daily listeners; a returning-from-break line that supplies a new status symbol; external, internal and philosophical problems identified as consumer debt, hopelessness, and credit-card companies selling debt people cannot afford; and a placed climactic scene, the Debt-Free Scream, performed on the show after listeners complete Financial Peace University (PDF pp. 158–159). The scene after the climax is the chapter's own plot point: the guide returns to tell the hero they have changed, as Obi-Wan does for Luke, Lionel for King George and Peter Brand for Billy Beane, because heroes do not recognize their own transformation (PDF pp. 159–160). Three from/to examples close the chapter — a pet food brand, a financial adviser, a shampoo brand — and the instruction is to write the "to" line first, since the "from" line is its opposite (PDF pp. 161–162).

### Chapter 12: Define Your Controlling Idea (PDF pp. 163–166)

**The second module the first edition did not have**, and Miller says it is the one he would keep if a reader created only one piece of copy (PDF p. 163). A controlling idea answers what the story is about; *The Lion King* is the worked example (PDF p. 163). Once set, all copy submits to it, and combining plots makes an audience burn calories working out what the story is (PDF pp. 163–164). The client example is Jeff Tomaszewski's MaxStrength Fitness, whose differentiator — twenty minutes, twice a week, resistance rather than cardio — was buried below the fold, and which Miller says belonged in the header, in every section, in the lead generator's title, in the follow-up emails, on the about page and in the CEO video (PDF pp. 164–165). Good messaging is described as an exercise in memorization: repeat the idea until the audience has it by heart and can repeat it to friends (PDF pp. 164–166). The controlling idea is derived from the finished BrandScript by asking what the story is really about, and it has been added as a field to the tool (PDF pp. 165–166).

### Chapter 13: How to Execute a Flawless StoryBrand Messaging Campaign (PDF pp. 168–174)

A clear message is worthless until it is repeated, and the goal is stated as installing the message in other minds — if you are building a brand, Miller writes, act as though your products are running for office (PDF p. 168). The example is an unnamed presidential favorite, a former governor of a large state with national name recognition, more than a hundred million dollars and party support, whose campaign was shrinking; his team said he was not a sound-bite kind of guy but a scholar who preferred town halls and nuance, and Miller told them they were preparing the man to be president and doing nothing to get him elected (PDF pp. 168–169). The repetition arithmetic follows: Miller says he has come to believe people must read or hear a message about eight times before they internalize it, that people ignore most commercial messages, and that if 90 percent are ignored the message must be repeated eighty times to be heard eight (PDF p. 170). The places named for that repetition are elevator pitches, conversation, the website, lead generators, videos, swag, retail walls, email, physical mail, sales reps, digital ads, podcast openings and keynotes (PDF p. 170).

Three steps: create the BrandScript; edit the sound bites until they get the reaction wanted, treating the first draft as a rough draft, since the commonest failure Miller sees is sound bites that are too elusive, and the only question a listener should have left is how to buy (PDF pp. 171–172); and repeat them until the public has them memorized (PDF p. 173). The Uber driver in Baltimore is the sound-bite test — the driver turned down the stereo, pulled over, and asked for messaging help with his girlfriend — and Miller's conclusion is that a working sound bite makes people turn down the radio (PDF p. 171). The memorization argument is illustrated by his household's poem-for-money arrangement and by the alphabet song, which he calls a marketing jingle that has worked for a century (PDF p. 173).

### Chapter 14: How to Get Your Website Right (PDF pp. 175–184)

Miller's stated first reason for caring about a website is not conversion but clarification: wireframing forces the offer into order, and the language then travels into conversations, emails and internal talking points (PDF p. 175). The chapter directs the reader to the AI tool to move past the blank page and to a certified coach to edit afterwards (PDF p. 175). Five best practices follow.

**An offer above the fold** (PDF pp. 176–178). The negative example is a cooking-school membership whose home page showed a carrot cake and an inside joke, and whose video explained how the founders met; Miller's wife eventually described the subscription in one sentence that he says should have been the header. The image is a first date: short, enticing, exclusively customer-centric. The text must promise an aspirational identity, promise to solve a problem, or state exactly what the business does, and a clothing-and-hair shop called Local Honey is the example of the third. Squarespace's header is offered as a model. **Obvious calls to action** (PDF pp. 178–180). Two placements, top right and the center of the header, following the eye's Z pattern; the buttons a different and preferably brighter color from any other, all identical to each other, repeated down the page because people scan rather than read; the transitional call to action next to the direct one in a less bright button. **Images of success** (PDF pp. 180–181). Pictures of the building waste mental bandwidth; Miller's recommendation is images of smiling, satisfied people, and of products in the hands of smiling people, with the caveat that not everybody need be smiling or it will not seem authentic. **A menu of products under one umbrella problem** (PDF pp. 181–182). The restaurant-without-a-menu analogy; a service business is told to package its services and name them, with a one-year maintenance package for an HVAC firm as the example; a client with a life-planning product, a strategy-planning product and a facilitator certification was unified under one umbrella line about a customized plan, with separate pages and separate BrandScripts beneath it and a certification button at the top and bottom of every page. **Very few words** (PDF pp. 182–183). The office phrase is to write it in Morse code; a paragraph above the fold is passed over; some of the most effective sites Miller has reviewed use ten sentences or fewer on the whole page; longer text goes behind a read-more link; and the exercise offered is to cut half the words. The chapter closes on staying on script — nothing on the site that does not come from a BrandScript category, though not necessarily in the BrandScript's exact words (PDF p. 183).

### Chapter 15: How to Execute a StoryBrand Messaging and Marketing Campaign (PDF pp. 185–216)

The longest chapter, and a checklist rather than an argument. Miller says nearly a thousand coaches have been certified in ten years and that this chapter is a summation of what they report works (PDF p. 185). The organizing model is three stages of a relationship, curiosity, enlightenment and commitment, each with its own collateral: sound bites and ads for curiosity, downloadable PDFs, webinars, email sequences and videos for enlightenment, and sales emails, countdown timers and special offers for commitment (PDF pp. 186–187). Seven steps follow.

**One, generate a BrandScript** (PDF pp. 187–188), with an audiobook and a radio-theater production offered as alternative routes. **Two, create a one-liner** (PDF pp. 188–192). It is modeled on a screenwriter's logline, and four loglines are quoted from *Rushmore*, *Pirates of the Caribbean*, *Star Wars* and *Tommy Boy*; what makes them work is clarity and promise. The one-liner has three parts, problem, product as solution, and result, and Miller notes in the same passage that it should communicate four ideas: who the customer is, their problem, the plan, and what life looks like afterwards. Length is deliberately loose — not one sentence, not four. The toothpaste pair shows the problem-first version beating the feature-first one; three "if you" openers are given as problem templates; and the solution half is to be kept very short, because rambling about features dilutes the product. The result half casts a vision, with regenerative ranching as the worked example. **Three, wireframe a landing page** (PDF pp. 192–198). Eight named sections in order: the header, carrying a large-text offer, a transitional call to action, a direct call to action and an optional value stack; the stakes, placed near the top because the problem is the hook; the value proposition, swinging back to positive because audiences like a story that alternates; the guide, introduced only once the right to be heard has been earned; the plan, three steps against cognitive dissonance; the explanatory paragraph, for due diligence; a lead generator advertisement, repeated in the body as well as a pop-up because mentioning it twice raises sign-ups; and the junk drawer at the foot of the page, where everything previously thought important goes. Each section in the text points at a figure the extraction did not carry. **Four, create a lead generator** (PDF pp. 198–201). It must provide enormous value and establish authority. Miller's own first one, a downloadable PDF about websites, was downloaded more than forty thousand times and is credited with taking the company past two million dollars, after which a free video series took lead generation further. Five types are listed: a downloadable guide, an online course or webinar, a software demo or free trial, free samples, and live events. Four titles are given as swipe material, from a financial adviser, an architect, a garden store's cocktail club, and a speaking coach.

**Five, create an automated email or text drip campaign** (PDF pp. 201–209). The origin story is a warehouse job at a publisher where Miller used FileMaker Pro to automate monthly thank-you letters to the top two hundred customers; his conclusion is that the letters worked even unread, because the logo crossed the customer's eyes, and that there is great power in simply reminding customers you exist. Hank's Belts is the text-message version: near-daily messages for months that never bothered him, and an order when he eventually needed a belt. A boxed aside says a 20 percent open rate is the industry standard, that anything above it is performing well, that a deleted email still accomplishes the goal, and that an unsubscribe is a good thing because it shrinks the bill and removes a person who was never going to buy. The recommended shape is a nurture campaign: three nurture emails then a sales email, repeated monthly, some brands never stopping. A nurture email's four paragraphs are a problem, a plan or idea that solves it, what life looks like solved, and the advice restated as three steps, plus a postscript, which Miller says is often the only part read. A sales email's four paragraphs are the problem, the product, life solved, and an assurance plus the price, any discount and the ask. Two full example emails for a dog-boarding client are printed, one nurture and one offer, and the offer email's call to action is noted as carrying scarcity because it is a onetime offer. On software, Miller names one CRM as a favorite, describes it as built for businesses with between one and twenty-five thousand subscribers, and discloses in the same paragraph that the company consulted with StoryBrand on its AI tools. **Six, collect and tell stories of transformation** (PDF pp. 209–211). A testimonial asked for plainly comes back as praise rather than a result, because customers are busy or are not writers; five leading questions are given instead, running from the problem before, through the frustration, the difference, the moment it worked, and life now, and the same questions serve for video. **Seven, create a referral system** (PDF pp. 211–215). Miller cites studies by the American Marketing Association that referrals and peer recommendations are up to 2.5 times more responsive than any other channel. The steps are to identify existing ideal customers, offer them a bonus or a coupon code, give them a tool such as a video or PDF rather than asking for their friends' addresses — an experience Miller says made him immediately uncomfortable when a consulting firm asked it of him — and optionally offer a reward or a 10 percent affiliate commission. Four real referral systems close the step: a test-prep academy's full refund for three referrals, a golf range's invite-a-friend buckets with a reported 40 percent rise in lesson sign-ups, a contractor's open-house parties that filled twelve months of schedule, and a wedding photographer's free anniversary portrait for three referrals. The chapter ends on a chess analogy: the seven pieces are the opening moves (PDF p. 215).

### Afterword, back matter and Notes (PDF pp. 217–231)

The afterword says tens of thousands of clients have been helped in the last decade and encourages the reader to share the radio-theater production (PDF p. 217). Back matter promotes the AI tool, the coach certification program, an online course, a private workshop, a sales training, and the coach directory, and states that StoryBrand takes no percentage of a certified coach's sales (PDF pp. 218–219, 225–229). The acknowledgments name the StoryBrand team, the first edition's editor, three HarperCollins editors, and a person thanked for help "fleshing out commercial evidence for the framework" (PDF p. 220). ## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** The book is about seven plot points, not a screenwriting method: a full screenplay would need more, and seven are what entering a customer's story requires (ch. 2, PDF p. 33). It is not a book about telling the company's story (Introduction, PDF p. 16).
- **The formula is not the whole answer.** Miller allows that brilliant screenwriters and brilliant marketers use the formula while avoiding cliché, and that when the framework is used well hardly anybody notices it is being used (ch. 2, PDF p. 37). He also says some brands appear to break the formulas and succeed, and then withdraws most of the concession: on close inspection this is "rarely the case" (ch. 2, PDF p. 37).
- **Not every story fits.** Miller says the formula holds in almost every story but not in all, and that a writer may bring in multiple guides or what he calls a "scattered climactic scene" (ch. 2, PDF p. 35).
- **Multiple desires are allowed, later.** The single-focus rule applies to the overall brand's BrandScript; more desires belong in the subplots, meaning a BrandScript per division, per product, and optionally per customer segment (ch. 4, PDF pp. 62–63; ch. 3, PDF p. 52).
- **Survival is not a Darwinian claim.** Miller stops to say none of the seven desires is evil, that they can all be taken too far, and that most people are "not nearly as Darwinian" as we have been led to believe, because we are interested in the survival of others too (ch. 4, PDF p. 66).
- **Villains have limits.** A villain must be real, and the book tells the reader never to go down the path of being a fearmonger; and in the same list it says a villain does not have to be serious, with a sports rivalry as the example (ch. 5, PDF pp. 75–76).
- **Drama has a truth condition.** Do not be afraid of a little drama, Miller writes, "especially if that drama is true" (ch. 5, PDF p. 75), and he concedes directly that brands must be careful to refrain from manipulating people with half-truths and exaggerations even while learning from the entertainment industry (ch. 5, PDF p. 72).
- **The three levels are a refinement, not a starting point.** The reader is told not to overthink the success module, to start with a sound bite that screams resolution, and to sharpen into external, internal and philosophical language later (ch. 10, PDF p. 150).
- **A philosophical problem is conditional.** The brand promise is to resolve an external problem, an internal one, and a philosophical one "if possible" (ch. 5, PDF p. 83).
- **The guide may talk about itself.** Playing the guide does not mean going quiet and humble; a guide can talk about itself all day long, provided it tells only the parts that support its care and competency, and Miller notes Mary Poppins gets more screen time than the hero (ch. 6, PDF p. 98).
- **Competency is not perfection, and authority was the wrong word.** As a guide "you do not have to be perfect", and arrogance never serves anybody and is a sign of defensiveness and weakness; the first edition's word, authority, is withdrawn in this edition (ch. 6, PDF pp. 94, 97).
- **Testimonials have a ceiling.** Three is a great number to start with, and Miller says to avoid rambling testimonials that heap endless praise, because a long one will not be read (ch. 6, PDF p. 99). Awards are "hardly necessary" (ch. 6, PDF p. 99).
- **The plan's length is left unresolved.** The stated range is at least three steps and no more than six, with phases beyond that; in the next paragraph the text says more than four steps may add to rather than reduce confusion (ch. 7, PDF p. 109).
- **An agreement plan need not be published.** Unlike a process plan, it often works in the background and does not have to be on the home page, "though it could be" (ch. 7, PDF pp. 110–111).
- **The dose of stakes is rationed.** Fear is salt in the recipe; too many warnings about imminent doom risk turning customers off, and the book block-quotes its own source saying high fear is blocked out and only moderate amounts change behavior (ch. 9, PDF pp. 133–134). Miller immediately adds that in his view almost nobody reading the book will overuse it (ch. 9, PDF p. 134), and elsewhere answers his own question about whether there can be too many stakes with "Almost never" (ch. 9, PDF pp. 131–132).
- **Fearmongering is conceded as real.** Miller writes that fearmongers "can give customers the cringe", and then says it is not the problem 99.9 percent of business leaders struggle with (ch. 9, PDF p. 131).
- **Not everybody smiling.** The recommendation of images of smiling people is limited in the next sentence: not everybody needs to be smiling, because that would not seem authentic (ch. 14, PDF p. 180).
- **The wireframe is adjustable.** The reader may add to or subtract from the eight-section landing page (ch. 15, PDF p. 198).
- **Some product menus really do need splitting.** Miller says most diverse offerings can find an umbrella theme, and allows that "certainly there are examples" where brands within an umbrella company need to be split up and marketed separately (ch. 14, PDF p. 182).
- **The site's words need not match the BrandScript's.** The words used in marketing do not have to be the exact text on the BrandScript, but the ideas should be the same (ch. 14, PDF p. 183).
- **The one-liner's shape is loose.** It does not have to be a single sentence and does not need to be four; think of it as a statement (ch. 15, PDF p. 190). Its three parts are also described, in the same passage, as four ideas to communicate (ch. 15, PDF p. 190).
- **A one-liner is not meant to inform.** Enlightening the customer is explicitly not the one-liner's job; a longer, more interesting explanation is fine only once the customer is ready to be enlightened (ch. 15, PDF p. 191).
- **The controlling idea's form bends.** It "needs to be a complete idea", and Miller says he will let an abbreviated three-word version slide once the full thought has been explained enough times (ch. 12, PDF p. 165).
- **Everything is a rough draft.** The BrandScript is a rough draft and stays a work in progress until it succeeds; the one-liner is a rough draft to be tested on friends, spouse, customers and strangers (ch. 13, PDF p. 172; ch. 15, PDF p. 192).
- **Execution is the limit.** Miller closes the back matter with the admission that nothing will change unless the reader executes (PDF p. 229), and the chapter 15 checklist opens by saying the campaign need not be created all at once (ch. 15, PDF p. 186).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, or company the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure; where the reason is this session's own search budget rather than the absence of a source, the entry says so.

- **The CRM the book names as its software recommendation, and discloses consulted with StoryBrand on its AI tools (ch. 15, PDF p. 209).** The company was acquired: Thryv Holdings closed its acquisition of Keap on October 31, 2024, for $80 million in cash subject to adjustment, reporting a combined company with over 100,000 SaaS subscribers (thryv.com/news, "Thryv Holdings Announces Closing of Strategic Acquisition of Keap"). That closing precedes the December 2024 Epub edition date on this book's own copyright page. The same company supplies the statistics-as-competency example in ch. 6, PDF p. 99, and the endnote for that example cites infusionsoft.com, accessed February 2017 (Notes, PDF p. 231), the pre-2019 name.
- **Tidal: Jay-Z's $56 million personal investment, and the claim that he "sold his majority interest" in Tidal for $350 million (ch. 6, PDF pp. 90, 92).** The purchase figure holds and the sale figure does not. Project Panther Bidco bought Aspiro in January 2015 for SEK 466 million, about $56.2 million. Square, now Block, agreed in March 2021 to acquire the majority stake and paid $297 million in cash and stock, holding 86.8% as of June 2022, with Jay-Z keeping a board seat (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_(service)). The book's figure is about $53 million higher than the reported one.
- **"Top Gun: Maverick" at $1.46 billion and the highest-grossing movie of Tom Cruise's career (ch. 2, PDF p. 35).** The claim about Cruise's career holds; the number is now higher. The worldwide gross is $1.503 billion, $722 million domestic and $781 million international, and the film became Cruise's highest-grossing on June 17, 2022 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top_Gun:_Maverick).
- **A 20 percent open rate is called "the industry standard" (ch. 15, PDF p. 204).** Both the number and the metric have moved. Mailchimp's own benchmarks page puts the all-industry average open rate at 35.63% and carries a disclaimer that "The accuracy of email open rates may be impacted by Apple's privacy changes and their Mail Privacy Protection (MPP) feature, and this should be considered as you interpret open rate data" (mailchimp.com/resources/email-marketing-benchmarks). MPP pre-loads tracking images, so a modern open rate is inflated by an unknown share; the book states the 20 percent figure with no source and no such caveat.
- **Prospect theory, attributed in the body to Daniel Kahneman in 1979, with "two to three times more motivated" to avoid a loss (ch. 9, PDF p. 132).** The paper is Kahneman **and Amos Tversky**, *Econometrica* 47, no. 2 (March 1979): 263–291, and the book's own endnote names both authors while the body text names only Kahneman (Notes, PDF p. 231; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospect_theory). The "two to three times" range is unchecked: the article gives only an illustrative 2:1 example and does not establish a standard coefficient, and no figure in that range could be tied to Kahneman's own words within this session's search budget.
- **StoryBrand's own offer, including the claim that the BrandScript is "completely free" as a bonus for buying the book (ch. 3, PDF p. 51).** StoryBrand's own site today leads with a line about clarifying your message so customers engage, and lists a two-day Nashville workshop from $1,999 per person, a four-week messaging intervention from $25,000, and a fractional CMO engagement with Miller from $100,000, plus an on-demand course and a guide certification program (storybrand.com). No free BrandScript tool and no StoryBrand Brain appear on that page. **Unchecked:** storybrand.ai, the URL the book directs the reader to nine separate times, returns a page whose content is rendered by script and could not be read by fetch, so whether the Brain is live and free is not established here.
- **Squarespace's header, quoted as a model of a short, relevant offer (ch. 14, PDF p. 177).** Not current. The headline above the fold on squarespace.com today is "A website makes it real", with a Get Started button and the line "Start for free. No credit card required." The wording the book quotes is not on the page.
- **Gardenary and StartAGarden.com: Nicole Burke was told nobody wakes up wanting to make gardening ordinary, took the domain, and the business is "poised to quadruple" (ch. 2, PDF pp. 39–40).** The company is live and sells courses, raised beds, tools, consulting and a coach academy, and its own site leads with the founder's mission — describing Gardenary as a global movement to make organic kitchen gardens beautiful and productive (gardenary.com). The name on the page is Gardenary, not the domain Miller recommended, and startagarden.com could not be reached. The quadrupling claim is unchecked; it is Miller's own forecast with no outside source.
- **TOMS Shoes, the one-for-one model, and a sale for more than $700 million (ch. 10, PDF p. 149).** The model has changed. TOMS's own about page describes giving "a portion of our profits" to children's education, health and well-being through cash grants, product donations and partnerships, and states that since 2006 it has given more than $200 million in shoe donations and monetary grants; it does not describe one-for-one (toms.com/us/about-toms.html). The $700 million figure is unchecked.
- **Dave Ramsey's radio show, "more than eight million daily listeners" (ch. 11, PDF p. 158).** The show is now called The Ramsey Show, and Ramsey Solutions' own page states it "reaches over 18 million combined weekly listeners" (ramseysolutions.com/shows/the-ramsey-show). The two figures use different denominators, daily against combined weekly, so they cannot be compared directly and the book's number is neither confirmed nor refuted.
- **Gerber's *Hello Trouble* campaign, quoted at length as the model of an aspirational identity (ch. 11, PDF pp. 155–156).** No longer running. Gerber Gear's home page today leads with a different line about trust not flinching, and *Hello Trouble* does not appear on it (gerbergear.com). The endnote for the campaign points at a Vimeo upload dated February 13, 2013 (Notes, PDF p. 231), so the campaign the 2025 edition quotes is twelve years old.
- **CarMax as "a $15 billion phenomenon" in one of the least-trusted industries, and selling more cars than its next three competitors combined (ch. 5, PDF p. 81; ch. 7, PDF p. 110).** The market position holds; the figure is dated. CarMax is still described as the nation's largest used-car retailer and operated 253 locations across 41 states as of January 2025 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CarMax). The $15 billion figure rests on the book's own endnote, a Forbes list dated May 2016, and the "next three competitors combined" claim on an *Automotive News* piece from December 2015 (Notes, PDF pp. 230, 231); neither was rechecked for this edition, and current revenue is unchecked.
- **Steve Jobs, the Lisa, and Think Different as "the first campaign" Jobs released after returning from Pixar (ch. 2, PDF p. 31).** Both halves are wrong; see section 8. Jobs was forced out of the Lisa project in 1980 and went on to lead the Macintosh (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Lisa). Think Different launched in 1997, created by TBWA\Chiat\Day's Los Angeles office, and it was not the first campaign of Jobs's return: BBDO had already pitched a "We're back" campaign under Gil Amelio, which Jobs rejected before inviting three agencies to present (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Think_different).
- **The freshness of the 2.0 edition's own sources.** Of the twenty-five endnotes, several are dated to the first edition's research: the Apple market-rank note is a Forbes piece from May 2016, the Hitchcock quotation is sourced to IMDB, the two CarMax notes are "accessed February 10, 2017", the statistics-example note is "accessed February 9, 2017", and the Clinton debate note points at a 2007 YouTube upload (Notes, PDF pp. 230–231). Two notes were refreshed for this edition and carry an access date of July 29, 2024. So the updated edition doubled its examples without rechecking most of the citations behind the ones it kept.
- **Not checked:** every client result in the book, because each is the author's or a client's own account with no outside source available — Kyle Shultz's $25,000 then $103,000 (ch. 2, PDF p. 39), Spectrum Brands' 99 percent sales increase on three words (ch. 1, PDF p. 23), the Calix training closing more than $2.5 million in one hour (ch. 8, PDF p. 116), Vitality Aesthetics (ch. 5, PDF pp. 79–80), MaxStrength Fitness (ch. 12, PDF p. 164), the gift retailers' numbered cards (ch. 4, PDF p. 60), the mattress brand (ch. 7, PDF p. 106), the clinic checklist (ch. 8, PDF pp. 123–124), Nēmah (ch. 6, PDF pp. 98–99), the golf range's 40 percent rise and the other three referral systems (ch. 15, PDF pp. 214–215), and the eight testimonials in the back matter (PDF pp. 222–224). Also unchecked: "more than one million copies" sold and the estimate of billions in increased revenue (Note from the Author, PDF p. 14); more than ten thousand businesses helped a year (Introduction, PDF p. 16); nearly a million BrandScript users (Note from the Author, PDF p. 14); nearly a thousand certified coaches (ch. 15, PDF p. 185); the claim that people spend more than 30 percent of their time daydreaming, attributed to unnamed neuroscientists (ch. 2, PDF p. 29); "more than three thousand commercial messages per day" (ch. 8, PDF p. 115); the belief that people must hear a message about eight times (ch. 13, PDF p. 170); the American Marketing Association studies putting referrals at up to 2.5 times more responsive than any other channel, which carries no endnote (ch. 15, PDF p. 211); and the Allstate Project Share Aware figures, whose endnote is an ABC News piece from January 2015 (ch. 9, PDF p. 130; Notes, PDF p. 231). This session's WebSearch budget was exhausted before these could be pursued individually, so several of them are unchecked for that reason rather than because a source is known not to exist.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Correlation presented as cause, throughout.** Every commercial result in the book is a single-arm before-and-after with no control and no other change accounted for. Kyle Shultz's second launch is credited to a rewritten sales page, and the passage itself supplies a confound it does not address — the same list had already been sold to six months earlier, so the two launches differ in more than their copy (ch. 2, PDF p. 39). Spectrum Brands' 99 percent increase is attributed to three words on packaging and signage in a test market with no comparison market named (ch. 1, PDF p. 23). The Calix training is credited with more than $2.5 million closed in one hour off emails that an account executive chose to send to a named list of customers already sitting on the fence (ch. 8, PDF p. 116). Nicole Burke's business is "poised to quadruple", and the sentence naming that forecast also names two alternative causes — a new product and a television show — and dismisses both in favor of clarity, without evidence for the choice (ch. 2, PDF pp. 39–40). The mattress representatives, the clinic checklist, the gift retailers' numbered cards and the golf range's 40 percent all have the same shape (ch. 7, PDF p. 106; ch. 8, PDF pp. 123–124; ch. 4, PDF p. 60; ch. 15, PDF p. 214).
- **Dated facts about the book's own flagship example.** The Apple passage places the Lisa as "the last project" Jobs worked on before he was let go, and Think Different as the first campaign he released after returning from Pixar (ch. 2, PDF p. 31). Section 7 has the later sources: Jobs was forced off Lisa in 1980 and went on to lead the Macintosh, and Think Different was preceded by a rejected BBDO pitch after his return. The endnote attached to the surrounding paragraph is a Forbes ranking from May 2016 (Notes, PDF p. 230), which supports the size claim and none of the chronology.
- **Anecdotes about real people with no citation.** The Lyndon Johnson and George Wallace conversation, in which Johnson is said to have told Wallace his legacy hung in the balance during negotiations over the Civil Rights Act of 1964, carries no endnote at all (ch. 9, PDF p. 132), and this read could not verify it against an outside source. The Clinton paragraph compresses the 1992 catchphrase and a town-hall exchange about the national debt into one passage, and the endnote covers only a YouTube upload of the debate, not the line (ch. 6, PDF pp. 95–96; Notes, PDF p. 231). The Pascal letter is hedged as "often credited" rather than sourced (ch. 1, PDF p. 28).
- **Editorial errors the update did not catch.** The client in the Vitality Aesthetics story is introduced as Stephen Boice and then called David four times in the next two paragraphs (ch. 5, PDF pp. 79–80). The villain section announces "four characteristics that make for a good villain" and prints a numbered list of five (ch. 5, PDF pp. 75–76). The chapter 15 step-by-step plan carries a stray letter, "Campaign l", between the noun and the clause that follows it (ch. 15, PDF p. 186). None of these is load-bearing, and all of them are in a chapter the edition claims to have reworked.
- **Rules that contradict themselves within a page.** The process plan is "at least three and no more than six" steps and then, in the next paragraph, more than four steps "may actually add to" confusion instead of reducing it (ch. 7, PDF p. 109). Miller answers his own question about whether there can be too many stakes with "Almost never" (ch. 9, PDF pp. 131–132), three pages before the book block-quotes its own source saying high fear is blocked out and only moderate amounts change behavior (ch. 9, PDF p. 134). The reader is told never to be a fearmonger (ch. 5, PDF p. 76) in a framework whose failure module is operationalized as a four-step "fear appeal" (ch. 9, PDF pp. 132–133). And the guide who must never be the hero "can talk about yourself all day long" (ch. 6, PDF p. 98), which is the behavior the rest of that chapter is written against.
- **The mechanism rests on one conversation.** The calorie-and-survival argument that carries the whole book is sourced to a back-porch conversation with one friend, Mike McHargue, described as having spent fifteen years using science-based methodologies in advertising (ch. 1, PDF pp. 20–22). No study is cited for it anywhere in the book. The related claim that people spend more than 30 percent of their time daydreaming is attributed to unnamed "neuroscientists" with no note (ch. 2, PDF p. 29), and the 3,000-commercial-messages figure has none either (ch. 8, PDF p. 115).
- **Aesthetic judgment presented as evidence.** The formula's universality is argued from the claim that indie films "fail miserably at the box office" because they break narrative formulas, and that critics who like them "are snobs who watch everything" (ch. 2, PDF p. 36). No box-office data is offered, and the sentence explains away the disconfirming case rather than testing it. The same move appears when Miller allows that some brands break the formula and succeed and then says that on close inspection this is "rarely the case" (ch. 2, PDF p. 37).
- **A claim about human psychology stated as audience fact.** The union resolution is explained as the joining of male and female characteristics, in which, in Miller's words, the man "needs to be tamed" and the woman needs "provision and protection" in order to become whole, offered as what an audience subconsciously considers and defended only as a formula that works for most moviegoing audiences, "Traditional though it may seem" (ch. 10, PDF p. 146). No source supports the psychological claim, and the evidence offered is box-office popularity.
- **A sample of successes, most of them customers.** The named failure in the book is Tidal, and section 7 records that it sold for a reported $297 million. Every other extended example is a success, and a large share are StoryBrand's own clients, mastermind members, workshop attendees, or certified coaches' clients (ch. 2, PDF p. 39; ch. 5, PDF p. 79; ch. 11, PDF p. 158; ch. 12, PDF p. 164; ch. 15, PDF p. 210). No client who applied the framework and did not grow appears anywhere, and the acknowledgments thank a person for help "fleshing out commercial evidence for the framework" (PDF p. 220), which describes evidence assembled to support a thesis rather than evidence the thesis was derived from.
- **Commercial interest running through the argument.** The book directs the reader to StoryBrand.AI repeatedly and to the coach directory at MarketingMadeSimple.com repeatedly (ch. 3, PDF p. 51; ch. 14, PDF pp. 175, 184; ch. 15, PDF pp. 186, 201, 216; PDF pp. 218–219, 229), and the back matter sells a workshop, a certification, a course and a sales training (PDF pp. 226–229). The one CRM recommended by name is disclosed in the same paragraph as having consulted with StoryBrand on its AI tools (ch. 15, PDF p. 209). Two people whose endorsements open the book, Michael Hyatt and Ken Blanchard, reappear inside it as brand examples — the Full Focus Planner as a conserving-time brand (ch. 4, PDF p. 65) and The Ken Blanchard Company among the inspiration brands (ch. 10, PDF p. 149), with Blanchard's Situational Leadership named as a model formula (ch. 2, PDF p. 25). None of these is disclosed at the point of use.
- **The update does not update the evidence.** The stated change in 2.0 is nearly double the examples and an AI tool (Note from the Author, PDF p. 14). Section 7 shows the citations behind the retained examples were largely not rechecked: most endnotes still carry 2016 and 2017 access dates, the Gerber campaign quoted at length is from 2013, the Squarespace header quoted as a model is not the one on the page, and the recommended CRM had been acquired before this edition's Epub date. Six chapters, including the three execution chapters, carry no endnote at all, and those are where the unsourced figures sit.
- **Deferred content.** The eight landing-page sections in ch. 15 are each described in a sentence and then shown in a figure (ch. 15, PDF pp. 192–198), and the book's own advice is to have the AI tool generate the page (ch. 15, PDF p. 198). A reader of the text alone gets the names of the sections and their order, and not the copy that fills them.
