## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the authors' frame, is that innovation fails so often because companies ask the wrong question, and that there is a better one: what job did the customer hire this product to do (Introduction, PDF p. 12). A job is defined as the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance, and customers do not buy products so much as pull them into their lives to make that progress; the book's metaphor is that they "hire" a product and, when it does the job badly, "fire" it (ch. 2, PDF p. 36). The claim is causal: correlation, which is what most customer data records, never says why a person chose one thing over another, and only a theory of what causes what can make innovation predictable rather than a matter of luck (Introduction, PDF pp. 11–13; ch. 2, PDF pp. 32–36). The subtitle of the argument is in the title: a company that understands the job is competing against luck while its rivals are still relying on it (Introduction, PDF p. 16). The evidence offered is two decades of case work — the milk shake study, Unilever's margarine, Southern New Hampshire University, Intuit, American Girl, IKEA, Medtronic, OnStar, Deseret News — gathered, the Acknowledgments say, person by person and company by company, because the authors had no dataset of the kind that produced the theory of disruption (Acknowledgments, PDF p. 206).

The authors say what the claim is not. It is not the theory of disruptive innovation, which they say explains competitive response and does not tell a company where to look for opportunity or what to build (ch. 1, PDF pp. 18–19). It is not a new word for a need: needs are generic and ever-present, directionally useful and insufficient to predict a choice, and the guiding themes of a person's life — being a good father, being a valued member of a congregation — are not jobs either (ch. 2, PDF pp. 39–40). It is not a segmentation of customers by their characteristics: the theory is not about who did something or what they did, but why (ch. 2, PDF p. 41). It is not a claim to universality — the book states where the theory does not apply (ch. 2, PDF p. 48; ch. 10, PDF pp. 198–200), and section 5 below collects those limits. It is not a set of tools to replace existing research: personas, ethnography, focus groups, and competitive analysis all stay, used with a different lens (ch. 4, PDF p. 74). It is not the deductive kind of theory, and the authors defend that: the construct was built inductively over two decades, and the words "hire" and "fire" are named as construct terms rather than ornament (ch. 10, PDF pp. 195–197). And it is not a claim to own the field: the authors note other voices in the jobs space, say they may use different words and methods, and offer the book as a common language rather than a verdict (ch. 1, PDF p. 27).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

The book is written in the first person singular, in Christensen's voice, by agreement among the four authors for readability; they say it is a collaborative work throughout (Introduction, PDF p. 14). "I" below is that narrator.

### Introduction: Why You Should Hire This Book (PDF pp. 9–16)

The opening claim is that this is a book about progress before it is a book about innovation (Introduction, PDF p. 9). The problem is stated with figures: a McKinsey poll in which 84 percent of global executives called innovation extremely important to growth and 94 percent were unsatisfied with their own performance, and a *strategy+business* article reporting that in 2015 a thousand publicly held companies spent $680 billion on research and development, 5.1 percent more than the year before (Introduction, PDF p. 10). Companies have more data than ever and are no better at innovating, because the data is organized by resemblance rather than by cause: this customer looks like that one, this product performs like that one (Introduction, PDF p. 11). The author's own demographic profile — his age, his height, his shoe size, his minivan — is offered as the example of attributes that correlate with buying a newspaper and cause nobody to buy one (Introduction, PDF p. 11). Nate Silver's ice cream and forest fires are borrowed to separate correlation from cause, and Chris Anderson's 2008 *Wired* declaration that correlation is enough is named as the belief under attack (Introduction, PDF pp. 10–11). Deming supplies the framing: a person who does not know how to ask the right question discovers nothing (Introduction, PDF p. 12). The better question is what job the product was hired to do (Introduction, PDF p. 12). Airbnb and Khan Academy are the opening examples of solutions that were, by the standards of their categories, worse and were hired anyway; Khan's videos are described by their maker as cheaper and crappier than the alternatives already online (Introduction, PDF p. 13). Five endnotes carry the two sources, two anecdotes — the author's son's pitching record as data that can never say why, and Todd Rose's account of the US Air Force finding that no pilot fit the average and inventing the adjustable seat — and the note that Jobs Theory and the Theory of Jobs to Be Done are one term (Introduction endnotes, PDF p. 16).

### Chapter 1: The Milk Shake Dilemma (PDF pp. 17–30)

The chapter opens on the puzzle that produced the theory of disruption — Digital Equipment Corporation's collapse around 1988, and the observation that nearly every minicomputer company failed at once, which the stupid-manager hypothesis cannot explain (ch. 1, PDF pp. 17–18). Disruption explains competitive response and does not say where to look for opportunity, does not predict which new products will succeed, and does not offer a road map; the author puts the odds of an intentional disruptor building exactly the right product at under 25 percent (ch. 1, PDF p. 19).

The milk shake story follows. A fast-food chain studied its milk shake for months, brought in customers matching the profile, asked them how to make it cheaper, chunkier, chewier, chocolatier, shipped the answers, and saw no change in sales (ch. 1, PDF pp. 19–20). Bob Moesta's team then stood in a restaurant for eighteen hours watching who bought and when, and found a surprising number sold before 9:00 a.m., alone, as the only purchase, taken away in a car (ch. 1, PDF p. 20). The job was a long, boring commute plus a hunger that would arrive mid-morning, and the competing hires were bananas, doughnuts, bagels, Snickers bars, breakfast bars, smoothies, and coffee, each failing for a stated reason: the banana is gone too fast, the doughnut leaves sticky fingers on the wheel, the bagel is dry and has to be spread while driving with the knees (ch. 1, PDF pp. 20–21). The thick shake and the thin straw win because they take twenty minutes and fit the cup holder (ch. 1, PDF p. 21). A second job exists in the same product at a different hour: the father who has said no all week and wants something harmless to say yes to, whose milk shake competes with a stop at the toy store or a game of catch (ch. 1, PDF pp. 21–22). The two jobs want opposite products — thicker for the commute, a half size for the afternoon — so averaging the survey answers yields what the book calls a one-size-fits-none product (ch. 1, PDF pp. 22–23).

The margarine story is the counter-case. Unilever held something like 70 percent of the US yellow-fats market; the author and his daughter stood in a supermarket counting twenty-one brands differentiated on fat percentage, a dimension irrelevant to any job they could name, and concluded that the competitive set included butter, cream cheese, olive oil, mayonnaise, Teflon, and cooking spray, products sitting in two other aisles (ch. 1, PDF pp. 23–24). No customer, the argument runs, walks in asking for something from the yellow-fats category (ch. 1, PDF p. 25). The presentation to Unilever's executives failed; the author called an early break and changed the subject, and reads their response as the sign of an organization whose assumptions made the question unaskable (ch. 1, PDF p. 25). The later history is offered as consequence rather than proof: butter passed margarine in American households by the mid-2000s, an analyst asked in 2013 whether Unilever should dispose of spreads, the division was separated in 2014, and the head of the margarine group was replaced in early 2016 (ch. 1, PDF pp. 25–26). The chapter closes on vocabulary — the field borrows tools built for other purposes — and on Uber as a company that identified and filled the job of urban transportation (ch. 1, PDF pp. 26–27).

### Chapter 2: Progress, Not Products (PDF pp. 31–52)

The chapter argues by analogy from the history of science. Humorism and bloodletting lasted almost two thousand years; miasma theory improved on it by accident; John Snow's Broad Street pump was correlation and saved lives without reaching the cause; Pasteur's germ theory supplied the causal mechanism and produced vaccines, antibiotics, and pasteurization (ch. 2, PDF pp. 31–33). The second analogy is the quality movement: Detroit's lemons were accepted as inherent randomness in a process assembling some thirty thousand parts, about two thousand of them unique, from several hundred suppliers in as many as seventeen countries, until Japanese manufacturers treated each defect as an anomaly with a specific cause and designed defects out (ch. 2, PDF pp. 33–34). Innovation, the author says, sits in a pre-quality-revolution state (ch. 2, PDF p. 35). Good theory teaches how to think rather than what to think, has a voice and no agenda, and dispenses its advice in if-then statements (ch. 2, PDF pp. 35, 47).

The definition follows, in three parts. **Progress**: a job is the progress a person is trying to make, movement toward a goal, a process rather than a discrete event, and not necessarily a problem (ch. 2, PDF p. 36). **Circumstance**: a job can only be defined relative to the context it arises in — where, when, with whom, while doing what, half an hour ago, next, and under what social, cultural, or political pressure, extending to life stage, family status, and financial status (ch. 2, PDF p. 37). Managers instead organize around product attributes, customer characteristics, trends, or competitive response, none of which the book calls wrong and all of which it calls insufficient (ch. 2, PDF p. 37). **Functional, social, and emotional complexity**: the example is childcare, where the functional dimensions matter and the question of who a parent will trust with a child probably matters more (ch. 2, PDF p. 38).

What is not a job: a need, which is generic and always true, and a guiding principle of a life, which is too abstract to trigger a purchase (ch. 2, PDF pp. 39–40). The Segway is the example of an invention built around a general need for efficient personal transportation with no circumstance attached (ch. 2, PDF pp. 39–40). The chapter then offers the minidocumentary exercise, five questions to capture: what progress, in what circumstances, against what obstacles, with what compensating behavior, and against what definition of quality and what tradeoffs (ch. 2, PDF pp. 41–42). Airbnb is read through it: the founder's own circumstance was wanting to attend a design conference he could not afford, and the service competes with hotels, with staying with friends, and with not making the trip (ch. 2, PDF pp. 43–44). Jobs are discovered rather than created, and they endure while solutions change: the job of sending information a long distance ran from the Pony Express through the telegraph to email, and teenagers wanting to talk past their parents moved from notes in the hallway to Snapchat (ch. 2, PDF p. 44). Competition is therefore wider than the category: cigarettes and Facebook can be hired for the same break, and Netflix's Reed Hastings is quoted saying the company competes with everything a person does to relax, video games and a bottle of wine included (ch. 2, PDF p. 45). BMW is the worked example: a company that had described itself as selling high-performance cars decided in 2008 that the real job was mobility, which put it against Tesla, Uber, Zipcar, and Google's car project, and produced the BMWi line and the DriveNow sharing pilot in Berlin, Vienna, San Francisco, and London (ch. 2, PDF p. 46). The chapter states the theory's limits before it ends, and closes on Copernicus and Ptolemy's epicycles as the picture of an industry getting better and better at a flawed model (ch. 2, PDF pp. 48–50).

### Chapter 3: Jobs in the Wild (PDF pp. 53–68)

Southern New Hampshire University is the long case. In 2003 it was a seventy-year-old second-tier college of a few thousand students; its online arm was a side project described by its president as a sleepy operation on a corner of the campus (ch. 3, PDF pp. 54–56). Applying a jobs lens produced two answers rather than one. Traditional applicants, in the circumstance of leaving home for the first time, asked about sports teams, climbing walls, and conversations with faculty, and were hiring the coming-of-age experience; roughly three thousand of them enroll a year, against a fixed set of local rivals (ch. 3, PDF p. 55). Online students averaged thirty, were juggling work and family, often carried debt from an unfinished degree, and wanted four things: convenience, customer service, credentials, and speed (ch. 3, PDF p. 56). Their competitor set was national, and it included nonconsumption — doing nothing at all (ch. 3, PDF p. 56).

The operational consequences are given in detail, because the chapter's argument is that the job, not the insight, is what changes a business. The online team moved two miles away into a former mill yard; the whole admissions process was drawn on a whiteboard and the hurdles circled and removed one by one (ch. 3, PDF p. 58). The generic email twenty-four hours after an inquiry became a phone call inside ten minutes, on the reasoning that the first institution to speak to a prospective student usually closes; calls run an hour or more and exist to surface anxieties (ch. 3, PDF p. 59). Financial-aid and transfer-credit decisions moved from weeks to days (ch. 3, PDF p. 59). The advertising was rebuilt around the emotional and social dimensions, with a bus handing framed diplomas to online graduates at home (ch. 3, PDF pp. 59–60). Every online student gets a personal adviser who notices red flags before the student does, calls after a bad unit test, and may send a replacement laptop (ch. 3, PDF p. 60). The reported results: closing on $535 million in revenue by the end of fiscal 2016, a 34 percent compound annual growth rate over six years, twelve hundred staff in the mill yard, more than seventy-five thousand students in thirty-six states and countries, roughly 10 percent surpluses, no online tuition increase in four years, a $2,500-a-year competency-based program, and a mention by President Obama in 2013 (ch. 3, PDF pp. 54, 60–61).

Three shorter cases follow. Sargento's prepackaged ultrathin cheese slices cleared $50 million in year one and exceeded $150 million in year two, against a base rate in which most of the thirty-four hundred consumer packaged goods launched that year did not survive twelve months; Nielsen's Breakthrough Innovation Reports for 2012–2016 tracked over twenty thousand launches and found ninety-two that sold more than $50 million in year one and held sales in year two (ch. 3, PDF pp. 61–62). FranklinCovey is the business-to-business case: revenue had fallen from a peak of $589 million in 2000 to $130 million in 2009, and three years of customer visits by the CEO — nearly four hundred of them — surfaced that the buyers of training were hiring it to be seen as mission-critical by their own line leaders, which led the company to stop selling courses and start selling all-access passes and outcomes, with a full-time coach stationed at one client's headquarters; 2015 revenue reached $220 million at a 9.2 percent compound growth rate (ch. 3, PDF pp. 62–65). Intuit is the third: small businesses were jury-rigging Quicken because they did not want accounting software, and QuickBooks shipped half the functionality of the sophisticated products at twice the price and became the category leader, because its competitors were a shoebox of receipts, a spreadsheet, extra hours at the office, or hiring a bookkeeper (ch. 3, PDF pp. 65–66). The chapter's pricing claim sits here: a clear view of the job means never overshooting what a customer will pay for, and customers who find the right product for their job are often willing to pay more (ch. 3, PDF p. 66). It closes on the message a well-fitted product sends, which the book renders as the company telling the customer, in three words, that it understands her (ch. 3, PDF p. 67).

### Chapter 4: Job Hunting (PDF pp. 70–90)

The opening case is a Detroit-area builder of downsizer condominiums priced $120,000 to $200,000, with squeakless floors, triple waterproof basements, granite counters, a thirty-page customization checklist, a sales team six days a week, and heavy Sunday advertising, getting traffic and almost no sales (ch. 4, PDF pp. 70–71). Moesta asked buyers to draw a timeline of how they got there, and found no demographic or psychographic profile and no decisive feature; the customization list was itself an obstacle (ch. 4, PDF p. 71). The recurring clue was the dining room table — people said they were free to move once they knew what to do with it — which Moesta understood only sitting at his own table at Christmas: the table stood for the family's life, and the barrier was not a missing feature but the anxiety of giving up something meaningful (ch. 4, PDF p. 72). One interviewee needed days and several boxes of tissues to clear one closet (ch. 4, PDF p. 72). The changes that followed: the second bedroom shrank by 20 percent to make room for a classic dining table, the company added moving services, two years of storage, and a sorting room on the premises, and it replaced thirty pages of choices with three finished variants, which cut cold-feet cancellations from five or six a month to one (ch. 4, PDF pp. 72–73). The company raised prices by $3,500, profitably, covering moving and storage, and grew 25 percent in 2007 while industry sales fell 49 percent (ch. 4, PDF p. 73).

The chapter then offers five places to hunt, with the caveat that it is not attempting to be comprehensive and does not believe there is one right way (ch. 4, PDF p. 73). **1. A job close to home.** Akio Morita advised against market research and shipped the Walkman over his marketing department's warning that nobody would buy a player that could not record; it sold over 330 million units. Sal Khan's videos began as help for one cousin. Sheila Marcelo started Care.com out of her own childcare struggle; the book reports nearly 10 million members across sixteen countries and revenue approaching $60 million (ch. 4, PDF pp. 75–76). **2. Competing with nothing.** Airbnb's Chip Conley says 40 percent of guests would not have made the trip or would have stayed with family; virtually no host would have rented a room otherwise (ch. 4, PDF p. 76). Kimberly-Clark's Depend line was successful and still left people who would rather use nothing: nearly 40 percent of adults over fifty have incontinence, one in three women over eighteen has some form of it, only a small share use any product, and people typically suffer two years before buying one, meanwhile using feminine hygiene pads as a stop-gap or simply withdrawing from travel, restaurants, and friends (ch. 4, PDF p. 77). The job was reclaiming a life, which required new materials so the product did not look or feel like a diaper and packaging with a transparent window so a buyer could see that; Depend Silhouette and Real Fit made $60 million in year one with 30 percent growth in year two and no harm to the existing line (ch. 4, PDF pp. 77–78). **3. Workarounds and compensating behaviors.** A friend's symbolic Bank of Daddy, crediting his children's allowance and paying a rate the real bank would not, is the example; ING Direct took away minimum balances and fees, ran online with no branch overhead and no wealth-management specialists, became the fastest-growing bank in the United States, and sold to Capital One in 2012 for $9 billion (ch. 4, PDF pp. 78–80). OpenTable is offered as a workaround solved (ch. 4, PDF p. 80). **4. What people do not want to do.** The book names these negative jobs and calls them often the best opportunities; the worked example is a parent who is sure a child has strep and whose job is not seeing the doctor, and the business is QuickMedx, forerunner of CVS MinuteClinic, which the book puts at more than a thousand locations in thirty-three states (ch. 4, PDF pp. 80–81). **5. Unusual uses.** Church & Dwight watched people put Arm & Hammer baking soda in laundry, toothpaste, carpets, and refrigerators, and built a product line on each job; the orange box is now under 7 percent of the brand's consumer revenue. NyQuil taken by people who were not sick produced ZzzQuil (ch. 4, PDF pp. 81–82).

The chapter closes on what it calls the emotional score. Todd Dunn, an innovation director at Intermountain Healthcare, sat as a patient on the crinkly examination paper and recognized that a material designed for a functional purpose was manufacturing anxiety; his surgeon then sketched the diagnosis on paper rather than use the state-of-the-art software, because turning to the screen would break eye contact with an anxious patient (ch. 4, PDF pp. 83–84). Dunn's conclusion about his own process is that his team had been observing people rather than the job, and that design requirements cannot be gathered in a conference room (ch. 4, PDF pp. 84–85). Procter & Gamble's ten-cent diaper in China is the second case: the effort went into a cheap containment device and the diapers did not sell, until a focus-group answer, laughed at in translation, revealed that the baby sleeping through the night had restored the parents' intimacy; P&G then ran a two-year study with the Beijing Children's Hospital's Sleep Research Center reporting that babies in Pampers fell asleep 30 percent faster and slept an extra thirty minutes, advertised the social and emotional benefit, and by 2013 held roughly 30 percent of the market with an estimated $1.6 billion in sales (ch. 4, PDF pp. 85–87). The chapter states that a consumer who cannot see his job in the product ends the matter, and that hiring a product for the wrong job risks losing that consumer permanently, which makes signaling who a product is not for part of the work (ch. 4, PDF p. 87).

### Chapter 5: How to Hear What Your Customers Don't Say (PDF pp. 91–115)

Pleasant Rowland did no research before founding American Girl and sat through one focus group in which mothers said their daughters would never want history-based dolls and that the accessories would end up in the vacuum cleaner; she sold the company to Mattel thirteen years later for $700 million (ch. 5, PDF pp. 91–92). The chapter's claim is that what people say is unreliable and that data is no refuge: everyone reports caring about the environment and stocks disposable diapers and plastic pods, and toy-industry data told Rowland that girls aged seven to twelve would not play with dolls (ch. 5, PDF pp. 92–93). Most data tracks only the purchase, which the book calls the Big Hire, and misses the moment of use, the Little Hire; a dress bought is not consumed until the tag comes off, and an app tracked by downloads says nothing about whether it is doing a job (ch. 5, PDF pp. 92–93). Because jobs already exist, hiring anything new means firing something, including the solution of doing nothing: wristwatches were fired when phones arrived, and the people who hired Depend Silhouette fired staying at home (ch. 5, PDF p. 93).

The four forces are set out here. Pushing toward change: the push of the situation, which must be more than nagging, and the pull of the new solution, which is where companies concentrate. Opposing it: the habits of the present, and the anxiety of choosing something new (ch. 5, PDF pp. 93–94). Kahneman and Tversky's loss aversion is cited for the claim that avoiding loss weighs about twice as heavily as an equivalent gain (ch. 5, PDF p. 94, and the endnote at PDF p. 115 citing the 1979 *Econometrica* paper). The anxieties named are cost, learning, and the unknown; health clubs are the example of an industry that only recently understood annual contracts as a barrier to joining (ch. 5, PDF p. 94). ING Direct opened cafés where no teller transaction is possible, purely so a virtual bank would feel real, and SNHU's nonprofit status and real campus are read as anxiety reducers (ch. 5, PDF pp. 94–95). The rule stated is that the pull of the new must exceed the sum of inertia and anxiety, and that anything with emotional or social dimensions is harder to fire (ch. 5, PDF p. 95). The argument is extended explicitly to business-to-business buying, where a plant manager's sleepless nights about supply and a first-time project manager's wish to look competent are the emotional and social dimensions inside a procurement process (ch. 5, PDF p. 95). Mercer is the worked case: its retirement consulting business was threatened by the shift away from defined-benefit plans, and the job its HR and finance buyers were hiring it for included being seen to have left no stone unturned; the Pension Risk Exchange replaced a six-month, opaque, quote-by-quote process with real-time trading plus a modeling module that let a client rehearse a buyout before committing, which the book describes as an anxiety-reducing practice run (ch. 5, PDF pp. 95–97).

Storyboarding is the recommended method, on Pixar's pattern of once upon a time, every day, one day, because of that; Airbnb storyboarded forty-five emotional moments for hosts and guests before launching, and its CEO says the realism of a storyboard is what forces the decisions (ch. 5, PDF pp. 98–99). The instruction on method is explicit: what you look for is moments of struggle, tradeoffs, and surprises, and how you look is ground-level extended narrative with a sample size of one, clustered afterward into patterns rather than broken into categories (ch. 5, PDF p. 99).

Most of the chapter is then given over to a lightly edited transcript of a jobs interview by Moesta and colleagues at the Re-Wired Group with a Chicago entrepreneur who had bought a mattress forty-five days earlier (ch. 5, PDF pp. 100–110). The introduction to it says plainly that its ambling, painstaking quality is intentional, that it is not a prototype for all customer research, and that there is no black box and no magical algorithm (ch. 5, PDF p. 100). The interview never asks about the mattress: it asks about the Costco trip, who was there, what else was in the cart, how long they were in the store, when he first thought about it (ch. 5, PDF pp. 101–104). What emerges is a four-year history — an overpriced pillow-top bought from an uncle, two good years, a year of waking with headaches and backaches, flipping and rotating and sleeping without pillows, a warranty that required an inch and a half of sinkage he did not have, three months of online research, a Groupon he nearly bought and did not because of the return problem, and a wife's blessing in the aisle that removed the last anxiety (ch. 5, PDF pp. 104–110). The reading offered: he looks like an impulse buyer and is not; he was firing the old mattress more than hiring a new one; the failed Little Hires every morning drove the decision; and Costco's returns policy removed the final obstacle (ch. 5, PDF pp. 110–111). The competing hires were Advil and Red Bull (ch. 5, PDF p. 112). The chapter also names what a single interview is worth — hypotheses and better questions, not a conclusion — and restates the sampling rule as many data points from a small sample rather than few from a large one (ch. 5, PDF pp. 111–113). An endnote records that the theory is compatible with design thinking rather than opposed to it (ch. 5 endnote, PDF p. 115).

### Chapter 6: Building Your Résumé (PDF pp. 116–137)

The chapter's claim is that new products succeed because of the experiences they enable and not the features they carry, and that the set of experiences is the product's résumé (ch. 6, PDF p. 116). American Girl is the case: the dolls are described by the author as nice rather than amazing, the typical purchaser is estimated to spend more than six hundred dollars in total, the company has sold 29 million dolls and takes more than $500 million a year, and Toys R Us, Walmart, and Disney have all tried similar dolls at a fraction of the price without denting it (ch. 6, PDF pp. 116–117). What is hard to copy is the surrounding design: dolls never sold in general toy stores, a catalog and then a small number of destination stores, doll hospitals, restaurants, birthday parties, historically researched books, and a belly band on the box that added two cents and twenty-seven seconds to packaging and was kept over the designers' objection because the split second of unwrapping is the experience (ch. 6, PDF pp. 117–119).

The job spec is introduced as the innovator's side of the job: the functional, social, and emotional dimensions, the tradeoffs the customer will make, the full competing set, and the obstacles and anxieties to be overcome, written down as the blueprint (ch. 6, PDF p. 120). Three layers are named — uncovering the job, creating the desired experiences, and integrating around the job — and the claim is that a company doing all three has something competitors cannot easily copy (ch. 6, PDF p. 121). IKEA is the example of a business with no secrets that nobody copies: the store layout, the flat packs, the child-care area, the café, the single included tool stored inside a piece of wood, and a customer base organized by a job rather than a demographic (ch. 6, PDF pp. 121–122). The premium-price argument is made here: the struggle is already costing the customer time, energy, and money, so a premium looks small against what the failure has cost (ch. 6, PDF pp. 122–123). The book then draws a line between that and the premium extracted through interdependence — printer ink, chargers, phone cases — which it says creates anxiety rather than resolving it and earns resentment rather than gratitude (ch. 6, PDF p. 123).

Medtronic in India is the obstacles case. Pacemakers sold poorly in a country where heart disease is the leading killer; interviews with more than a hundred physicians, nurses, administrators, and patients found four barriers — awareness, diagnostics, navigating the care pathway, and affordability — and the biggest competitor was nonconsumption (ch. 6, PDF pp. 124–125). The local general practitioner had about thirty seconds per patient, and a referral dropped the patient into a system where he was a stranger and paying out of pocket (ch. 6, PDF pp. 125–126). Medtronic ran screening clinics with local cardiologists, created a loan program, and learned from patients that a loan expiring at death was what they wanted while their families preferred a bridge loan because they would rally to raise the money; loans were approved in about two days with minimal paperwork and no asset mortgage (ch. 6, PDF p. 126). The patient counselor role, first called Sherpas, became popular enough that hospitals asked for it outside the program (ch. 6, PDF p. 126). The reported figures to December 2015: around 167,000 screened, 89,900 counseled, around 15,000 treated, and financial assistance in approximately 550 cases (ch. 6, PDF p. 127).

Car rental is the negative example — an industry whose complaints are about the experience and not the cars, competing on price and fleet, with customers working around it — and Uber the positive one, where knowing the driver, the wait, the route, and the payment removes the anxieties of hailing a cab (ch. 6, PDF pp. 127–129). Amazon's reviews are called the hardest thing for a would-be competitor to copy, because they let a buyer find people who hired the product for the same job; the book notes Amazon's 2015 weighting of newer, verified, and helpful-voted reviews, and cites PowerReviews for the claim that as many as 95 percent of consumers use reviews and 86 percent call them essential, with nearly a third of consumers under forty-five consulting them for every purchase (ch. 6, PDF pp. 129–131). Reviews cut both ways, and the chapter calls it a new obligation to convey who should not hire the product: Airbnb coaches hosts to describe a listing so guests are not disappointed, and an endnote records a restaurateur whose early good reviews brought foodies he never wanted and then bad reviews from them (ch. 6, PDF p. 131 and the endnote at PDF p. 137).

The chapter closes on the purpose brand — a brand that has become synonymous with a job — listing Uber, TurboTax, Disney, Mayo Clinic, OnStar, Harvard, Match.com, OpenTable, and LinkedIn (ch. 6, PDF p. 132). FedEx is the worked example: the job of getting something there overnight with certainty had always existed, the alternatives tarnished their own brands doing it badly, and FedEx built the brand by doing the job rather than by advertising, becoming a verb; Starbucks, Google, and Craigslist are named as brands built the same way with little early advertising (ch. 6, PDF pp. 133–134). Milwaukee's Sawzall and HOLE HAWG are the counter-example inside one company: two purpose brands that the book says have held more than 80 percent of their two job markets for decades, while the same company's other tools, sold under the corporate name, are not celebrated (ch. 6, PDF p. 133). Volvo is the cautionary tale: a purpose brand for safety since 1927, blurred after Ford bought it in 1999 and chased luxury, unprofitable by 2005, sold to Geely at a substantial loss in 2010, growing again in 2015 under a renewed safety focus, and the author says he fears the purpose brand may be gone for good (ch. 6, PDF pp. 134–135). Brands that fail to integrate around a job are said to become category placeholders competing on price (ch. 6, PDF p. 134).

### Chapter 7: Integrating Around a Job (PDF pp. 139–159)

Competitive advantage, this chapter argues, is conferred by processes rather than by resources or products, because resources are fungible and products are copyable (ch. 7, PDF p. 140). The opening case is the author's own week of appointments at the Mayo Clinic, where one person owned the sequence, booked specialists in real time, made sure an MRI was done by 11:30 for a 2:00 appointment, and eliminated the anxieties before he could form them; every specialist keeps openings for real-time needs (ch. 7, PDF pp. 139–140). A traditional hospital coordinates with good intentions and gives every patient a different experience, which the book says is still a process, just not a deliberate one, quoting Deming: a person who cannot describe what they are doing as a process does not know what they are doing (ch. 7, PDF pp. 140–141).

Toyota opened its plants to rival executives twice a month with nothing off-limits, because the advantage lay in processes that are hard to see and harder to copy; a GM manager who toured it told *This American Life* that the visitors were asking the wrong questions (ch. 7, PDF pp. 141–142). Pixar published its creative process and nobody has matched it; SNHU gives tours to other institutions for the same reason (ch. 7, PDF p. 142). Processes are named as part of an organization's unspoken culture, citing Edgar Schein (ch. 7, PDF p. 142). McKinsey is the example of processes robust enough that consultants of very different backgrounds can be plugged into them (ch. 7, PDF pp. 142–143). SNHU's shift is given as a measurement story: response time moved from weeks to minutes, the university now pays the ten-dollar transcript fee itself rather than asking the prospect to chase it, and the general claim is that processes aligned with a job move complexity and nuisance from the customer to the vendor (ch. 7, PDF pp. 143–144).

On organizing: reorganizations are common and mostly fail, with a 2010 Bain & Company study of fifty-seven major reorganizations finding fewer than a third produced meaningful improvement and some destroying value (ch. 7, PDF p. 144 and the endnote at PDF p. 159). What matters more than reporting lines is how parts of the organization interact to deliver the job, and in most companies nobody owns the customer's job (ch. 7, PDF p. 145). The historical example is Dwight Harken — Taddy Hall's grandfather — who noticed in 1952 that patients were surviving complex surgery and dying in post-op because the critical-care job had no owner in any existing function, and invented the intensive care unit (ch. 7, PDF p. 145). The modern one is the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, where Ethan Bernstein and the implementation team put research, markets, and regulations in one division and supervision, enforcement, and fair lending in another, against the usual functional silos, and ran a weekly policy committee focused on one job at a time; Bernstein's account is that everyone in the room had scars and that a job was what kept the meeting from becoming an argument about them (ch. 7, PDF pp. 145–147).

Measurement follows the job: SNHU counts minutes to respond, Amazon counts when an order is delivered rather than shipped, and Intuit builds a metric per product from the customer benefit (ch. 7, PDF p. 147). Scott Cook's counter-example is a registration wall added so the sales organization could upsell, which produced revenue and made registering hard, and he notes that the revenue figure cannot say how much Intuit would have had anyway (ch. 7, PDF pp. 147–148). Cook is also quoted admitting the hard part: the metrics do not fall out of the systems, there is no automatic way to measure the hours an accountant did not spend, and Intuit interpolates from surveys and server data (ch. 7, PDF p. 148). Amazon's delivery promise is presented as hundreds of processes behind one line of text, with the innovation process starting from a mock press release read by engineers and analysts as well as marketers (ch. 7, PDF p. 149). The chapter adds that processes must stay flexible even as they stay consistent, because jobs endure and solutions change, and borrows the programming idea of subroutines for modular processes reused across activities (ch. 7, PDF pp. 149–150).

OnStar is the long case. It began as what its founding CEO calls the coolest brochure ware, a bundle of features for auto-show coverage, conceived as a luxury option (ch. 7, PDF pp. 151–152). GM's CEO ruled that the division would get no internal credit for selling cars, which forced it to find a business of its own (ch. 7, PDF p. 152). Two anomalies broke the frame: luxury buyers canceled the concierge service, and Chevy drivers subscribed at the same rate as Cadillac buyers (ch. 7, PDF p. 152). Chet Huber required every one of his three hundred staff to spend an hour listening to real calls, and what they heard — crashes in progress, cars still moving, people screaming — reframed the job as peace of mind while driving, which explained the Chevy result, since peace of mind is not a luxury upgrade (ch. 7, PDF pp. 152–153). Directions turned out to be less about finding a restaurant and more about routing through safe streets in an unfamiliar place after dark (ch. 7, PDF p. 153). The processes built around that job are the point of the case: a midcycle validation process invented outside GM's three-to-five-year development cycle, so that OnStar could ship its fifth generation while rivals who bought the same technology were still on the third, tested against every combination of windows, rain, CD player, and deployed airbag (ch. 7, PDF pp. 154–155). Ford and Qualcomm's Wingcast, announced in 2000 for a 2003 launch, was scrapped two years later (ch. 7, PDF p. 155). Anshu Sharma's stack fallacy is brought in for the general form of Ford's mistake — engineers overweight their own layer and underweight the application above it — with the vegetable garden and the restaurant as the non-technical illustration, and the aside that eight of ten restaurants fail within five years (ch. 7, PDF pp. 155–156). FranklinCovey returns at the end: revenue doubled and profit rose tenfold over six years by shifting from selling training to owning outcomes, two-thirds of the R&D budget now goes to process innovation, and Marriott could staff ten people against a program that most customers cannot (ch. 7, PDF pp. 156–157). The chapter's own counterweight is the milk shake ending: the fast-food chain never implemented the changes, because local managers found the process and resource changes too hard and the idea died quietly (ch. 7, PDF p. 157).

### Chapter 8: Keeping Your Eye on the Job (PDF pp. 160–175)

The chapter opens on Levitt's quarter-inch hole and Drucker's warning that the customer rarely buys what the company thinks it is selling, calls them the two most important marketing insights of the last century, and observes that marketers' actions say otherwise (ch. 8, PDF p. 160). V8 is the case. In a crowded juice aisle the brand's managers had framed their task as differentiating against other drinks; after reading an early jobs article they looked for a job instead and found one, told through a customer who had promised his mother he would eat his vegetables and hated peeling carrots and boiling spinach (ch. 8, PDF pp. 161–162). Reframed, V8 competes against vegetables rather than against Diet Coke, the advertising was changed to juxtapose it with peeling and boiling, and sales are reported to have quadrupled in under a year (ch. 8, PDF p. 162). Then the team moved on, and the line refragmented into Spicy Hot, Lemon, Picante, Roasted Chicken, Low-sodium, Organic, Splash, and V-Fusion, which the author reads as clarity lost (ch. 8, PDF p. 162). An endnote records that the manager who told him the story is deliberately unnamed (ch. 8 endnote, PDF p. 174).

The three fallacies follow. **Active versus passive data.** Before launch a company is immersed in the unstructured context of the struggle, which the book calls passive data — always present, never loud, with no voice or champion. After launch, sales, customers, investments, and competitive benchmarks generate active data that is easy to track and shouts, and managers move from the hazy story to the crisp spreadsheet (ch. 8, PDF pp. 163–166). The book insists that this feels like progress and is a shift in the model of reality, because all data is an abstraction built on a decision about what to collect (ch. 8, PDF p. 166). Its examples of managing the number instead of the thing are schools teaching to the test, medicine treating blood pressure rather than its cause, and a share buyback that raises earnings per share while changing nothing (ch. 8, PDF p. 166). Discount retail is the industry example: a field organized around products and price points in which Walmart, Costco, and Target outlasted Kmart, Ann & Hope, Woolworth, Zayre, Bradlees, and Caldor, with no way to predict the winner in advance, and the observation that nobody has a job of spending a couple of hours at the store (ch. 8, PDF pp. 163–164). Levitt's railroads are cited for the same point: the need for transportation grew and the railroads assumed themselves to be in the railroad business (ch. 8, PDF p. 164). **Surface growth.** The marginal cost of selling more to existing customers is low and the profit is alluring, so companies copy or acquire adjacent products and end up doing many jobs for many customers, which confuses buyers and leaves the company exposed to a rival focused on one job (ch. 8, PDF pp. 166–167). The *New York Times* is the example, with four reader jobs each met better by someone else: the *Economist* for a weekly briefing, television for unwinding, the free *Metro* for the commute (ch. 8, PDF p. 167). An endnote makes the same case about DeWalt after Black & Decker bought it (ch. 8 endnote, PDF p. 174). **Conforming data.** Data bends toward the view it is asked to support; the book cites Nate Silver on prediction failures following the signals that tell the story we want, and describes the meeting in which sales, marketing, R&D, and the business head each arrive with data modeling reality through their own incentives, none of them wrong and none of them objective, and none of them the customer's job (ch. 8, PDF pp. 168–169). Gerald Zaltman is quoted on treating facts as insights and on research used to prove points, and on decisions that look like a leap of faith after the data has already been skewed toward one option (ch. 8, PDF p. 169).

The chapter's strongest general claim is about the status of numbers: Johnson and Kaplan are cited for financial statements being the product of estimation, negotiation, debate, and politics; the book says all data is man-made and carries the agenda of whoever created it, wittingly or not, and that the subjectivity of numbers hides behind their precision while the subjectivity of ethnography is obvious; Tom Monahan's joke about endowing a Museum of False Precision is the image (ch. 8, PDF pp. 169–170). Google Flu Trends is the worked example: an algorithm matching fifty million search terms against 1,152 data points that a 2014 *Science* paper found had overestimated US flu cases for more than two years, with the errors attributed in part to the engineers' choices about what to include, faults the authors named algorithmic dynamics and big data hubris (ch. 8, PDF pp. 170–171). An endnote enlarges the point with Bobby Kennedy on what gross national product does not measure (ch. 8 endnote, PDF pp. 174–175).

### Chapter 9: The Jobs-Focused Organization (PDF pp. 176–194)

TurboTax opens the chapter. For years the team polished the interview that collects a customer's data, debating which of some 150 requested features to build, with no way to choose between them; a new general manager reframed it: customers were not hiring a better interview, they were hiring the product to get their taxes done (ch. 9, PDF pp. 176–177). The goal became eliminating the interview rather than improving it, which the book says produced an immediate burst of creativity; the progress reported is downloading W-2 data from a payroll company, and a 2015 experiment prefilling one chapter of the questionnaire that produced a noticeable rise in completion even where customers had to correct the prefilled data (ch. 9, PDF p. 177). Cook estimates a true no-interview version might take a decade (ch. 9, PDF p. 177). Four benefits of a jobs-focused organization are listed: distributed decision making, resource alignment, inspiration, and better measurement (ch. 9, PDF p. 178).

Mission statements are the foil. Five are quoted from Fortune 500 companies — Becton Dickinson, Bristol-Myers Squibb, BNSF, Chevron, J.P. Morgan — as representative and too general to guide a daily decision, which the book likens to the themes of a life rather than to jobs (ch. 9, PDF p. 179). Unilever's Lifebuoy is the contrast and, notably, the redemption of the margarine story: a job nested under the mission of helping children in emerging markets reach five, in circumstances where people wash their hands for about seven seconds and rarely more than fifteen, and where nearly 400,000 Indian children under five die a year of diarrheal disease; the product is a soap that changes color at ten seconds (ch. 9, PDF pp. 179–180). Culture is described as what emerges when people solve problems the same way and it works, and the military idea of commander's intent is borrowed for a job spec that lets people decide without being told, with Roman emperors sending a governor over the hill as the older image (ch. 9, PDF pp. 180–181). Mercer's Jacques Goulet is quoted on the value of the phrase being its plainness (ch. 9, PDF p. 181).

OnStar returns as the two-sided compass. Clarity about peace of mind made it easier to decide what did not belong, and Huber says the focus greatly simplifies (ch. 9, PDF p. 182). Hurricanes Katrina and Rita are the example: during Katrina, callers were told their plan did not include real-time directions and were offered an upgrade; before Rita, the employee responsible simply gave everyone in the crisis area every service, a decision Huber says took about fifteen seconds in his office, and then the team cobbled together a system to detect and divert calls from the region (ch. 9, PDF pp. 182–183). The second example is an algorithm to tell 911 responders the severity of a crash from change in velocity, seat-belt use, direction of force, and number of cars, which the engineers volunteered for because it was aligned with the job (ch. 9, PDF p. 184). SNHU's version is a career adviser buying a struggling student a $200 grocery card on her own initiative, and a help-desk employee and an adviser making a case to the dean for a very ill student a few credits short and then flying to present her diploma in the hospital (ch. 9, PDF pp. 184–185). American Girl's internal shorthand was that the company is story and not stuff (ch. 9, PDF p. 185).

Measurement gets its own section. Cook says Intuit measured everything that rolled off the servers and was not measuring whether it was improving customers' lives, because that is hard (ch. 9, PDF p. 186). Amazon is the counter-model, with vast selection, low prices, and fast delivery monitored minute by minute, delays treated as defects rather than accidents, a price-scraping robot checking hundreds of benchmark products twice a day with human review below a margin threshold, and an old Nike shoe handed out as an award (ch. 9, PDF pp. 186–187). SNHU keeps one statistic in front: whether graduates would do it again, which as of early 2016 stood at 95 percent (ch. 9, PDF p. 187).

Deseret News is the chapter's long case and the book's clearest story of a job found in an audience nobody had named. On the night of the Boston Marathon bombing in 2013 its then-CEO Clark Gilbert watched the newspapers in his hotel lobby carry a story that a full news cycle had overtaken (ch. 9, PDF p. 187). Newspapers had historically served four or five jobs — classifieds, opinion, breaking news — and muddled them (ch. 9, PDF p. 188). What began as demographic segmentation ended as a job: to be well-informed and confident while staying true to one's beliefs, held by three subgroups the team called tolerant believers, devoted denominationalists, and strugglers, together like-minded believers, whom Deseret's research put at nearly 56 percent of American news consumers and who could not be found on demographic or psychographic axes (ch. 9, PDF pp. 188–189). Gilbert's description of their reading habits is that they admired the rigor of the *New York Times* and felt unrecognized by it, and heard their values from Sean Hannity in a register they found angry (ch. 9, PDF p. 189). The functional half of the job was borrowed from the *Dallas Morning News* as an acronym, PICA: Perspective, Insight, Context, and Analysis, on the circumstance of the morning after a story has broken; the emotional half layered family and faith on top (ch. 9, PDF p. 190). The operating consequences are given: cover five bills out of thirty in a legislative session because they affect a family, and use an internal rallying cry that the paper is not the *Sacramento Bee*, which the book says became the worst insult available inside the organization; some employees were restructured or counseled out (ch. 9, PDF pp. 190–191). Reported results: circulation gains against traditional print rivals, online traffic up, and social followers from the low millions to over 100 million across the FamilyShare Network, built around the job rather than around the publication (ch. 9, PDF p. 191). The chapter ends on why this is hard to hold: a start-up's organizing unit is the customer's job, and growth replaces it with customers, products, competitors, and investors (ch. 9, PDF p. 192).

### Chapter 10: Final Observations About the Theory of Jobs (PDF pp. 195–205)

The last chapter defends the theory's status and draws its boundaries. Jobs Theory was built inductively over two decades, because failure in innovation was too common to start from a proposition of causality and test it deductively (ch. 10, PDF p. 196). A construct is defined as an abstraction that lets an observer see dynamics rather than static correlations, with Laurent's chemical visualizations and Adam Smith's invisible hand as precedents; "job" is named as such a construct, and "hire" and "fire" as the terms that made buying and selling visible (ch. 10, PDF p. 196). The objection that stories about real people are not data is answered by saying numbers were made by people who chose what to include and what to discard, citing Johnson and Kaplan again, and that stories are rich where numbers are broad and shallow (ch. 10, PDF pp. 196–197).

On anomalies, the author says a theory needs them, that finding one disproves nothing, and that the academic habit of publishing a disproof and resting on it helps nobody; he invites readers to send him what the theory cannot explain (ch. 10, PDF pp. 197–198). The boundaries follow, drawn against the misapplication of the word "disruption" (ch. 10, PDF p. 198). Two tests are given. A job described in adjectives and adverbs is not a job: convenience is an experience, not a job, and a valid one is expressed in verbs and nouns (ch. 10, PDF p. 198). And a job must sit at a level of abstraction where the candidates come from different product categories: a chocolate milk shake in a twelve-ounce disposable container is a preference, because only milk shakes qualify, while wanting something to occupy a drive and hold off a 10:00 a.m. hunger admits bananas, doughnuts, bagels, Snickers, and coffee (ch. 10, PDF pp. 198–199). The house-wrap example makes the same point twice: a specification listing coefficients of friction, thermal conduction, and toughness admits only Tyvek or nothing, while wanting a family warm in a Boston winter admits blown pulp, fiberglass, Tyvek, extra sweaters, open windows, dehumidifiers, or moving to San Francisco (ch. 10, PDF pp. 199–200).

The chapter closes with the theory applied outside business. *How Will You Measure Your Life?* asks what job children hire parents to do and what job a wife might hire a husband for, noting she could hire a tradesman, friends, her profession, or live without the job done (ch. 10, PDF p. 201). *Disrupting Class* holds that school is not the job: children need to feel successful and to have friends every day, and a student for whom school does not do that can fire it and hire a gang, a minimum-wage job and a car, athletics, or electronic games (ch. 10, PDF pp. 201–202; the same argument appears in the ch. 4 endnote at PDF p. 89). *The Innovator's Prescription* holds that most people want to be healthy enough not to think about health, while fee-for-service providers earn when members get sick, so the two jobs are misaligned; Intermountain, Kaiser Permanente, and Geisinger are named as providers aligning them by carrying the cost of care (ch. 10, PDF pp. 202–203). The last pages extend the question to elected officials and to congregations, and restate that good theory teaches how to think rather than what to think (ch. 10, PDF p. 203). The closing line of the argument is that venture capital is an industry organized around innovation as a game of odds, and that the reader should leave relying on luck to the other guys (ch. 10, PDF p. 204).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the authors limit the claim, admit a counter-case, or say the theory does not apply.

- **Where the theory does not work at all.** Jobs Theory is stated to be useless where there is no real struggle, or where existing solutions are good enough, or where the decision rests almost entirely on mathematical analysis, commodities trading being the named case; in those circumstances there is no bundle of social, emotional, and functional needs, only a rational decision a computer could make as well (ch. 2, PDF p. 48). Cost and efficiency are said not to be core elements of a job (ch. 2, PDF p. 48).
- **The theory is one of many, and its boundaries matter.** The nature of the problem dictates whether this is the right theory, as with every theory, and knowing that a theory does not explain something is itself valuable because it sends the reader elsewhere (ch. 2, PDF p. 48). The authors invite a better set of processes if anyone has one (ch. 2, PDF p. 48), welcome other voices in the jobs space using different words and methods (ch. 1, PDF p. 27), and ask readers to send in what the theory cannot explain (ch. 10, PDF pp. 197–198).
- **Two explicit boundary tests.** A job stated in adjectives or adverbs is not a job — convenience is an experience (ch. 10, PDF p. 198). A job whose candidate solutions all come from one product category is a preference or a technical specification, not a job (ch. 10, PDF pp. 198–200).
- **A need is not a job, and a life theme is not a job either.** Feeling like a good dad is important and will not trigger a purchase, because the concept is too abstract for a company to build against without knowing the circumstance (ch. 2, PDF p. 40).
- **Jobs are not created, and the solution is not the thing to love.** Snapchat is named as vulnerable to a competitor who understands the same job better, and the instruction is to focus on the underlying job rather than the present solution (ch. 2, PDF p. 44).
- **On method there is no one right way.** The five hunting grounds are offered as a sampling and not a comprehensive or step-by-step manual, and the authors say plainly they do not believe there is any one right way to identify jobs (ch. 4, PDF p. 73). Existing research — personas, ethnography, focus groups, customer panels, competitive analysis — stays valid as a starting point (ch. 4, PDF p. 74).
- **The mattress interview is not a template.** Its ambling, painstaking quality is deliberate, it is explicitly not a prototype for all customer research, and there is no magical algorithm and no black box (ch. 5, PDF p. 100).
- **One interview decides nothing.** Asked what a mattress maker should take from that transcript, the book answers: not too much, because uncovering jobs is about clustering insights rather than a single eureka; what a single interview yields is hypotheses and better questions for the next one (ch. 5, PDF pp. 111–112).
- **Not every premium price is the good kind.** Printer ink, chargers, and phone cases are premiums extracted through interdependence; they create anxiety rather than resolving it, and the customer pays while resenting the company (ch. 6, PDF p. 123).
- **Reviews cut both ways.** The same mechanism that helps a good product get hired lets a customer who hired it for the wrong job damage it, which is why the chapter treats conveying who should not hire a product as a new obligation rather than an option (ch. 6, PDF p. 131 and the endnote at PDF p. 137).
- **Org charts are not the enemy.** An organizational structure is called necessary, experts in finance and marketing and customer service are needed, and large-scale reorganizations are said to be not usually practical; the recommended route is processes, metrics, and culture over time rather than a restructuring (ch. 7, PDF pp. 144–145; Chapter Takeaways, PDF p. 158).
- **Efficiency is not the enemy either.** Functional oversight and efficiency are called a requirement of competitive markets; the qualification is that efficiency creates value only in a process that is performing a high-priority job (ch. 9, PDF p. 193). The book asks that optimization carry a factor for job alignment rather than be abandoned (ch. 7, PDF p. 149).
- **Processes are said to need flexibility.** Jobs endure, solutions change, and a process that cannot change risks reshaping the job to fit itself (ch. 7, PDF pp. 149–150; Chapter Takeaways, PDF p. 158).
- **A great process can still be rejected.** The milk shake changes were never implemented, because local managers found the process and resource-allocation changes too hard, and the idea died quietly; the book calls this an organizational immune system (ch. 7, PDF p. 157).
- **The theory is retrospective in its examples and does not claim to be.** The authors note the temptation to retrofit an explanation onto a success and say the value of the theory is in predicting new ones rather than explaining past ones (ch. 1, PDF pp. 27–28).
- **The ethics caveat.** The theory explains why customers make the choices they make and not whether a company should solve a given job; cigarettes are named as a product hired for an array of jobs and bad for the customer, and ethical choices are called equally important to get right (ch. 1 endnote, PDF p. 30).
- **Disclosed interests.** Endnotes record that the author has served on the board of trustees of SNHU and on FranklinCovey's board since 2004 (ch. 3 endnote, PDF p. 68), serves as a volunteer trustee of Intermountain Healthcare (ch. 4 endnote, PDF p. 90), and has served on the editorial advisory board of Deseret News (ch. 9 endnote, PDF p. 194). Innosight, whose senior partner David Duncan is a coauthor, is a consultancy Christensen cofounded and the firm on the Medtronic and Mercer engagements (ch. 1, PDF p. 27; ch. 5, PDF p. 95; ch. 6, PDF p. 124).
- **An Airbnb caveat.** The claim that Airbnb's circumstances differ from a hotel's is limited by an endnote to the company's early years, after which it identified other jobs higher up the market (ch. 2 endnote, PDF p. 51).

## 7. What has changed since publication

- **Clayton Christensen (throughout; the invitation at ch. 10, PDF pp. 197–198).** The lead author died on January 23, 2020, at sixty-seven, of complications of leukemia, having been diagnosed with follicular lymphoma in 2010 ([Wikipedia, Clayton Christensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clayton_Christensen)). The book's closing invitation — send me the anomalies the theory cannot explain, and I am building a way to improve these theories collaboratively online — can no longer be taken up with him. ## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Every case is chosen after it worked.** The evidence is ten or so successes — the milk shake, SNHU, Intuit, American Girl, IKEA, Medtronic, OnStar, Deseret News, FranklinCovey, Uber — with no company that applied Jobs Theory and failed, and no base rate against which the successes could be read. The book sees the objection and names it: it is tempting to retrofit an explanation onto a success, and the value of the theory is said to be predicting new ones rather than explaining past ones (ch. 1, PDF pp. 27–28). It then spends nine chapters on retrospective cases and offers no prediction made in advance and later checked. The nearest thing to a negative case is the fast-food chain that declined to implement the milk shake changes (ch. 7, PDF p. 157), which is a story about a company not adopting the theory rather than about the theory being wrong.
- **The construct absorbs both outcomes.** Success is explained by nailing a job and failure by losing sight of one, and nothing in the book says what evidence would distinguish them at the time. The V8 story runs both directions in two pages: sales quadrupled because the job was found, then the line refragmented because the job was lost, with the author stating outright that he does not know the internal workings and is inferring (ch. 8, PDF p. 162). Unilever's margarine is read the same way from an admitted outsider's vantage (ch. 1, PDF p. 25).
- **The method rests on the self-report the book distrusts.** Chapter 5 argues that customers cannot say what they want and that their actions contradict their words (ch. 5, PDF pp. 92–93), and then builds its instrument out of retrospective self-report: the mattress interviewee reconstructs four years of feeling from memory, dates his wife's complaints to "about six months ago", and does not himself say that the returns policy removed his last obstacle or that his wife's assent was the decisive anxiety — those are the interviewer's inferences (ch. 5, PDF pp. 105–106, 110–111). The chapter's own answer, that a single interview yields hypotheses and not conclusions (ch. 5, PDF pp. 111–112), is a limit on the reader and not on the cases the book itself builds from one or two accounts.
- **The boundary tests arrive after the cases, and some cases do not clearly pass them.** Chapter 10 rules that a job stated in adjectives or adverbs is not a job, and that a job's candidate solutions must come from different product categories (ch. 10, PDF pp. 198–200). Peace of mind while driving (ch. 7, PDF p. 153) and being well-informed with news that reflects one's values (ch. 9, PDF p. 189) are both offered earlier as exemplary jobs; both are closer in form to the mission statements chapter 9 rejects as too general to guide a decision (ch. 9, PDF p. 179) than to the commute job whose candidates are bananas, bagels, and coffee. The book does not test its own cases against its own rule.
- **Price is excluded from the definition and used throughout the cases.** Cost and efficiency are said not to be core elements of a job, and a decision that is essentially a calculation is placed outside the theory (ch. 2, PDF p. 48). Yet QuickBooks wins at twice the price with half the functionality (ch. 3, PDF p. 66), IKEA's flat packs and single tool are partly an economics of assembly (ch. 6, PDF pp. 121–122), affordability is one of Medtronic's four barriers and produces a loan program (ch. 6, PDF pp. 125–126), and a whole section argues about which premiums customers will pay gladly (ch. 6, PDF pp. 122–123). The definition and the cases are using price differently.
- **Nonconsumption is sized by the party selling into it.** The claim that 40 percent of Airbnb guests would not have traveled comes from Airbnb's head of global hospitality (ch. 4, PDF p. 76); the incontinence prevalence figures that make the Depend opportunity large are Kimberly-Clark's own research (ch. 4, PDF p. 77); the 56 percent of American news consumers who are like-minded believers is Deseret's research (ch. 9, PDF p. 189). Each number sizes a market for the organization that produced it, and the book reports them without an outside check.
- **Interests are disclosed in endnotes rather than at the point of use.** The author sat on SNHU's board of trustees and has sat on FranklinCovey's since 2004 (ch. 3 endnote, PDF p. 68), is a volunteer trustee of Intermountain (ch. 4 endnote, PDF p. 90), and has served on Deseret News's editorial advisory board (ch. 9 endnote, PDF p. 194). Those four supply the book's four longest organizational cases. Innosight, which the author cofounded and where coauthor David Duncan is a senior partner, ran the Medtronic and Mercer engagements (ch. 5, PDF p. 95; ch. 6, PDF p. 124), and coauthor Taddy Hall led the Nielsen project that produces the twenty-thousand-launches figure and the Sargento case (Introduction, PDF p. 14; ch. 3, PDF pp. 61–62). A reader meeting SNHU on PDF page 53 finds the board membership fifteen pages later.
- **Figures without a citation.** The opening McKinsey poll at 84 and 94 percent carries no endnote (Introduction, PDF p. 10), unlike the *strategy+business* and *Wired* claims on the same page. So do the under-25-percent odds for a deliberate disruptor (ch. 1, PDF p. 19), Milwaukee's more than 80 percent of two job markets for decades (ch. 6, PDF p. 133), and the claim that eight of ten restaurants fail within five years (ch. 7, PDF p. 156), which is a widely repeated statistic and is used to carry a point about stack fallacy.
- **The argument against data is made with data.** The book holds that all data is man-made and that numbers hide their subjectivity behind precision (ch. 8, PDF pp. 169–170), and its own case for the problem is built from percentages: 84 and 94 percent of executives, $680 billion of R&D, ninety-two breakthroughs out of twenty thousand launches. It does not apply its own standard to those.
- **The invitation the book closes on cannot be accepted.** Readers are asked to send the anomalies the theory cannot explain, and told that a way to improve the theories collaboratively online is being developed (ch. 10, PDF pp. 197–198). Section 7 records that Christensen died in January 2020; no successor channel is named in the text.
- **The examples have aged unevenly, and the aging cuts at the argument in two places.** American Girl's advantage was located in an experience delivered through destination stores, doll hospitals, and restaurants, which by 2025 is seven US stores; Deseret News's turnaround was in a print product that stopped printing daily in January 2021. Both are section 7 entries, and both are cases the book offers as durable because experiences integrated into processes are said to be hard to copy (ch. 6, PDF p. 121; ch. 7, PDF p. 142). Neither was undone by a copyist.
