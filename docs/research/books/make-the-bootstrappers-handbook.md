# Make: The Bootstrapper's Handbook, read back

## 1. Source

*MAKE: Bootstrapper's Handbook — building startups the indie way*, Pieter Levels (@levelsio). Self-published as a web-delivered PDF sold from readmake.com; the file has no copyright page, names no publisher, and carries no ISBN. The only date printed anywhere is "Last updated: 2020-12-19" on the ownership page (PDF p. 2), and the author says the book "is continuously updated" like one of his products (Foreword, pp. 11–12), so this read-back describes that 2020-12-19 revision and no other. Read 2026-09-10 and 2026-09-11 from the `pdftotext -layout` extraction of the 222-page PDF, front to back.

**Page rule.** The book prints no page numbers; the footer of every page is a small decorative slash. This file cites the PDF index: cited page = PDF page, offset zero, confirmed by opening PDF pages 25 and 26 in the PDF itself and finding no printed number on either. The book has no numbered chapters. It has a TL;DR (pp. 3–5), a Foreword (pp. 6–13), a section titled "This book" (pp. 14–17), seven named chapters — Idea (pp. 19–36), Build (pp. 37–75), Launch (pp. 76–113), Grow (pp. 114–130), Monetize (pp. 131–170), Automate (pp. 171–183), Exit (pp. 184–215) — and an Epilogue (pp. 216–221). ## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, page by page, with its own exceptions, what has changed since the 2020 revision, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that one person can build a profitable internet business alone, with no investment, by working through seven stages in order: get an idea from a problem in your own life, build it yourself with the tools you already know, launch it early and often where your audience is, grow it organically without ads, monetize by asking users for money, automate the running of it with scripts he calls "robots", and exit only when buyers come to you (TL;DR, pp. 3–4; Epilogue, pp. 217–218). He calls this "the indie way" and "bootstrapped", meaning self-funded from the resources you already have, and argues the odds of a bootstrapped business are "way higher" than a venture-funded one because the goal is reached as soon as the founder can pay himself a living (Foreword, pp. 9–10). The evidence is the author's own products, chiefly Nomad List and Remote OK, which he reports as $83,000 a month, or $1M ARR, at the time of writing, built alone, profitable at margins "up to 90%", and highly automated (Foreword, p. 11). The book is presented as a proof of its own method: it was pre-sold as a Typeform for $22.49 before a line was written, raising "$50,000+" from thousands of pre-orders, and was drafted in public on Google Docs and Twitch (This book, pp. 15–17).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a technical book, and the tools used to build a product "don't really matter" (Build, p. 39). It is not a promise: the book will not make the reader successful, and success is "all up to you" (Foreword, p. 8; Epilogue, p. 219). It is "not startup porn" but "startup life", to be used as a handbook while shipping (TL;DR, p. 5). It is not about scaling a proven business with a team; the book is about "the beginning" (Build, p. 42). It is not anti-venture-capital in absolute terms: VC "has its function and place" (Build, p. 43), and his "VC is bad, indie is good" is a deliberately simplified marketing message (Launch, p. 106). It is not a template to copy: a reader who clones the author's moves is "already too late" (Epilogue, p. 219). And its Exit chapter is the one part the author had not completed himself, written from a sale he rejected (Exit, p. 185). The author also warns twice that the whole book may rest on survivorship bias and that his perspective is "probably biased and skewed" (Foreword, pp. 8, 11).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### TL;DR, Foreword, and This book (pp. 3–17)

The TL;DR states the seven stages in a paragraph each and adds an eighth heading, Ethics, which is "not a chapter but important": no spamming, no manipulating users, no faked social media, no abusing a strong position, and being ethical is itself "a competitive advantage" because most companies are not (TL;DR, pp. 3–5). The Foreword thanks users, followers, family, and named makers, including Patrick McKenzie, Jon Yongfook and Derek Sivers (Foreword, pp. 7–8). The author says he hardly reads books, that books are "outdated by definition", and that the media's picture of startups — San Francisco, $10 million rounds, 100-hour weeks — is wrong because "only 10% or less exit" of VC-funded companies (Foreword, pp. 8–9). Bootstrapping does not exclude going big later, because investors love companies with "proven revenue" (Foreword, p. 10). Persistence is expected: a maker may need to ship 10 to 30 products over one to three years before one works, and a product that does not take off early "probably won't take off later" (Foreword, p. 12). "This book" describes the pre-order on a Typeform at $22.49, the Workflowy list where buyers wrote what they wanted, the "$50,000+" raised before writing, and the live-streamed drafting, with 90% of the process in the open (This book, pp. 15–17).

### Idea (pp. 19–36)

Ideas come from "solving your own problems", because many other people are likely to share a problem the founder has, and because the founder is "the greatest expert" at it (Idea, pp. 20–21). The example is Nomad List, a private spreadsheet of about 25 cities' temperature, internet speed and cost of living that the author tweeted with edit rights left open by accident; after a month, thousands of people had added data on more than 250 cities (Idea, pp. 20–21). The counter-example is a health-care app, which the author says he could not build well because he is not a doctor (Idea, p. 21). First-hand experience matters after launch too; when the author stopped traveling "the product suffered", and a rapper who gets rich loses the world he rapped about (Idea, p. 22). Boosted Boards is quoted saying its founders knew what buyers wanted because they "were the target market" (Idea, p. 22). The Lean Startup instruction to talk to customers is called "an outside perspective"; the advice is to focus on one's own problems (Idea, p. 23). To get original ideas, become a more original person: stop reading books for ideas, and "get outside" (Idea, p. 24). The author states and then partly concedes the privilege critique of solving only your own problems (Idea, p. 24). Start from the problem, never the solution; GPS friend-maps are the recurring solution in search of a problem, and Tinder is GPS applied to a real one (Idea, pp. 24–25).

To get big, start small: niches are too small in economic value for big companies to bother with, and 1,000 people paying $83.33 a month is $1,000,000 a year, tabulated as $5,000 × 200 people down to $10 × 100,000 people, and by subscription from 500 people at $167 a month to 10,000 at $9 (Idea, pp. 25–27). The worked example is booking software for hairdressers, narrowed to hairdressers "that focus on African hair", then widened niche by niche, then vertically into payments, then into a platform; Facebook, Apple, Microsoft, Google and Uber are named as companies that started small (Idea, pp. 27–29). The founders who promise a billion-dollar company are, in the author's experience, the ones who "fail really, really bad" (Idea, pp. 29–30). Keep a list of ideas — the author uses Workflowy, previously Trello, with lists named Concepts, Promising, Building, Success and Failure, plus a one-sentence post-mortem (Idea, pp. 30–31). Work alone early: collaborations "can be very dangerous" because of groupthink, and a person is "more rational" alone (Idea, pp. 31–32). Share ideas freely: ideas are "a dime a dozen", illustrated with Derek Sivers's 2005 multiplier table, where the most brilliant idea with no execution "is worth $20" (Idea, pp. 32–34). The chapter's resources page lists Workflowy, Trello, Stripe, Zapier, Intercom, Olark and Typeform, and the homework is three problems a day for seven days (Idea, pp. 35–36).

### Build (pp. 37–75)

Build fast and minimal, because cheap VPS hosting, PaaS, NGINX and web frameworks mean an idea becomes a basic product "in a matter of days", and users now accept minimal interfaces (Build, pp. 38–39). Apple is the named exception that ships without user testing, but the reader is "not Apple (yet)" (Build, p. 38). The downsides of fast development are bugs, untested devices, abandoned products and spaghetti code, answered with the observation that users do not care what the code looks like (Build, p. 39). Perfection is "your enemy": perfectionism mostly "creates inaction", users tolerate bugs that get fixed, and Gmail is said to have stayed in beta "for a decade" (Build, p. 40). The limit: do not make bad products, "minimum needs to be minimum good", and an MVP must function, with a way to report bugs instantly (Build, p. 41). Build it yourself rather than outsource, because a maker fixes a bug "in 5 minutes" over morning coffee while an outsourced fix waits on a developer on holiday in Peru; about five well-funded direct Nomad List competitors, with $1–10 million and 10–30 people each, "didn't go anywhere" (Build, pp. 41–43). Doing everything yourself does not work in the long term, but the book is about the beginning (Build, p. 42). Two contrasting scripts are printed: the VC narrative ending "Bye money!" and the sustainable one ending in a sale for $500,000 to $10,000,000 (Build, pp. 44–46). Funding removes the "natural tension" of having to survive (Build, p. 46). Costs: a $5 server, a free editor, a $100 Apple license, against developers at $50 to $250 an hour, an MVP of roughly 100 hours, and a full app from $50,000 up; offering a developer half the profit for the idea is "ridiculous" because an unexecuted idea's market value "is $0" and a San Francisco developer starts at $150,000 a year (Build, pp. 47–48).

Learn to code the way you learned to ride a bicycle; skip courses and bootcamps, and Google each step starting with how to make an HTML page (Build, pp. 48–49). Use the tools you already know; people who argue about the best language, he says, "are not shipping products" (Build, p. 49). Facebook on PHP, Twitter on Ruby, Google on Java, Reddit on Python and Hacker News on Arc show the language does not decide the outcome (Build, p. 50). The author's "light stack" is HTML, CSS and JavaScript in front of PHP and SQLite, MySQL or PostgreSQL; SQLite is said to be "faster than the filesystem"; front-and-back-end frameworks such as Meteor, React and Vue are "too convoluted" for beginners at the time of writing; prefer a technology that has been around "a decade or more" (Build, pp. 50–52). Tool obsession is procrastination, and the right question is "why did you make that?" not what it was made with (Build, pp. 52–53). Learn only what you need now, as with D3.js for one chart (Build, pp. 53–54). Web versus native: web develops faster and updates instantly, native feels faster but needs an install and store review; Uber was a hybrid; a web view inside a big company's native app, opened from a WhatsApp link, is "the real future platform"; and a maker can "ALWAYS go native later" (Build, pp. 54–57). Constraints are advantages: no money, no office, no coding skills, no connections (Build, pp. 57–59). A no-code MVP is built in about 30 minutes from Carrd, Typeform with Stripe, Zapier, Gmail, Google Sheets, Trello and Twilio, for a luggage-pickup startup (Build, pp. 59–67). APIs are explained with Nomad List's own JSON and OpenWeatherMap; a business can be built on other companies' APIs, but sole dependence on one company's API is "a bad place", since they can shut it down or rename a key without telling you (Build, pp. 67–72). The conclusion adds a rule not stated earlier: spend "max. one month" on the MVP, then launch (Build, p. 73).

### Launch (pp. 76–113)

A launch is one of the most important parts of a startup, and most people are "overconfident" about it (Launch, p. 77). Launches are "arbitrary these days" under continuous deployment, so pick a day and make it an event (Launch, p. 78). Before launching, make sure it works: an email signup box "is not a product", and is not to be launched (Launch, p. 78). Walk the new-user flow "countless times", then add an email box that offers something useful and segmented rather than a newsletter, because most sites see "a giant drop" in traffic once the launch ends (Launch, pp. 79–80). Push notifications are "incredibly valuable" if not abused (Launch, p. 80). Analytics: Google Analytics is "good enough" in most cases, Mixpanel is "crazy expensive", Amplitude is mostly free, and Hotjar streams what users see, of which the ethics "might be questionable" (Launch, pp. 80–81). A feedback box (Olark, then Intercom) is "super important"; the author uses it for messages, not on-call chat, and hears within ten minutes when he breaks something, making his users a "continuous testing army" (Launch, pp. 81–82).

Where to launch: Product Hunt, Hacker News and Reddit reach a huge mass of people, against the counter-argument to launch "where your customers are" — pet-food buyers are on pet forums, not Hacker News (Launch, p. 83). Product Hunt, described as founded in 2014 by Ryan Hoover as an email newsletter, brings around 10,000 visitors and 300 concurrent users, converts worse than search traffic, and is "packed with tech journalists", which may add another 50,000 visitors over following weeks (Launch, pp. 83–84). Submit at midnight Pacific, name the product plainly, write a tagline a non-user understands, upload an animated GIF thumbnail and 8–16 zoomed screenshots and a video under 30 seconds, tell your followers without asking for upvotes, and write a humble introduction in the comments (Launch, pp. 85–88). Hacker News is "the hard 'core'", flags posts with more comments than upvotes, discounts voting rings, and is best approached as a Show HN with a personal title; five people upvoting across the first hour "will usually already" reach the front page; it brings around 50,000 to 100,000 visitors and 1,000 concurrent; it dismissed Dropbox (Launch, pp. 88–90). Reddit's front page brings 50,000 to 500,000 visitors and 5,000 to 25,000 concurrent users, so make dynamic pages static with `php index.php > index.html`; pick a relevant subreddit first, then r/internetisbeautiful or r/dataisbeautiful (Launch, pp. 91–94). BetaList, from 2010, costs about $129 to skip a two-month queue and brings 500 to 1,000 visitors (Launch, p. 95).

"There will be hate"; do not fight strangers, fix the little bugs during the launch, and be nice, because people who are listened to "come back with love" (Launch, pp. 95–96). Do not ask everyone to share, and do not buy fake upvotes or followers; the zeitgeist wants the "authentic and organic" (Launch, pp. 96–97). "Content marketing" is "a sleazy term"; the author's 1.5 years of travel blogging built the audience that launched Nomad List, and he suspects text matters less every year (Launch, p. 98). Press brings mainstream users but "increasingly matters less"; control the angle and fact-check the article (Launch, pp. 98–99). To get press, build a personal list of journalists who cover your niche, expect "less than 5%" to write, so five features take about 100 targeted emails; Submit.co is the directory (Launch, pp. 99–101). A bad pitch is long, vague, boastful and carries a legal disclaimer; a good one is two sentences with a link, and a puppy named Robert DeNiro makes it personal (Launch, pp. 101–105). Controversy gets press: Trump is the example of winning "through controversy", and GoFuckingDoIt.com went viral on its name and its rule that the author keeps the money (Launch, pp. 105–106). Lists of tech and mainstream outlets follow, to be taken "with a grain of salt" (Launch, pp. 106–108). Keep launching: every feature is a launch, and "side project marketing" — Buffer's Pablo and Respond, Crew's Unsplash and HowMuchToMakeAnApp.com — earns a fresh launch per mini-app (Launch, pp. 108–109). Motivation is intrinsic when people use it or pay; a product that stops motivating is to be sold or killed; the author works on several products at once, 80% of focus on the main ones (Launch, pp. 109–110). The launch, nowadays, "is perpetual" (Launch, p. 111).

### Grow (pp. 114–130)

Two weeks after launch, traffic drops; growth is "somewhat of a mystical art" the author has managed only a few times (Grow, p. 115). Growth hackers are not to be hired — "99.99% of them are useless" — and growth should have been in the product from the start (Grow, p. 115). Organic growth is preferred because it is free and because it gives "direct feedback" on whether the product is good enough: paid users vanish when the ads stop, which he calls a gigantic problem for funded startups (Grow, pp. 115–116). Paid traffic can be layered on once there is traction, with "100,000+ MAU" as his threshold (Grow, p. 116). Fake users, likes and followers are "the fastest way to failure"; the author tried bought Instagram engagement for Nomad List and got no organic traction from it (Grow, pp. 116–117). New users come from launching again every few months with a version number, from spinning off a strong feature as its own product (Places To Work, from Nomad List's coworking data), and from telling stories to press: each feature as a narrative, the founder's own path as another (Grow, pp. 117–119). "Build in public" — Buffer's open blog, Drew Wilson live-streaming a week of building Plasso — works because people like to be part of a success or failure story, with the clause that it only makes sense if the audience are potential users (Grow, pp. 119–120). Arq Backup's 2017 apology for a version that deleted backups is the transparency example: "show your mistakes" and fix them (Grow, pp. 120–121).

Make sharing easy: social networks are "up to 30%" of web traffic, so fill Open Graph and Twitter Card meta tags on every page, generate a share image per page (Product Hunt uses URL2PNG; a marathon app could render each route on a map and screenshot it), and Gyroscope's shareable fitness maps are the model (Grow, pp. 121–123). Put every interactive state in a human-readable URL, as Nomad List does with `cities-with-fast-internet-safe-for-women-to-travel-alone-near-a-beach` (Grow, pp. 124–125). Launching an API can grow brand awareness, but when Nomad List opened all its public data, six look-alike clones appeared within 90 days, along with App Store apps built on the data with no link back, so the author disabled it; require registration and an API key (Grow, pp. 125–126). Build with your users through the feedback box, because the people who send ideas "are usually the best users", while still building for yourself (Grow, pp. 126–127). Measure against competitors with SimilarWeb, inaccurate below about 10,000 visits a month; a rough rule is that 1,000 visits "usually means $1-$10" if monetized properly (Grow, p. 127). The conclusion: make a great product, because growth "is a multiplier" for one (Grow, pp. 127–128).

### Monetize (pp. 131–170)

Monetizing early is "the best thing" a maker can do, because without revenue a project never becomes a company (Monetize, p. 132). Josh Pigford of Baremetrics is quoted on not "running a charity" (Monetize, p. 133). Makers are "so incredibly scared" to charge; of 1,000 active users, no more than 5% will pay, so a business with 1,000 paying users needs a project with 20,000 potential free users, and a project that cannot reach that scale is best shut down (Monetize, pp. 133–134). Charging brings internet hate, illustrated with a mock Reddit post from "GrumpyCat2019"; the advice is not to lower prices or go free, because the complainers "are not your customers" (Monetize, pp. 134–135). Build with monetization in mind early, especially without cash flow; constraint produces the best work, and being "heavily bound by resources" is called a competitive advantage (Monetize, p. 136). Monetization is validation: Buffer's 2011 "Plans and Pricing" button led to a signup box, and the author's version is a real Buy button and card modal that ends with an apology that the card was not charged, logging intent that a landing page cannot show (Monetize, pp. 137–138).

Business models, in turn: limit features to paid users with an upgrade modal and move them to payment "before they change their mind" (Monetize, p. 138); pay-per-feature, which the author thinks "has lots of potential" on the web (p. 139); ads, of which traditional networks are dead — Google AdSense pays about $1 per 1,000 views, or $500 a month on Nomad List — while native ads sold directly to a fitting company pay "5x to 20x as much", $2,500 to $10,000 a month in his case, and must be labeled "Sponsored" (pp. 139–142); sponsorships at $250 to $2,500 a month from funded companies (p. 142); patronage, from Middle Ages painters to Patreon and Overcast's in-app support that unlocks no features, which "bootstrapped the first year" of Nomad List via a Typeform (pp. 143–145); and subscriptions, tabulated for 1,000 customers at $75 as one-time versus yearly, with 25% growth and 7% churn reaching $1,693,897 in year five against $183,105 one-time (pp. 145–148). Subscriptions are "somewhat of a holy grail" for sellers and easy to value, but from the customer's side they "might not be that great": "forgotten subscriptions" may be up to half of subscription revenue, nobody the author knows asks inactive subscribers whether to renew, and his middle ground is a lifetime membership priced at the predicted lifetime value (Monetize, pp. 148–149). Community is a natural subscription: Nomad List's chat was seeded on Slack in 2014 and its forum on Discourse; a $1 charge to stop spammers rose to $5, $25 and $50, and at ten signups a day it made $500 a day, or $15,000 a month (Monetize, pp. 149–153). Job boards are B2B: Dribbble charges $299 for 30 days and posts about five a day, so $540,000 a year (Monetize, pp. 153–154). Conditional payments charge for failure, as GoFuckingDoIt does (p. 154). Typeform and Basecamp began as agencies that productized a client problem; own the rights to client work (pp. 155–156). Learn from competitors: premium versus low-cost, Android with 80% of the market and Apple with 20% and more of the money (pp. 156–157). Keep experimenting with buy buttons and prices (p. 157). When is monetizing done: $2,000 a month is "maybe $1,200/month" after tax and insurance; how big to get is personal; estimate a competitor's revenue from headcount; widen the market, as Nomad List did to general travelers, or grow it, as Airbnb grew vacation rentals (pp. 157–159).

Payment platforms: Stripe, Braintree and PayPal, always better than a merchant agreement of one's own (Monetize, pp. 159–160). Stripe "revolutionized" online payments at its 2011 launch, Stripe Checkout is the newbie product, subscriptions and proration are handled for you, and cards are tokenized so a breach is useless to hackers; at the time of writing Stripe was available in America, Canada, Western Europe and Singapore (Monetize, pp. 160–163). Braintree is in more countries; PayPal's technology is "highly archaic" but its conversion is high because most of the world does not pay by credit card — Dutch people use what the book calls a debit system named "Cirrus", Latin Americans fear card fraud, Germans use SOFORT — and PayPal locks accounts, so sweep the balance monthly (Monetize, pp. 161–163). Stripe Atlas opens a US company for founders elsewhere (p. 163). Offering both card and PayPal raised the author's conversion about 40%, from roughly $10,000 to $14,000 a month, and he later dropped PayPal because bookkeeping became "so difficult" (Monetize, p. 164). A Typeform connected to Stripe is instant monetization for an MVP (pp. 164–165). Refunds: refusing one is "the worst you can do"; say sorry, refund at once, ask why, and avoid chargebacks, which cost the amount plus a fine of "$35 usually" (Monetize, pp. 165–166). Bookkeeping: above $50,000 a year in revenue, pay up to $5,000 for an accountant who knows Stripe and cross-border sales tax, quiz 25 of them, automate webhooks into a ledger, save every invoice as a dated PDF, and limit money to as few sources as possible (Monetize, pp. 166–167). The chapter closes with the line that an app without monetization "is a charity" and one with it "is a business" (Monetize, p. 168).

### Automate (pp. 171–183)

Once a product is "feature-complete for most users", the goal is to remove the founder "from the equation": "avoid hiring, build robots", because hiring brings training, management and liability, and humans are complex and "relatively slow" (Automate, p. 172). A robot is a task the author used to do himself, scripted and scheduled as a cron job; he has "about 700 to 2,000" running and lists them on his team page (Automate, pp. 172–174). Examples: organizing Nomad List meetups from Foursquare data, fetching weather and air quality for 1,000 cities hourly, patching the server, texting him when CPU or disk misbehave, scraping Tinder for popular cities, parsing 100-plus job boards for Remote OK, screenshotting city pages for share images, charging GoFuckingDoIt cards after a deadline, and downloading his phone bill into his bookkeeping (Automate, pp. 173–174). Cronitor watches whether cron jobs ran; Zapier workflows count as robots too (Automate, p. 175). Do not automate what is "not worth" automating: the automation must not take longer to write than the time it saves (p. 175). Robots ask humans for help through checks — a script that loads a page and emails if a keyword is missing, or UptimeRobot alerting when Amsterdam vanishes from a filtered list (pp. 175–176). Humans cover what cannot be scripted: most support is self-serve — signing up, canceling, refunding, changing plan or card — and the edge cases go to a contractor paid $2,500 a month or a standby developer at $50 an hour and up who invoices through PayPal; where revenue is the goal, "a human equals a robot" (pp. 176–178). Passive income "is a myth"; three years of hard work followed by three of automation is "compressed income" (p. 178). DAOs on Ethereum are named as the philosophical endpoint (p. 178). The "bus test" is passed with a set-up of robot scripts, two contract sysadmins, alerts, one contractor with bank access as "the executive", one or two people as "the board", and prepaid hosting and domains (Automate, pp. 178–180).

### Exit (pp. 184–215)

The author received "tens of offers" in four years; one reached due diligence at "millions of dollars" and took six months before he rejected it over an earn-out (Exit, p. 185). Bootstrapped companies do sell, as Plenty of Fish did for $575 million in cash (Exit, p. 186). About one inquiry in a hundred "actually goes through" (p. 188). Buyer types: the "deadbeat" offering 100 to 1,000 times less than market value, who make up 90–99% of messages; the "competitive snooper", who runs due diligence for intelligence and backs out, countered by a breakup fee, normally 1–3% but the author would ask 10%, as AT&T paid $4 billion on a $39 billion deal; the "string-you-alonger", handled by refusing calls; the serious formal buyer with NDAs; and the serious informal buyer, "a tiny percentage" and the one to wait for (Exit, pp. 188–191). Brokers such as FE International and Empire Flippers charge "10-15% of the selling price" plus a buyer fee and have "a short-term mindset" (pp. 191–192). Bargaining power comes from not needing to sell; let the buyer bid first, or if you open, open "aggressively high", ten times revenue in his example, supported by an Adam Galinsky passage on first offers (pp. 192–195). Valuation: a revenue multiple by growth rate, from a Tomasz Tunguz chart, worked as $100,000 revenue at 33% growth ≈ 7x ≈ $700,000, then compared with four more years of cash flow and a $2.19 million future value, so a sale trades high-risk cash flow for "low-risk, low reward" cash (pp. 195–197). Seller's discretionary earnings can make a DIY business "a net negative business" and, for the seller, mostly reduce negotiating power (pp. 197–198). Industry comparables work like house prices (p. 198). Negotiate cash over stock, especially private stock; expect tax; "earnouts are bullshit" because the new owner can sabotage the target; smaller sales go by asset purchase agreement; keep the non-compete narrow; indemnification "is a difficult part of negotiation" needing a lawyer (pp. 199–202). A letter of intent lists price, earn-out, structure, due-diligence timeline, non-compete and indemnification (p. 202). Prepare: a holding company may lower tax; "Delaware, Singapore and the UK" are the attractive jurisdictions; check GPL dependencies with a license checker; tidy the bookkeeping (pp. 202–204). Due diligence is a financial and technical audit; escrow and holdback escrow protect both sides (pp. 204–205). Post-sale: celebrate, because rituals work; expect emotional fallout, as Markus Persson tweeted after the $2.5 billion Minecraft sale; see a psychologist, Talkspace named; FIRE and the Trinity study's 4% rule turn $1 million into "$40,000/year, or $3,333/month" forever (pp. 205–209). Careers after: stay on with vesting or an earn-out, which the author would minimize in favor of cash; angel investing, which "generally [has] a negative return"; another company, with a Gompers et al. quote giving a 30% success rate for previously successful founders against 18% for first-timers; or volunteer work through Catchafire, Fast Forward, Hashtag Charity or the Peace Corps (pp. 209–214). The best time to sell, he closes, is when you "don't want to sell" (Exit, p. 215).

### Epilogue (pp. 216–221)

The seven paragraphs of the TL;DR are repeated verbatim (Epilogue, pp. 217–218). The farewell's message is "take action", the biggest competitive advantage because most people do not, and "listen to your intuition": the ideas in the book are by definition outdated, so take inspiration and "do your own thing" (Epilogue, pp. 218–220).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **The whole book.** There is "strong survivorship bias" at work, because the people who fail do not write books (Foreword, p. 11); books are "outdated by definition" and the author may already have changed his mind (Foreword, p. 8); the book is "just ideas" that may be wrong and biased (Foreword, p. 12). The Epilogue repeats that following the book will not make the reader successful and that its ideas are already outdated (Epilogue, p. 219).
- **Solving your own problems.** The privilege critique is "somewhat valid": a wealthy Western man will solve his own comfortable problems and not those of women or of a developing country; the author's answer is to widen access so everyone can solve their own (Idea, p. 24). Collaboration can work for idea generation too; the objection is to groupthink, not to talking to customers and users (Idea, p. 32).
- **Fast and minimal.** Apple ships without user testing and that is fine for Apple (Build, p. 38). Perfectionism is "necessary in smaller details", only not in the whole (Build, p. 40). There is "a limit to this": no bad products, and "minimum needs to be minimum good" (Build, p. 41).
- **Build it yourself.** Doing everything yourself does not work in the long term; once a model is proven it is smart to repeat it with people working for you (Build, p. 42). Among VC-funded companies there are "lots of exceptions" where it went right (Build, p. 43). Venture capital "has its function and place" (Build, p. 43), and a founder with "a big money pile" may as well hire builders (Build, p. 47). The sustainable script itself ends with borrowing or an angel or VC round to fund expansion once the model is proven (Build, p. 45).
- **Tools.** This "isn't a technical book" and the tools do not really matter (Build, p. 39). The advice is not to learn PHP but to use what one already knows (Build, p. 51). Merged front-and-back-end frameworks are "too convoluted" only at the time of writing, and the author guesses front and back end will merge eventually (Build, p. 51). Every no-code tool named is "subject to change", and only "the general concept remains" (Build, p. 59).
- **Web first.** Others will say the platform should follow how users will use the app, and the author calls that "a good point"; and no platform choice "has to be permanent" (Build, p. 57). Whether web and native converge is "impossible to predict" (Build, p. 56).
- **Where to launch.** Launching where the customers are is "a great point"; for a pet-food service the tech platforms may be wrong, and it "really depends" (Launch, p. 83). Product Hunt converts worse than search traffic and its comments are "not radically honest" (Launch, p. 84). Hacker News is "notoriously hateful" and often wrong, as with Dropbox (Launch, p. 90). A post that fails the first time is usually "not for Hacker News" (Launch, p. 90).
- **Asking for votes.** Asking "a few friends" for a head start is distinguished from asking everyone (Launch, p. 96), and the Hacker News advice is exactly that: five people over the first hour (Launch, p. 89).
- **Press.** Press "increasingly matters less"; it may kickstart traction and is needed less once traction exists (Launch, p. 99). The outlet lists will be "outdated quickly" (Launch, pp. 106–107). Controversy is offered with the caveat that a swear word mostly just looks "cheap and cheesy" (Launch, p. 106).
- **Organic growth.** Paid and organic can be combined and segmented, which is "probably smart" (Grow, p. 116), and paid traffic is endorsed once there is traction, at "100,000+ MAU" (Grow, p. 116). Building in public has "a clause": the audience must be potential users (Grow, p. 120). Growth techniques still apply; the point is that they multiply a good product (Grow, p. 128).
- **An API.** Sharing data through an API carries "big risks", and the author disabled his own (Grow, p. 126).
- **Building with users.** The author is "not saying" to build for other people; users offer perspective on one's own problem space (Grow, p. 127). Traffic is "a very limiting metric"; a competitor with more visits may make less money (Grow, p. 127).
- **Monetization.** Building with monetization in mind "may limit your creativity"; with cash flow and runway a maker can experiment more, which can lead to "more interesting projects" (Monetize, p. 136). Ads are not dead in native form, and a native ad must still be labeled "Sponsored" or "Ad" (Monetize, pp. 139–142). Patronage "has its time and place", and when it fits is for the reader to judge (Monetize, p. 145). Subscriptions "might not be that great" for the customer, switching to them may bring "a drop in sales", and the author himself is tired of signing up for more (Monetize, p. 149). In most markets there is "room for both" a low-cost and a premium product (Monetize, p. 157). How far to go "depends heavily on your objectives" and on time taken from family and friends (Monetize, pp. 157–158).
- **Payment platforms.** Stripe "may or may not be available" where you live (Monetize, p. 161); PayPal's buyer protection "usually favors buyers" (p. 162); offering PayPal raised conversion but made bookkeeping "so difficult" that the author dropped it (p. 164); local processors are more complicated but sometimes the only choice (p. 163).
- **Refunds.** The customer is not always right, but it is "important to listen" (Monetize, p. 165).
- **Bookkeeping.** Perfectly accurate books are hard, which is what the accountant is for (Monetize, p. 167); bookkeeping is not a place to "save money" (p. 166).
- **Automation.** Do not automate what is "not worth" it; the famous trope is the script that takes longer than the task (Automate, p. 175). Some things the author "really couldn't automate away" and need a human on standby (Automate, p. 176). Passive income "is a myth"; monthly situations will always need a human (Automate, p. 178). Going fully decentralized is hard and the author does not know how yet (Automate, p. 178).
- **Exit.** The chapter is the only part the author "didn't complete" himself (Exit, p. 185). The buyer-odds figures are "rough guesstimates" (Exit, p. 191). Whether to use a broker is "up to you" (Exit, p. 192). On negotiation tactics, the author does not know which is right (Exit, p. 192). Opinions on multiples "vary broadly", the Tunguz chart is "one perspective" (Exit, p. 196), and the sale arithmetic is "highly simplified" and "a very optimistic scenario" (Exit, p. 197). The author is "not an accountant" and sends tax to one (Exit, p. 199); structuring depends on the country and needs "a fiscal advisor" (Exit, p. 203); indemnification needs a lawyer (Exit, p. 202). Selling is not necessary; "life-long founders" exist (Exit, p. 214), and starting another company may be "addiction to work" — the author's own phrase for a business book advising against a business (Exit, p. 213).

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Survivorship bias, named and then unaddressed.** The author warns twice that his perspective is probably skewed because those who fail do not write books (Foreword, pp. 8, 11), and the book then argues almost entirely from his own successes: Nomad List, Remote OK, Hoodmaps, GoFuckingDoIt. The failures he says outnumber them — most of his other projects "failed (some miserably)" (Foreword, p. 11) — are not examined, so the reader cannot see what the method looked like when it did not work.
- **Figures without a source.** The platform traffic numbers (Launch, pp. 84, 90, 91, 95), the 5% of active users who pay (Monetize, p. 133), social networks at up to 30% of web traffic (Grow, p. 121), 1,000 visits meaning $1–$10 (Grow, p. 127), the 5x–20x native-ad premium (Monetize, p. 141), forgotten subscriptions at up to 50% (Monetize, p. 149), the under-5% journalist response rate (Launch, p. 100), and the 1-in-100 serious buyer (Exit, p. 188) are stated as fact or rule of thumb with no citation; where the author does flag one as a guess, as with the buyer odds (Exit, p. 191), the flag is the exception.
- **Dated or wrong facts.** Gmail's beta lasted five years, not a decade (Build, p. 40); Product Hunt began in 2013, not 2014 (Launch, p. 83); Cirrus is a Mastercard ATM network, not a Dutch debit system (Monetize, p. 162); Stripe's dispute fee is $15, not $35 (Monetize, p. 165); Android's share is nearer 72% than 80% (Monetize, p. 157); Stripe's country list is far longer than the four regions named (Monetize, p. 163). Section 7 has the later evidence for each. The Facebook origin is given as a "Hot Or Not" app by "Marc Zuckerberg" (Idea, p. 29), a misspelling and a loose description of Facemash.
- **Correlation presented as cause.** Nomad List's price rises are credited with sign-ups returning to the same level after a week (Monetize, p. 152) with no control; GoFuckingDoIt's press is credited to its name and its rule (Launch, p. 106); Buffer's side projects are credited with "a crazy amount of press" with no number (Launch, p. 109); adding PayPal is credited with a 40% conversion lift (Monetize, p. 164) with no mention of what else changed. Each is one product, once.
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** The whole subscription argument is a spreadsheet of an imagined business at 1,000 customers, 25% growth and 7% churn (Monetize, pp. 146–148); the author's own products are one-time and lifetime memberships, and he reports being "a bit tired" of subscriptions himself (Monetize, p. 149). The valuation worked example applies a 2016 venture-scale SaaS multiple chart to a $100,000 business (Exit, pp. 196–197). The Galinsky passage is about negotiation in general and is applied to selling a company (Exit, pp. 194–195). The Exit chapter as a whole rests on one rejected offer (Exit, p. 185).
- **Internal tension.** The book says never to ask people to upvote (Launch, pp. 87, 96) and also that five friends upvoting over the first hour is what gets a Hacker News post to the front page (Launch, p. 89), a practice the HN FAQ forbids. It says to stop reading books (Idea, p. 24) and that text matters less every year (Launch, p. 98), in a 220-page book. It says growth should have been in the product "from the start" (Grow, p. 115) and then lists a chapter of growth techniques. It says "a human equals a robot" as a module in a business (Automate, p. 177) and, in the same chapter, that the point of automation is a life with friends and family (Automate, p. 180). It advises against building on one company's API (Build, p. 72) after building the no-code MVP entirely on Carrd, Typeform, Zapier, Stripe and Twilio (Build, pp. 64–67).
- **Examples that no longer hold.** Boosted Boards is defunct (Idea, p. 22); Plasso's domain does not resolve (Grow, p. 119); Overcast now sells a subscription and shows ads rather than asking for patronage (Monetize, p. 144); Dribbble's job board is free with a paid tier (Monetize, p. 154); Nomad List itself is now free (Monetize, p. 152; section 7). The tool lists at each chapter's end are, as the author says, already outdated (Build, p. 59; Launch, p. 106).
- **A model with no room for the seller's own time.** The seller's-discretionary-earnings section shows a DIY business can be "a net negative business" once the founder's labor is priced, and the author's response is that the metric mostly "reduces the negotiating power" of the seller (Exit, p. 198) rather than that it might be true; the same omission runs through the $83,000-a-month and $15,000-a-month figures, which are revenue, not the founder's earnings after his own hours.
- **Controversy as a method, weakly bounded.** Trump is offered as a marketing example on the ground that it is "impossible to deny" it worked (Launch, p. 105), and the only limit given is taste, that a swear word "just looks cheap and cheesy" (Launch, p. 106); the Ethics paragraph that forbids manipulating users (TL;DR, p. 4) is not brought to bear on it.
