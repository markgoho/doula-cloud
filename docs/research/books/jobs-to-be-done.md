## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that innovation fails because companies do not agree on what a customer need is, and that Jobs-to-be-Done Theory fixes this by supplying a framework for "categorizing, defining, capturing, and organizing" every need a customer has and by tying customer-defined performance metrics, "desired outcome statements", to the job the customer is trying to get done (ch. 2, p. 48; Introduction, pp. 26–27). Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) is the process that puts the theory into practice: with a complete set of needs and quantitative data on which are unmet, a company can "predict which new concepts and offerings will win", and innovation goes from "a game of chance" to "a science" (ch. 2, pp. 48, 52). The evidence offered for the whole claim is one study: traditional innovation processes succeed 17% of the time on average across twelve published sources, and ODI 86% of the time across 21 client launches, so ODI is "five times more predictable" (Introduction, pp. 21–24). The book's own summary line is "ODI replaces luck with a predictable process" (Introduction, p. 29). The commercial law under it is that new products win when they get a job done "better (faster, more predictably, with higher output) and/or more cheaply", and that customers switch when a product gets the job done about 20% better (ch. 3, pp. 62–63; ch. 2, pp. 48–49).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not the "ideas-first" approach of brainstorming, fail-fast, and idea filtering, which he calls "inherently flawed" and "doomed to failure" (ch. 1, pp. 31, 35–36). It is not voice-of-the-customer in "the customer's own words", which "will more often than not result in the wrong inputs" (ch. 1, p. 42). It is not a hunt for latent needs: "let's not assume customers have latent needs" (ch. 1, p. 45). It is not asking the customer for solutions: "Coming up with the winning solution is not the customer's responsibility. It is the responsibility of the company" (ch. 1, p. 38). It is not the question "What job did you hire that product to do?", which he calls "a common mistake" and "indicative of a product-centric mindset" (ch. 4 §II, p. 87). A job is not a situation the customer is in, and not a "job story" that crams everything into one statement (ch. 4 §II, pp. 89–90). A job map is "not a customer journey or customer experience map" (ch. 4 §III, p. 91). And it is not qualitative research alone: finding segments "requires statistically valid market research, not just observation or other qualitative research methods" (ch. 2, p. 52).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Foreword (Alex Osterwalder, pp. 11–13)

Osterwalder opens with a Simon-Kucher & Partners finding that "72% of all new product & service introductions fail to live up to expectations" and frames the book as part of the toolbox he says a company needs to avoid being disrupted (Foreword, p. 11). He credits his own Value Proposition Canvas to reading Christensen's *The Innovator's Solution* (2003) and Ulwick's 2008 *Harvard Business Review* article "The Customer-Centered Innovation Map", then discovering Strategyn's ODI process (Foreword, p. 12).

### Introduction: The Failure That Led to Success (pp. 14–29)

The author's origin story is the IBM PCjr of 1984, which the *Wall Street Journal* called "a flop" the day after launch and which "cost IBM over a billion dollars"; his lesson was that if the team had known the metrics customers would judge the product by, it could have designed to them (Introduction, pp. 14–15). Five years of trying voice of the customer, QFD, TRIZ, Six Sigma, and conjoint analysis led to a 1990 idea in North Sydney: apply Six Sigma process control to the process the customer executes rather than to the product (pp. 15–16). He founded The Total Quality Group in October 1991 with a method called CD-MAP; the first client, Cordis, yielded 75 desired outcome statements from interventional cardiologists, 19 new products by mid-1993 that were all "number 1 or 2 in the market", market share from 1% to more than 20%, and a stock price that "more than quadrupled" (pp. 16–17). The company became Strategyn in 1999, the year of the first of twelve patents on the process (p. 18). Christensen met him in late 1999, was "kind enough to cite Strategyn and me as originators of these practices" in *The Innovator's Solution*, and introduced Mark Johnson and Matt Eyring; an offer to join Innosight as a partner in 2004 was declined (pp. 18–19). HBR published "Turn Customer Input into Innovation" in 2002 and McGraw-Hill published *What Customers Want* in 2005 (pp. 19–20).

The evidence chapter follows. Christensen's September 2016 HBR line that innovation "can be far more predictable" is quoted, and Strategyn's own study is offered as the data behind it: "a Harvard Business School trained independent researcher" found traditional success rates averaging 17% across twelve sources and ODI at 86% (pp. 20–21). The twelve sources are tabulated on p. 23, from Frost & Sullivan's 0.3% and 1% to Cooper's 67% and Griffin's 59%, with the average printed as 17% and Strategyn as 86%; Frost & Sullivan's "only one in 300 new products significantly impacts a company's growth" and PwC's 11% of venture investments reaching liquidity are pulled out as bullets (pp. 22–23). On the ODI side, the researcher interviewed 43 Strategyn clients, anonymously and without incentives; each chose one of four success metrics (revenue, market share, customer satisfaction, or ROI); of 21 projects that produced launches, 18 were rated successes by the sponsoring company, five won industry awards, and the three failures were attributed by the sponsors to "a poor job of executing the commercialization" (pp. 23–24). The rest of the Introduction lays out the Theory, Process, Practice structure and the ten questions the author says an innovation process must answer, from "Who is the customer?" to "What new products must we create?" (pp. 26–29).

### Chapter 1: Why Do Innovation Projects Fail? (pp. 31–46)

Two approaches exist, ideas-first and needs-first; the first is "inherently flawed" and the second "often flawed in its execution" (ch. 1, p. 31). Ideas-first is entrenched: about 68% of large businesses use gated development, whose first step is idea generation, citing Cooper's *Winning at New Products* (2001), and Skarzynski and Gibson, Teresa Amabile, Tom Peters, Thomas Watson, and *The Innovator's Guide to Growth* are quoted for "numbers game" and "fail fast" thinking (pp. 33–35). Three reasons it fails: a customer has 50 to 150 needs of which 5–80% may be unmet, so the chance of an idea hitting all of them blind is "close to zero", supported by a footnote computing three competing ideas for each of 15 unmet needs as 3 to the power of 15, "14 million ideas" (pp. 35–36); evaluation and filtering are done without knowing the needs, so success rates stall at 10–20% (pp. 36–37); and customers "cannot articulate the solutions they want", which is the company's job, not theirs (p. 38). Needs-first has Levitt's 1960 "Marketing Myopia" and Garvin's finding that understanding user needs is the "primary discriminator" of success behind it (p. 40), yet companies "nearly always fail to uncover all or even most" needs because "a common language for communicating a need does not exist": in Strategyn's own research, "95% of managers say there is internal disagreement on what a need is" (pp. 41–42). Griffin and Hauser's 1993 definition of a need as a benefit "in the customer's own words" is rejected (p. 42); IDEO's anthropologists seeking "Vuja De" make innovation "more of an art than a science", and Gerry Katz's view that outcomes and jobs are no different from wants, needs, and requirements is quoted and denied (pp. 43–44). The widely held belief in latent needs, backed by Hamel and Prahalad, the PDMA glossary, and A. G. Lafley, is called "far from the truth" (pp. 44–45).

### Chapter 2: Jobs-to-be-Done Needs Framework (pp. 47–61)

Levitt's quarter-inch hole and Christensen's "People buy products and services to get a job done" are "only the tip of the iceberg"; the "game-changing implication" is a framework for all of a customer's needs plus metrics tied to the job (ch. 2, pp. 47–48). Products that get the job done "20% better or more are very likely to win" (pp. 48–49). The framework has six need types: the core functional job, desired outcomes on it, related jobs, emotional and social jobs, consumption chain jobs, and the buyer's financial outcomes (p. 49; figure, p. 50). A market's customers collectively have "well over 100 needs", and 200 or more in health care and social media (p. 49). Need statements are "mutually exclusive" and the set "collectively exhaustive" (p. 51). The Bosch circular saw team, knowing a segment's 14 unmet needs, conceptualized a solution "in just 3 hours" (p. 52). Markets are not homogeneous, segments need "statistically valid market research", and personas built on demographic and psychographic data "are highly misleading as they usually create phantom targets" (p. 52). The core functional job is one statement such as *cut a piece of wood in a straight line* or *pass on life lessons to children*, defined first, with every other need relative to it (pp. 53–54); market selection means picking job executors and finding their most important, least satisfied jobs (p. 54); in platform markets such as banking and social media the core jobs are found with factor analysis (p. 55). A job is stable across technologies (record players to streaming), has no geographical boundaries, and is solution-agnostic (pp. 55–56). Desired outcomes are the metrics customers use at each job step, 50 to 150 of them, structured to be "measurable, controllable, actionable, devoid of solutions, and stable over time" (pp. 57–58). Related jobs, 5 to 20, explain the wireless presenter's value over the telescopic pointer (p. 58). Emotional jobs are how customers want to feel, social jobs how they want to be perceived, 5 to 25 of them, and they inform value proposition, marketing, and positioning (p. 59). Consumption chain jobs cover receiving, installing, learning, cleaning, repairing, disposing; Dyson's bagless system and non-iron shirts are the examples, and the purchase process itself is one such job (pp. 59–60). The buyer weighs 40 to 80 financial outcomes; where buyer and user are one person, the book has them interviewed "wearing the 'buyer's hat'" (p. 61).

### Chapter 3: The Jobs-to-be-Done Growth Strategy Matrix (pp. 62–79)

The observed law: products win when they get a job done better and/or more cheaply (ch. 3, pp. 62–63). A two-by-two of better/worse against more/less expensive gives five conclusions: better and pricier wins only underserved customers; better and cheaper wins everyone; worse and cheaper wins overserved customers and nonconsumers; worse and pricier wins only customers with no alternatives; and "stuck in the middle", after Porter, wins nobody new but may hold an incumbent's customers (pp. 63–65; figures, pp. 64, 66–67). The five named strategies with their examples: differentiated (Nest, Nespresso, the iPhone 2G, the Aeron chair, Whole Foods, Emirates, Bang & Olufsen, BMW, the original PlayStation, Dyson), dominant (Google Search, Google AdWords, UberX, Netflix streaming, Progressive's nonstandard auto insurance, Vanguard), disruptive (Google Docs, TurboTax, Dollar Shave Club, eTrade, Coursera), discrete (airport drinks past security, stadium concessions, check cashing and payday lending, remote ATMs), and sustaining (pp. 67–69). Examples are sourced at the product level, so Uber runs three strategies at once, UberBLACK differentiated and UberPOOL disruptive (p. 70). The matrix only works once a company knows whether its segments are underserved or overserved; a differentiated play fails in an overserved segment and a disruptive one in an underserved segment (p. 71). Differentiated: Nest sold at "seven times the price" of incumbents, $250 against $35, took under 10% share and an estimated 25%-plus profit share, and a company can move down-market later as Apple did with iPhone generations and Uber with UberX (pp. 72–73). Dominant: "always the most appealing approach for a new market entrant", winnable with a product at least 20% better and 20% cheaper; Netflix against Blockbuster and Kroll Ontrack in e-discovery are the cases (pp. 73–74). Disruptive: Christensen "was correct"; it serves overserved customers and nonconsumers for different reasons, and "the process of disruption" is a series of products starting worse-and-cheaper (pp. 74–76). Discrete: check cashing, StubHub, airport water, and surge-priced airline seats, with Mylan's 2016 EpiPen backlash as the reputational cost (pp. 77–78). Sustaining: under 5% better or cheaper, "New market entrants should avoid" it, customers switch at "upwards of 20% better", and it suits incumbents holding share (pp. 78–79).

### Chapter 4: Outcome-Driven Innovation (pp. 81–123)

ODI "rids the innovation process of its deficiencies", has an 86% success rate, and runs in ten steps from defining the customer to a product strategy (ch. 4, pp. 81–83). **I. Define the customer** (pp. 83–86): managers answer "We have many customers"; the book reduces them to three job executors, the core job executor who supplies functional outcomes (the surgeon), the product lifecycle support team who supply consumption chain outcomes, and the purchase decision maker who supplies financial outcomes (the operating-room manager); a product with nothing to install or set up "is far more valuable than one that does" (pp. 83–86). **II. Define the job** (pp. 86–91): too narrow limits growth (a kettle maker hearing "boil water" when the job is *prepare a hot beverage*, which Keurig then took), too broad is unactionable, and the test is whether the company "can and will" address the job end to end (pp. 87–88); take the customer's perspective, growers *prevent weeds from impacting crop yields* rather than *kill weeds* (p. 89); keep statements one-dimensional, never a "job story", with 100 or more separate statements (pp. 89–90); leave emotion and outcomes out of the functional job (p. 90); define the job, not the situation, so a boring commute is not a job but *get breakfast while commuting to work* is (pp. 90–91); the format is verb + object + contextual clarifier (p. 91). **III. Uncover desired outcomes** (pp. 91–95): a job map shows what the customer is trying to get done, not what they are doing, and is "not a customer journey or customer experience map"; consumption chain jobs are captured separately (pp. 91–92); the completed map "lays out the long-term strategy", a single platform that gets the entire job done (p. 92); the universal job map has eight steps, define, locate, prepare, confirm, execute, monitor, modify, conclude (p. 93; figure); 50 to 150 outcomes per job, each statement built as direction of improvement + performance metric + object of control + contextual clarifier, per the 2008 *MIT Sloan Management Review* article (pp. 93–95); any interviewing method works, and outcomes "don't change over time" so the set "is an important company asset for years" (p. 95). **IV. Find segments** (pp. 95–100): personas and conjoint both "fail to distinguish between customers with different unmet needs"; "we have proven" that demographics and psychographics "will nearly always fail" to explain unmet needs, the 28-year-old Montanan and the 55-year-old Floridian sharing bad internet (p. 96); segments arise from added complexity, Bosch's finish carpenters and drivers with many stops (p. 97); the method is outcomes, then a survey of "usually between 180 and 3,000 customers", then factor and cluster analysis, then profiling questions (p. 98); a product fails without a segment "large enough to warrant the investment" (p. 99). **V. Define the value proposition** (pp. 100–102): Coloplast found 10 of a nurse segment's top 15 unmet outcomes were about the wound not getting worse, went to market with "We prevent complications", and saw double-digit growth without changing product or price; Cordis gained 3 share points in 1992 and Arm & Hammer's Animal Nutrition division over 30% revenue growth in 2014 the same way (pp. 100–102). **VI. Competitive analysis** (pp. 103–106): comparing feature sets, "speeds and feeds", is "a waste of time"; customers rate competing products against the outcome set instead; Bosch's table (dummy data, p. 104) shows importance, satisfaction, opportunity, and DeWalt and Makita satisfaction for eight of 75 outcomes rated by 270 tradesmen; "table stakes" are outcomes both very important and very satisfied; 14 outcomes scored above 10 (pp. 103–106). **VII. Innovation strategy** (pp. 107–108): Bosch's 2x4-cutting segment was overserved, its finish-cut segment underserved with 14 unmet outcomes; a disruptive saw and a laser-based saw were rejected and a dominant strategy chosen, producing the CS20, "the company's best-selling circular saw in North America for over 10 years" (pp. 107–108). **VIII. Target hidden opportunities** (pp. 109–111): opportunity score = importance + max(importance − satisfaction, 0), with 200 of 270 rating importance 4 or 5 (7.4) and 75 of 270 rating satisfaction 4 or 5 (2.8) giving 12.0; 10 or greater means underserved; the Opportunity Landscape plots underserved, appropriately served, and overserved, with overserved outcomes as cost-reduction targets (pp. 109–111). **IX. Market strategy** (pp. 112–116): six steps, target offerings at segments (an industrial pump maker and cavitation), message un-messaged strengths (Cordis from 1.5% to 5% share in six months), an outcome-based value proposition (Coloplast), digital marketing on the keywords of job steps and outcomes since buyers "rarely start by entering the product name", a 5–10 question survey to assign leads to segments, and sales tools (Arm & Hammer's 30% year-on-year growth "without changing its product or pricing") (pp. 112–116). **X. Product strategy** (pp. 117–123): seven actions, borrow features (Microsoft packaging internal tools), accelerate the pipeline (Cordis's stent, one of about 40 initiatives, first to market and "$1 billion dollars in revenue"), partner or license (an automaker screening over 100 partners), acquire (Vi-COR), devise features ("Companies do not lack ideas... What they need is insight into the customer's underserved outcomes"), add services (AMO), and conceptualize the ultimate single-platform solution (an agricultural company that watched a competitor buy it first) (pp. 117–123).

### Chapter 5: Case Studies (pp. 124–161)

Six Strategyn engagements. **Microsoft** Software Assurance: renewals were falling; two surveys of about 100 procurement managers and 300 IT professionals on roughly 75 and 100-plus outcomes revealed the offering touched "one tiny piece of their job"; Microsoft packaged tools it already used internally, a licensing server, a conflict checker, a training voucher program, and access-restriction templates, and beat its revenue goal by over 10% the year the changes were announced (ch. 5, pp. 124–129). **Kroll Ontrack**: lawyers "didn't know what you were talking about" when asked for features in the new e-discovery market; about 100 outcomes, Harvester and imaging tools for the top two, a custom-coding feature, an acquired clustering search, ESI Consulting, a dominant strategy, and revenue "from $11 million to over $200 million in about 6 years" (pp. 129–134). **Arm & Hammer** Animal Nutrition, as an interview with director Scott Druker: the customer turned out to be the dairy producer, not the nutritionist the company had been asking; the job was "optimizing herd productivity", not nutrition; over 165 outcomes; large and small dairies shared unmet needs and "geography wasn't necessarily the determinant"; the biggest revenue impact was changing the messaging from milk and fat production, which none of the 165 outcomes named, to outcomes; results came "almost immediately", "The dairy economy helped", revenue grew more than 30%, and the ODI work was the template for acquiring Vi-COR in January 2015 (pp. 134–145). **Bosch**: entering the North American circular saw market against DeWalt; roofers, framers, and finish carpenters interviewed; about 85 outcomes rated by about 270 tradesmen; on average "there were no unmet needs", so segmentation was required; a finish-carpenter segment over 30% of users had 14 unmet outcomes; the DirectConnect cord and a line-of-sight cutout answered them at no added cost; concept testing projected satisfaction from 63% to 87%; Lowe's and Home Depot asked for a two-month delay to build stock; Popular Science named the CS20 a top 100 innovation (pp. 145–150). **Abbott Medical Optics**: materials managers replenishing lenses for cataract surgery; about 100 outcomes, 200 surveyed, a segment with about 50 underserved; the fix was dedicated advocates and regional "pods" plus a schedule-planning and inventory module; NPS up nearly 10%, loyalty index up 14 points, the NorthFace award two years later (pp. 150–155). **Hussmann**: four years of LED products nobody bought; 1,500 shoppers, 200 store merchandisers, and 50 executive merchandisers prioritized over 300 outcomes; eight underserved lighting outcomes (uniformity, true product color) became the EcoShine line; share went "from a fraction of a digit to double digits" in a year and revenue from thousands to tens of millions, at lower cost and with faster engineering (pp. 155–161).

### Chapter 6: Becoming an ODI Practitioner (pp. 163–176)

Written for the internal change agent the author was at IBM; the process, tools, and templates are "embedded in our online platform at Strategyn.com/ODIpro" with mentoring available (ch. 6, pp. 163–164). The practitioner runs six phases in 84 steps: initiate the project (15 steps, including screeners, recruiting, job-map interviews, and in complex markets a quantitative study to validate the jobs), uncover needs (18 steps, from outcome-gathering interviews to the buyer's financial outcomes and the factors that make some customers struggle more), gather quantitative data (18 steps, including profiling and willingness-to-pay questions, vendor selection, translation, fielding, and cleaning), discover opportunities (10 steps of segmentation models, profiles, competitor strengths, and willingness-to-pay drivers), formulate the market strategy (12 steps through value propositions, messaging, a digital marketing proposal, a customer-acquisition tool that assigns segments, and sales training), and formulate the product strategy (10 steps of weaknesses, targets, facilitated ideation, and portfolio deliverables) (pp. 166–175). The qualifications asked for include a process orientation, qualitative and quantitative research experience, Six Sigma training, and "Strong knowledge of PowerPoint, Excel, and Word" (pp. 175–176).

### Chapter 7: Transforming the Organization (pp. 177–186)

Adoption needs "one trained internal ODI Practitioner per business unit", certifiable online "over the course of a few weeks", with a narrowly scoped "project sprint" done in as little as four weeks; the cross-functional team should take a course but need not certify (ch. 7, p. 177). "In a small organization with only 1 product, we recommend hiring Strategyn to gather the qualitative and quantitative data"; large ones build an Innovation Center of Excellence, ideally on an existing Six Sigma program (pp. 177–178). Three phases: a two-day workshop that leaves the team agreed on who the customer is, the job, the job map, and what a need is (pp. 179–182; the before-and-after table on p. 181 moves the team from "The team believes customers have latent needs" to "customers can articulate their needs when they are defined around the job-to-be-done"); a survey of "usually between 180 and 3000 people" and the analyses (pp. 182–183); and training on using the data for digital marketing, sales messaging, repositioning, product improvement, R&D, and M&A (pp. 184–185). A team "can expect to complete its three-phase outcome-driven journey in as little as four weeks" (p. 186).

### Chapter 8: The Language of Jobs-to-be-Done (pp. 187–196)

A glossary, offered because "A common language of innovation has the power to unite an organization" (ch. 8, p. 187). Entries that carry the book's definitions: a customer need is a functional or emotional job or a desired outcome (p. 188); a market is "A group of people + the core functional job they are trying to get done" (p. 191); a market segment is a group with a unique set of underserved or overserved outcomes, often because they execute the job in a unique circumstance (p. 192); an opportunity is an unmet need, underserved or overserved (p. 193); the Opportunity Algorithm's importance and satisfaction are the percentages rating an outcome very or extremely important or satisfied (p. 193); ODI "has an 86 percent success rate—a five-fold improvement over the industry average" (p. 194); and a segment description, "synonymous with the term outcome-based persona", is the situation, the functional and emotional jobs, and the underserved and overserved outcomes (pp. 195–196).

### Chapter 9: Learn More (pp. 197–203)

A timeline from Levitt's 1962 quarter-inch hole through the PCjr (1984), the 1990 epiphany, Cordis (1992–1994), the first patent filing (1996), Motorola's TalkAbout radio and a division growing "from 0% to 18%" (1997), the Christensen meetings (1999), the 2002 HBR article named one of the year's best business ideas, *The Innovator's Solution* (2003), the 2004 case studies, *What Customers Want* (2005), the 2008 HBR and MIT Sloan articles, the 2010 track record study, J&J Ethicon's harmonic blade and its Edison Gold Award (2010), the 250th engagement (2013), the twelfth patent (2015), Christensen's *Competing Against Luck* and this book (2016), and Strategyn's 25th year (ch. 9, pp. 197–201). A list of the author's books and articles follows (p. 202), then contact details, ODIpro, Jobs-to-be-Done.com, and JTBDinstitute.org (p. 203).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **ODI fails too.** Three of the 21 launches in the track record study were unsuccessful; the sponsors "did a poor job of executing the commercialization" (Introduction, p. 24). At Cordis, initiatives "that did not speak to customers' needs were defunded altogether" (ch. 4 §X, p. 119). "Innovation is far from simple" (Introduction, p. 28), and defining the job "is not that easy" (ch. 4 §II, pp. 86–87).
- **Not for everyone, and not easy.** Becoming a practitioner "is not easy and it's not for everyone" (ch. 6, p. 164); candidates are asked to meet "all (or at least most)" of nine qualifications including Six Sigma training (ch. 6, pp. 175–176). Druker's closing line is "It's not trivial to do... it's not an easy exercise" (ch. 5, p. 145). "In a small organization with only 1 product", the author's answer is to hire his firm for the research (ch. 7, p. 177).
- **Demographic segments have a use.** Druker allows that "normal demographic methods for segmenting customers" are "helpful for sales resource prioritization", only not for finding opportunities (ch. 5, p. 138).
- **Qualitative methods are fine for gathering, not for prioritizing.** Desired outcomes "can be uncovered using any of the popular interviewing methods" (ch. 4 §III, p. 95); the quantitative survey is what segments and ranks them (ch. 2, pp. 52–53; ch. 4 §IV, p. 98).
- **The market average may show nothing.** Across all 270 Bosch respondents "there were no unmet needs"; the opportunity existed only inside a segment (ch. 5, p. 146). A segment must also be "large enough to warrant the investment" (ch. 4 §IV, p. 99).
- **A job can be too big for the company.** If the company does not have and will not acquire the capabilities to tackle the broader job, "the job is defined too broadly from a practical standpoint" (ch. 4 §II, p. 88). In platform markets the core job is hard to find without factor analysis (ch. 2, p. 55).
- **Each strategy has a losing situation.** A differentiated strategy "would likely fail" in an overserved segment and a disruptive one in an underserved segment (ch. 3, p. 71); disruptive is "often a less profitable strategy" (ch. 3, p. 76); discrete "can also be viewed as exploitative", with EpiPen as the case (ch. 3, p. 78); sustaining is wrong for a new entrant but "a good strategy for an incumbent" (ch. 3, pp. 78–79). Ideation with new technology can add cost without getting the job done better, as Bosch's laser idea "would have had little impact" (ch. 4 §VII, p. 108).
- **A company is not limited to one strategy.** Uber runs three; a firm "can choose, as Uber did, to create separate products" for overserved and underserved segments (ch. 3, pp. 70, 76).
- **Not every consumption chain job applies.** "Not all of these consumption chain jobs apply in every situation" (ch. 4 §I, p. 85).
- **Buyer and user may be one person.** Then the interviewer has to keep the "buyer's hat" on or the wrong outcomes come out (ch. 2, p. 61).
- **Emotional and social jobs are inputs to marketing, not to the job.** They "inform the decisions that lead to the creation of the value proposition and the effective marketing, positioning, and design", and are kept out of the functional job statement (ch. 2, p. 59; ch. 4 §II, p. 90).
- **The results have other causes.** Druker, asked about the 30% year, says "The dairy economy helped—it was a factor in it" (ch. 5, p. 142), and that agriculture "was a little bit outside of where Strategyn normally works" (p. 144).
- **Most of the firm's work is repositioning, not new markets.** "More often than not, we find ourselves trying to figure out the core functional job(s) an existing customer is trying to get done" rather than discovering new jobs (ch. 2, p. 55).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, company, or tool the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source outside the author's own publications could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure.

- **The 86% against 17% track record study (Introduction, pp. 21–24; ch. 4, p. 81; ch. 8, p. 194).** The only copies found are Strategyn's own PDF, "Innovation Track Record Study" (strategyn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Innovation-Track-Record-Study-Strategyn-1.pdf, also mirrored at innovationmanagement.se, 2010), which repeats the book's method: 43 client interviews, 21 launches, 18 successes, each rated by the sponsoring company. The researcher is not named in either the book or the report, and no third-party replication or review was found. The figure is unchecked as to its independence; Strategyn still prints it on its site (strategyn.com/outcome-driven-innovation-process).
- **Simon-Kucher's 72% (Foreword, p. 11).** Holds as stated: Simon-Kucher's 2014 Global Pricing Study of about 1,600 companies reported that 72% of new products introduced over the previous five years failed to meet their profit targets (simon-kucher.com, "Global Pricing Study 2014"; strategyonline.ca, "72% of all new products fail: study", 2014). It is a pricing consultancy's survey of self-reported profit targets, not launch outcomes.
- **Cordis, 1% to more than 20% share and 19 products (Introduction, pp. 16–17; ch. 4, pp. 101, 114, 119; ch. 9, p. 197).** The share and stock figures are unchecked; they appear only in the author's HBR article and his books. ## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **The headline number is self-graded.** The 86% rests on 21 launches rated successful by the sponsoring companies themselves, on a metric each chose after the fact from four options, with the three failures attributed by those same sponsors to "commercialization" rather than to the method (Introduction, pp. 23–24). The 17% it is set against averages twelve figures from 1997–2008 that measure different things, from "significantly impacts a company's growth" to plain success (pp. 22–23). The researcher is unnamed, the sample is the firm's own clients, and section 7 found no copy of the study outside the firm's site. "Five times more predictable" (p. 21) is the ratio of two numbers that are not measuring the same event.
- **Every case is the author's client, and the book ends as a brochure.** The six case studies are Strategyn engagements (ch. 5, pp. 124–161); the Foreword's author credits Strategyn for his own canvas (Foreword, p. 12); chapter 6 sends the reader to "Strategyn.com/ODIpro" with "Mentoring support" (ch. 6, p. 164); chapter 7 recommends that a small company hire the firm (ch. 7, p. 177); and the last page is contact details (ch. 9, p. 203). No engagement that did not work is described, and the three failures in the study are not named.
- **Correlation presented as cause.** Arm & Hammer's 30% year is credited to messaging while the interviewee himself says "The dairy economy helped—it was a factor in it" (ch. 5, p. 142). Cordis's stock quadrupling (Introduction, p. 17) and Microsoft beating a revenue goal "even before the fully revised product was available" (ch. 5, p. 129) are attributed to the method with no control and no other cause considered. Coloplast's double-digit growth "without changing its products or its pricing" (ch. 4, p. 101) has no timeframe beyond "less than six months" and no comparison to the market.
- **Numbers asserted, not sourced.** "95% of managers say there is internal disagreement on what a need is" (ch. 1, p. 41), "50 to 150 needs" and "5-80% of those needs may be unmet" (p. 35–36), "we have proven that demographic, psychographic, and behavioral and attitudinal data will nearly always fail" (ch. 4, p. 96), the 20% switching threshold (ch. 2, p. 48; ch. 3, p. 78), and the 20%-better-and-20%-cheaper rule (ch. 3, p. 73) are stated from "our experience" without data. The 3 to the power of 15 footnote (ch. 1, p. 36) assumes fifteen independent needs each answered by one of three independent ideas, which the book's own claim that needs come in structured sets contradicts.
- **A dated fact.** The Bosch CS20 is called "the company's best-selling circular saw in North America for over 10 years" (ch. 4, p. 108) without mention of its 2005 safety recall (section 7), and Hussmann, AMO, and Kroll Ontrack are presented as going concerns when two had already been sold and the third was sold two months after the copyright date.
- **The origin claim and the "hire" question sit awkwardly together.** The Introduction leans on Christensen having cited "Strategyn and me as originators" (Introduction, p. 19) and on his popularizing the idea that people "hire" products (p. 19), then chapter 4 calls asking what a customer hired a product to do "a common mistake" and "product-centric" (ch. 4, p. 87). Section 7 records that the field has since split into two schools on exactly this line.
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** Chapter 3's five strategies are illustrated with famous products (Nest, Netflix, Google Docs, Dollar Shave Club) that Strategyn did not work on and for which no outcome data is shown (ch. 3, pp. 67–69); the matrix is asserted from "Our experience and the work of others in this field" (p. 64). The "ultimate solution" and "single platform" advice (ch. 4, pp. 92, 122–123) rests on one unnamed agricultural company. The claim that jobs have "no geographical boundaries" (ch. 2, p. 56) is stated without a study across geographies.
- **The method is sized for a corporation.** Every worked example is a survey of 200 to 1,750 respondents (ch. 5, pp. 125, 146, 151, 157), the practitioner is one of 84 steps and six phases (ch. 6, pp. 166–175), a business unit needs a certified practitioner (ch. 7, p. 177), and the book's only advice for a small company is to buy the research (p. 177). The chapter 7 promise of "as little as four weeks" (p. 186) is for a company that already has a cross-functional team, a survey vendor budget, and 180 to 3,000 reachable customers.
- **Internal tension on what a need is.** Chapter 1 says needs cannot be captured in "the customer's own words" (p. 42) and that companies should not depend on the customer for solutions (p. 38); chapter 4 says "Customers know perfectly well how they measure success when executing a job, and are very capable of dictating those metrics" (ch. 4, p. 93). Both can be true, but the book does not say where dictation ends and translation into the required syntax begins, beyond pointing to a 2008 article (p. 95).
- **Figures the text cannot carry.** The Opportunity Landscape (ch. 4, pp. 110–111), the ODI ten-step diagram (p. 82), the market and product strategy diagrams (pp. 113, 117), and the phase-two and phase-three tables (ch. 7, pp. 183, 185) are referred to in the prose and are absent from the text extraction; a reader of the text alone has the algorithm and none of the pictures the chapter is built around.
