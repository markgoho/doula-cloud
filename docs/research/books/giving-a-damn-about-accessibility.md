# Giving a Damn About Accessibility, read back

## 1. Source

*Giving a Damn About Accessibility: A candid and practical handbook for designers*, Sheri Byrne-Haber, published by UX Collective (ISSN 2766-5267), 2021. No ISBN appears anywhere in the extracted front matter. Read 2026-09-10 from the `pdftotext -layout` extraction of the 49-page PDF, front to back.

**Page rule.** The book prints a page number in a running footer, either "Preface N", "About the author N", or "Giving a damn about accessibility N", and this file cites the printed page. Printed page = PDF index, offset 0, found by opening PDF page 4, whose footer reads "Preface 4", and PDF page 41, whose footer reads "Giving a damn about accessibility 41". The front matter (title page, copyright page, an epigraph) occupies PDF pages 1-3 and carries no printed number; cited here as "front matter, PDF page N". The book has no table of contents and no numbered chapters in the ordinary sense: four chapters each carry a single circled numeral (①-④) as their only heading, laid over a full-bleed title graphic, so this file cites them by title, e.g. "Ch. 1, Technology Doesn't Make Accessibility Hard, p. 9". A closing, unnumbered section, I Am Convinced! Now What?, and back matter about the UX Collective occupy pp. 47-49 and are cited as "Closing" and "Back matter".

## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, page by page, with its own exceptions, what has changed since 2021, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis is its own title and its first chapter heading: technology doesn't make accessibility hard, people who don't "give a damn" do (Ch. 1 title, p. 8-9). The chapter opens by naming the mechanism directly: every difficulty traces to one root cause, people not giving a damn "about others who are different" (Ch. 1, p. 8). The author's supporting claim across the book is that accessibility is already technically solved — the standards are two decades old and, in her figure, "4 hours" is enough to start applying them competently (Ch. 1, p. 24) — so what remains is a set of named human behaviors and organizational postures: eight categories of people who obstruct progress (Ch. 1), the expectation that a first attempt will fall short (Ch. 2), the temptation to treat accessibility expertise as an interchangeable add-on (Ch. 3), and the gap between minimum compliance and empathetic practice (Ch. 4).

The publisher's preface states what the book is not, ahead of the author's own chapters: not "too prescriptive," not "too aspirational," not "too charity-driven," and not a vehicle to sell "accessibility consulting services or tools," which it says other published guides often are (Preface, p. 4). Its own preview of the reality a reader is heading into: a first effort "will be awful," accessibility "isn't an add-on," and good practice is not a stopping point (Preface, p. 5).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Preface (UX Collective editors, pp. 4-5) and About the author (pp. 6-7)

Digital accessibility is defined broadly, spanning physical disability, neurodiversity, and a socio-economic dimension: people with disabilities face discrimination in the US at a rate the preface puts at two and a half times the rate people without disabilities face (Preface, p. 4). About the author credits Byrne-Haber's accessibility leadership at McDonald's, Albertsons, and VMware, her co-authorship of the Digital Accessibility Maturity Model, a JD/MBA/MIS background, and Trusted Tester v5 and IAAP certification (pp. 6-7), alongside a personal stake: a deaf daughter and her own lifelong wheelchair use (p. 7).

### Ch. 1, Technology Doesn't Make Accessibility Hard, People Who Don't Give a Damn Do (pp. 8-25)

Eight named categories of people who obstruct accessibility work, each its own short section. People allergic to change, opened with a Grace Hopper line about humans disliking change (p. 10). People who demand a business case, which the chapter calls "ableist" (p. 12) on the reasoning that access is a civil-rights question under the ADA and comparable Australian and Canadian law, not a cost-benefit one (pp. 11-12). People who demand proof a specific WCAG level applies, tracing an abandoned Obama-era DOJ rulemaking and a Supreme Court case against Domino's that was sent back to the lower court without a ruling on the facts, plus a figure of 3,550 digital accessibility lawsuits filed in 2020 (pp. 13-14), and a forecast that WCAG 2.2 would ship "Fall 2021" and WCAG 3.0 ("Silver") no earlier than 2023 (p. 14). People who demand proof of the specific fix, illustrated with a moving-carousel example: WCAG requires a pause mechanism, never specifically "a button" (p. 15). People who ship inaccessible new features ahead of fixing old ones, citing Twitter's June 2020 "Voice Notes" launch, which shipped with no non-audio equivalent, and a livestreamed dismissal of colorblind-accessibility requests by a Dead by Daylight employee (pp. 16-18). People who treat the affected group as too small to matter, drawing the comparison to COVID/herd-immunity reasoning and citing 26% of the US population and almost 20% of college students as having a disability (pp. 19-20). People who don't see disabled people as customers, calling the position a "circular logical fallacy" (p. 21), beside a sourced-to-Wikipedia definition callout for "Assistive Technology" (p. 22). People who sell or buy accessibility overlays, plugins, and widgets, citing that 10% of accessibility lawsuits filed by late 2020 targeted companies that had installed one, and pointing to a "35-page scathing indictment" of overlays by Karl Groves (pp. 22-24). The chapter closes with a four-item remedy list: invest and embrace change; reward accessible releases, citing an Apple bonus policy tied partly to accessibility; include people with disabilities in the process; and solicit executive support (pp. 24-25).

### Ch. 2, Your First Attempt at Making Anything Accessible Will Be Awful (pp. 26-33)

Five numbered reasons a first attempt is expected to fall short. No one starts a new skill at expert level, compared to learning a language or an instrument, noting WCAG's guidelines can be satisfied multiple ways with no ranking given (p. 28). Perfectionism is the wrong approach, because WCAG's non-prescriptive nature leaves "perfect" undefined, and waiting for it only prolongs discrimination (p. 29). Even a person with a disability can't represent every disability, noting "just over 29%" of the accessibility-testing profession is itself disabled, and naming accessibility "not a zero-sum game" (pp. 30-31). Improvement requires accepting criticism without defensiveness (pp. 31-32). Accessibility is a continuous process, not a project, illustrated with an "Accessibility Flywheel" diagram linking accessibility focus, features, operational practices, and disabled customers (pp. 32-33). The chapter closes on the figure that 98% of websites are inaccessible, so a first attempt "couldn't possibly do any worse" (p. 33).

### Ch. 3, Accessibility Is Not an Add-on Service (pp. 34-38)

Opens with an anecdote about a contact-center vendor pitching accessibility testing as one line item among many, likened to a restaurant that also sells fast food (p. 35), arguing accessibility consulting is a specialization comparable to privacy or security consulting (p. 35). Three named mistakes of new market entrants: assuming anyone can become an accessibility tester, noting disabled coworkers are rare outside North America and Europe because of disability unemployment rates the chapter puts "as high as 90%" (pp. 36-37); rarely employing people with disabilities themselves, describing firms that outsource disabled-tester labor to charities at a fraction of standard pay (p. 37); and claiming to do every kind of accessibility testing, including PDF/UA testing, without being able to explain the acronym (pp. 37-38). Closes noting accessibility-consulting demand rose through COVID-19 while general software-consulting demand slowed (p. 38).

### Ch. 4, Turning Good Accessibility into Great Accessibility (pp. 39-47)

Opens on the figure that only 2% of web pages do a good or great job (p. 41), then six paired "good vs. great" distinctions. Compliance vs. empathy: going past the targeted WCAG level (the example given is AAA touch-target size), extending color-contrast checks beyond text into colorblindness modes and focus indicators, holding employee-facing tools to the same bar as public ones, and using what the book calls "premium language" in copy (pp. 41-42). Product vs. the whole experience: extending accessibility to documentation, training, support, and surveys, and even to physical packaging, naming Microsoft's Xbox packaging as the standard (pp. 42-43). User research vs. incorporating disabled people's own feedback, including testers with disabilities rather than simulated impairment, because congenital and acquired disability produce different lived perspectives (pp. 43-44). Accessible design systems, because one design-system defect propagates to "every single component" that consumes it (p. 45), naming Clarity (VMware), Lightning (Salesforce), Spectrum (Adobe), and Carbon (IBM) as accessible open-source examples, and citing PayPal's contribution of accessibility fixes back to Bootstrap (pp. 45-46). Accessibility as a release gate, with an exceptions process for anyone who wants to ship around it (p. 46). And speaking about accessibility at general design conferences rather than only accessibility ones, described as reaching "the non-believers" (p. 47).

### Closing, I Am Convinced! Now What? (pp. 47-48)

A checklist of next actions: read books, subscribe to newsletters and blogs, watch videos, take courses, attend conferences, find tools, and get certified, crediting a further Accessibility Resource Center (p. 47).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits her own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **WCAG is not prescriptive about mechanism.** The carousel example: a pause control does not have to be "a button" — any component that stops the motion satisfies the guideline (Ch. 1, p. 15).
- **"Perfect" is not the bar.** The warning against perfectionism does not excuse low effort; it limits itself to the point at which a tester stops chasing an undefined "perfect" and moves to the next item needing attention (Ch. 2, p. 29).
- **A disability does not make someone a universal representative, even for that disability.** Three people with identical hearing loss can use three different communication modes, so even a disabled practitioner is "looking through" one lens among several (Ch. 2, p. 30).
- **"Bias" is used in a neutral sense.** The author limits the word explicitly: she does not mean intentional discrimination, only that limited exposure narrows what a person notices (Ch. 2, p. 30).
- **Testers with disabilities are necessary but not exclusive.** Not every tester "needs to be disabled," though balance matters (Ch. 3, p. 37).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter.

1. **Demanding a business case for accessibility is "ableist," because access is a civil-rights question, not a cost-benefit one** (Ch. 1, pp. 11-12). Record: `CLAUDE.md`'s cross-cutting expectation, stated on every feature with no business-case gate: "What is built is usable by everyone who has to use it." [#447](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/447) cites the same standing expectation, recorded in `docs/testing.md`: "`CLAUDE.md` carries accessibility as a standing expectation on every feature."
2. **Which WCAG conformance level applies has to be argued and proven case by case, because the ADA does not name WCAG and the case law is unsettled** (Ch. 1, pp. 13-15). Record: `app/e2e/accessibility.e2e.ts`'s own comment states the level is "pre-argued rather than picked here," citing [ADR-0021](../../adr/0021-govuk-is-the-reference-for-service-patterns.md)'s adoption of the GOV.UK Design System and the WCAG 2.2 AA bar GDS holds itself to; `docs/testing.md` records the same ruleset, `wcag2a`/`wcag2aa`/`wcag21a`/`wcag21aa`/`wcag22aa`.
3. **Whether an organization holds its employee-facing tools to the same accessibility bar as its public-facing ones** (Ch. 4, p. 42). Record: [ADR-0024](../../adr/0024-layout-is-intrinsic-and-320px-is-a-conformance-commitment.md) states its conformance figure "binds a Staff-only admin screen" exactly as hard "as a screen someone reads on a train." `docs/testing.md`'s route inventory for `app/e2e/accessibility.e2e.ts` scans Staff-only routes (the roster, the settings screens, `/account`) under the same WCAG ruleset as the Client portal and the signed-out shell.
4. **A design system's own accessibility defect propagates to every component built from it** (Ch. 4, pp. 45-46). Record: [ADR-0021](../../adr/0021-govuk-is-the-reference-for-service-patterns.md) adopts the GOV.UK Design System as the reference for service patterns, names the Atomic Design layers of [ADR-0018](../../adr/0018-templates-are-a-design-system-layer-with-two-named-exits.md) as the project's own component tiers, and names `app/src/lib/styles/tokens.css` as the one place color, type, and spacing truth lives; its 2026-09-01 amendment names Inclusive Components (Heydon Pickering) and Adrian Roselli's writing as the accessibility-first fallback when GOV.UK has no pattern.
5. **Making accessibility a release gate in a product's own ship process** (Ch. 4, p. 46). Record: [#447](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/447) decided this rather than leaving axe a reporting-only check; `docs/testing.md` records the reasoning: a violation "fails the build," so "day one is green" and red afterward "means a regression."

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, standard, tool, or company the book leans on, checked in September 2026. "Unchecked" means WebSearch was unavailable for the rest of this session (its per-session budget was exhausted) and no reliable source was reachable by a direct URL fetch in its place.

- **WCAG 2.2, forecast to ship "Fall 2021"** (Ch. 1, p. 14). Changed: the W3C's own published Recommendation page states WCAG 2.2 became a formal Recommendation on 12 December 2024, more than three years after the book's forecast (w3.org/TR/WCAG22).
- **WCAG 3.0 ("Silver"), forecast "no earlier than 2023"** (Ch. 1, p. 14). Holds, and then some: the W3C's own WCAG 3 introduction page, current as of September 2026, calls it "an incomplete draft" not expected to finish for "a few more years" (w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/wcag3-intro).
- **98% of websites are completely inaccessible** (Ch. 2, p. 33). Close, and lower: the WebAIM Million's 2026 report finds 95.9% of home pages carry a detectable WCAG failure, up from 94.8% in 2025 (webaim.org/projects/million). The overwhelming-majority claim holds; the specific figure has drifted down two points.
- **Trusted Tester v5, named as the author's own certification** (About the author, p. 7). Holds: the US Section508.gov site's current Trusted Tester page confirms v5 remains the version DHS trains and certifies on, v4.0 no longer supported (section508.gov/test/trusted-tester).
- **A "35-page scathing indictment" of accessibility overlays, credited to Karl Groves** (Ch. 1, p. 24). Partly changed: the live Overlay Fact Sheet (overlayfactsheet.com) lists Groves as its first signatory and a source for several of its supporting articles, but the document itself is a multi-signatory community statement rather than a piece he alone authored. Its substance is unchanged: it still argues full compliance cannot be reached with an overlay, and that remediation belongs at the product's own source.
- **Clarity, VMware's design system, named as the chapter's lead accessible-design-system example** (Ch. 4, p. 45). Changed: the `vmware/clarity` repository was archived by its owner on 27 March 2023, with development split into separate Clarity Angular and Clarity Core projects; the single project the book names no longer exists in that form.
- **Lightning, Salesforce's design system, named alongside Clarity** (Ch. 4, p. 45). Changed, very recently: the `salesforce-ux/design-system` repository was archived on 12 August 2026, weeks before this read-back.
- **Spectrum (Adobe) and Carbon (IBM), the other two systems named on the same page** (Ch. 4, p. 45). Hold: both repositories remain actively maintained as of September 2026, and both publish their own accessibility claims in their READMEs.
- **The Supreme Court's handling of the Domino's accessibility case, and the figure of 3,550 digital accessibility lawsuits filed in 2020** (Ch. 1, pp. 13-14). Unchecked.
- **PayPal's contribution of accessibility fixes back to Bootstrap** (Ch. 4, p. 46). Unchecked.
- **Apple's bonus policy tied partly to accessibility** (Ch. 1, p. 24). Unchecked.
- **Twitter's accessibility team, said to have been hired within 48 hours of the "Voice Notes" incident** (Ch. 1, pp. 16-17). The 2020 incident itself is not in dispute; whether that team still exists under the platform's current ownership is unchecked.
- **Dead by Daylight's promised colorblindness accessibility roadmap** (Ch. 1, pp. 17-18). Unchecked.
- **8% male colorblindness, 26% of the US population, and "almost 20%" of college students with a disability** (Ch. 1, pp. 19-20). Unchecked; these are commonly cited figures this read-back did not independently re-verify against a current source.
- **"Just over 29%" of the accessibility-testing profession identifying as disabled** (Ch. 2, p. 30). Unchecked.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Figures without a source.** The 3,550-lawsuit count, the "as high as 90%" disability-unemployment figure, 8% male colorblindness, the 26%/20% disability-prevalence figures, and "just over 29%" of testers identifying as disabled all appear with no citation in the text (Ch. 1, pp. 14, 19-20, 36; Ch. 2, p. 30).
- **Single incidents generalized into a category.** The Twitter and Dead by Daylight stories are each one company's one misstep, used to stand for an entire class of "people who prioritize new features" (Ch. 1, pp. 16-18); the ex-VMware-CEO ACL anecdote is one executive's one experience, used to argue a general empathy point about physical accessibility (Ch. 4, p. 42).
- **A confident technical forecast that did not hold.** WCAG 2.2's "Fall 2021" date (Ch. 1, p. 14) is stated with the same certainty as the litigation-risk argument built next to it in the same section; section 7 found the standard shipped more than three years later.
- **A "best current example" list in a fast-moving field.** Of the four accessible open-source design systems named on one page (Ch. 4, p. 45), one had already been archived and split apart by the time of this read-back, and a second was archived weeks before it — evidence that a named-example list of this kind ages faster than the book's other claims.
- **A single root cause asserted, not tested against a rival explanation the book itself supplies.** Chapter 1 states that "one root cause" explains every accessibility difficulty (p. 8), yet Chapter 3 separately describes accessibility expertise as scarce and slow to acquire — a resourcing constraint the book never weighs against the single-cause claim it opens with (Ch. 1, p. 8; Ch. 3, pp. 36-37).
