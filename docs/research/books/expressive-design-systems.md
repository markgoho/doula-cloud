## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, chapter by chapter, with its own exceptions, what has changed since 2019, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's own frame, is that a design system should be purpose-built, should support a range of expression, and should inspire use, rather than only unify a product's components (Introduction, p. 5). Unity, all of a brand's elements working "as one," is distinguished from cohesion, the quality that keeps an interface "easy to understand" across an experience, and a system with the first but not the second still loses people — illustrated by the New York City subway's 1960s signage (Ch. 1, p. 8). The book names a third quality, harmony, the product of every design element combining into "pleasing layouts," as the target of the whole book (Ch. 3, p. 50).

The author says what the claim is not. It is "not a step-by-step guide" to building a system from nothing; the stated purpose is to help a team "integrate brand expression and range" into a system that already exists, and the book is deliberately not linear (Introduction, p. 5). It assumes the reader's team is "already working in systems," documented or not, rather than starting a pattern library from zero (Ch. 1, p. 3). Team size scopes the claim too: for a small team chasing efficiency, "a small set of documented components" may be all that is needed, which the book distinguishes from the fuller, cross-disciplinary work the rest of the book describes (Introduction, p. 2).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Foreword (Alla Kholmatova, unnumbered front matter, PDF page 5)

Kholmatova frames the problem the book answers: it is now easy to stand up "a functional design system," but many go unused, or are used only partly, once built. She credits the book with a "compelling conceptual framework" for grasping how a system expresses a brand, not only its day-to-day components.

### Introduction (pp. 1–5)

The opening claim is that products drift toward inconsistency without a shared language, and that the drift tracks scale, not a team's choice (p. 1). The evidence is a quoted account from Pinterest's Andreas Pihlström, who describes designs for "our website, apps and marketing" losing "the same personality" as features were added with no unifying vision (p. 1). The chapter distinguishes three terms often used interchangeably: a pattern (or component) library is reusable UI building blocks; a style guide is documentation of brand language and component rationale; a design system is the broadest term, encompassing both plus underlying principles (p. 2). Four bulleted benefits (faster builds, better products, improved maintenance, stronger product-team focus) are weighed against four bulleted frustrations (rigid systems, monotonous systems, overly specific systems, unsupported systems), and the book's own term, "expressive design system," is defined by three qualities: purpose-built, supporting a range of expression, and inspiring use (pp. 3–5).

### 1 Purpose and Principles (pp. 7–20)

The chapter opens with the 1960s New York City subway signage project by Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda, used to illustrate unity versus cohesion: the resulting 185-page graphics manual gave the system both, where unity means brand elements "work together as one" and cohesion is what keeps an interface understandable across an experience (pp. 7–9). A purpose statement answers four questions — the goal, why it matters, how the system will help, and who it is for — gathered from stakeholders and, ideally, from user interviews (pp. 9–12). Design principles are then defined as guidelines a team follows toward more aligned decisions, illustrated with Intercom's "conversations, not transactions" and quotes from designer Mark Boulton and Salesforce's JD Vogt on how principles speed decisions (pp. 13–15). Effective principles are described as specific and opinionated, grown from within the team, and usable as decision-making tools (p. 13). A six-step principles workshop is given — choose a cross-disciplinary team, gather materials, establish goals, brainstorm one principle per Post-it, converge and theme, rank and vote — aiming for five principles, since people start forgetting them past that count (pp. 16–17). Spotify's "TUNE" acronym (Tone, Usable, Necessary, Emotive) is given as an example of principles turned into a decision-making tool (pp. 18–19).

### 2 The Process Behind the System (pp. 21–49)

Brasilia, planned for "five hundred thousand people" and now home to two million, illustrates a plan that failed to consider "how real people behave" (pp. 21–22). Planning starts with a product-and-user inventory, documented in a spreadsheet with fields for product name, platform, audience, purpose, and product owner (pp. 22–23), then an ecosystem map: choose an audience, list their tasks, break one task into a flow, map the flow to product sections, note the owning teams, and visualize the flow with screenshots — the Sprint customer-support example shows navigation, typography, and spacing all changing mid-flow (pp. 24–27). An audit follows, covering both design language (quality and consistency of type, color, iconography, space) and user interface components, run as a team exercise using a visual, collaborative tool such as AirTable, choosing categories, then screenshotting and categorizing (pp. 29–31). Components are evaluated by frequency and by whether high-frequency components are also "high-value" — unique to the product, like Airbnb's listing cards (pp. 32–33) — then grouped by purpose rather than appearance, an approach credited to Alla Kholmatova's *Design Systems* (p. 34). Vox's consolidation of eighty-one HTML content components used five criteria: does it add value, is it used by three or more of eight brands (with an exception for a single brand's must-have), does it engage readers, and does it uphold the design principles (pp. 35–36). A worked example follows: Eater, the Verge, and Polygon each had visually distinct scorecard components that solved the same problem, mapped by user goal and content, and consolidated into a shared "product card" with two purpose-driven variations, the venue card and the game card (pp. 39–42). Documentation guidance follows — name, purpose, example, with optional usage/anti-usage/content/related-pattern sections — illustrated by Vox's "river" metaphor for an article list, replaced with the clearer, more inclusive "Story Feed" (pp. 43–45), and by Shopify Polaris's discovery that developers want code first while designers and content strategists scroll for rationale (p. 46). The chapter closes on rollout: an incremental rollout tests components in context and shows progress sooner but risks a "limbo period" of mixed old and new styles, while a large-scale redesign avoids that mixing at the cost of more time up front (pp. 45–48).

### 3 Communicating Your Brand (pp. 50–74)

Harmony, every element combining into "pleasing layouts," is introduced as a third quality alongside unity and cohesion, needed because components are designed in isolation but used in combination (p. 50). The chapter's core is a four-layer component hierarchy: basic components (buttons, links, text fields — the smallest, most reused, and most flexible, where color and typography should stay functional), composite components (arrangements of basic components judged by how well each "solves their chosen problem," where type and color can be more expressive), containers (full-width regions where color signals content shifts and space controls density), and layouts, "also known as templates," the layer that decides how a screen's pieces are arranged (pp. 51–59). The Guardian's own item/slice/container hierarchy is given as a worked example of the same idea (pp. 58–59). Brand decisions are then framed as big levers and small dials: four levers — size, scale, density, and weight — set a design's global feeling, and dials (typography, spacing, color values) are the granular choices that should follow from the levers rather than be set independently, illustrated by Vox.com ("explanatory," denser, less size contrast) against the Verge ("entertaining," larger, more size contrast) (pp. 60–65). Type, space, and color systems are each given a short recipe: a base font size and a modular scale ratio for type; a base spacing unit (often 4px) and a linear-or-nonlinear progression, named either descriptively, by T-shirt size, or numerically, for space; and hue/saturation/luminance as the three levers for color, illustrated by Lyft's discovery of fifteen internal variations of "pink" (pp. 65–73). The chapter closes with an Eliel Saarinen quote on designing a thing within "its next larger context" as the summary of harmony (p. 72).

### 4 Making Room for Variation (pp. 75–101)

Paula Scher's Public Theater identity is the opening example: keeping the typography and color system but varying it by season avoided both the boredom of sameness and the lost recognizability of redesigning per play (pp. 75–77). Three kinds of deviation are named — unintentional divergence (a documentation gap), intentional but unnecessary divergence (a designer wanting to feel unconstrained), and intentional, meaningful divergence, the book's own goal, solving a user problem no existing pattern reaches (p. 76). Three contexts justify variation: brand (Vox Media's newsletter modules were consolidated because their variation was purely visual, while the Verge's masthead component was kept flexible because it was tied to brand strategy and later reused across other Chorus-platform sites), audience (Airbnb's standard versus Plus listing pages share fonts, iconography, and components but pull density, weight, and scale differently to feel more premium), and environment (Shopify's Retail team needed a larger, higher-contrast point-of-sale interface usable "at arm's length" under bright light, which led Polaris to build a dark mode) (pp. 78–85). Components can be varied by content, structure, or style (p. 86), while the visual language is varied through theming, defined as a token/role/value system — "$text" is a token, "primary text color" its role, and a hex value what the role resolves to per theme (pp. 87–89). Type theming is called the hardest case, because fonts differ in optical sizing and vertical metrics (pp. 89–91); the book recommends letting teams choose a base size and a font ratio rather than every individual size value (pp. 92–93). Space should be themed "as a lever," not by individual dials, with three density settings (compact, default, loose) as the recommended ceiling on variety, illustrated by Gmail's density setting (pp. 94–96). Color theming is best done through "color groups" — a small number of named background categories (Vox Media used light, dark, bright, and muted) each paired with a fixed set of foreground roles — rather than per-element variables; Vox Media's own first attempt produced "almost three hundred" variables and is given as the cautionary case (pp. 96–100). The chapter closes on Stewart Brand's *How Buildings Learn*, whose layers (site, structure, skin, services, space plan, stuff) model how different parts of a design system should change at different rates (p. 101).

### 5 Managing Design Systems (pp. 102–116)

The chapter opens with a cooking metaphor, a design system as a "pantry," not a cookbook, and argues the right component hierarchy depends on team scale: Bootstrap, used by many unrelated teams, is nearly all basic components, while Mailchimp's system is mostly composite components tailored to one product (pp. 102–104). Systems should evolve from real use: a Shopify logistics-team designer found a banner component didn't fit inside a card, paired with the Polaris team to fix it, and the fix was folded back into the shared system (pp. 104–106). Buy-in is won by including people in planning exercises, pitching differently to disciplines (developers respond to the "Don't Repeat Yourself" principle, designers to better user experiences), and sustained onboarding (pp. 106–108). Maintaining a system takes different skills than building one — product management, community management, data and research — and rotating people between the systems team and product teams is recommended to keep both sides informed (pp. 108–109). Design-system ambassador programs at Etsy, GOV.UK, and Sprout Social are given as a model, quoting Sprout Social's Ben Lister on ambassadors as an organization's "eyes, ears, and voice," and GOV.UK's own design-system working group and its published review/recommend/advocate responsibilities (pp. 109–110). Nathan Curtis and Jina Anne's four governance models — solitary, centralized, federated, and cyclical — are summarized, with cyclical (a centralized team plus welcomed product-team contributions) named as the one that sounds ideal but is harder to run than it looks (p. 111). Support channels (a Slack channel, weekly office hours at Shopify) and contribution guidance (GOV.UK's published "Community" section and contribution criteria) round out the chapter, alongside a warning against over-supporting people past the point of letting them solve problems themselves (pp. 111–113). The chapter closes on John Gall's law from *Systemantics* — a working complex system is always found to have "evolved from a simple system" that worked — as the argument for starting small in every dimension (components, guidelines, governance) rather than shipping a large system at once, and Donella Meadows's observation that a system's greatest diversity appears at its borders (pp. 114–116).

### Conclusion, Acknowledgments, Resources, References, Index (pp. 117–126)

The conclusion contrasts Levittown, the first mass-produced American suburb, whose developer William Levitt refused to sell to people of color, saying a housing problem and a racial problem "can't... both" be solved at once, with Morris Milgram's Concord Park, built with the same idyllic features but racially inclusive (p. 117). The point drawn from the contrast is stated directly: "systems aren't neutral" (p. 117), reflecting the values of whoever builds them. The book's closing claim is that a design system is "an opportunity" to make products more accessible and inclusive, contingent on "a heterogeneous group of people" contributing to it (pp. 117–118). Acknowledgments, a Resources list (organized under Systems thinking, Design, and Service design, including Christopher Alexander's *A Pattern Language* and *The Timeless Way of Building*, Donella Meadows's *Thinking in Systems*, and Josef Albers's *Interaction of Color*), a References list of the book's shortened URLs, and an Index close the book (pp. 118–126).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits her own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** A small team chasing efficiency may need only "a small set of documented components," not the full cross-disciplinary process the book describes (Introduction, p. 2).
- **Principles change.** Principles "can and do change" — the book explicitly warns against treating them as permanent (Ch. 1, p. 13).
- **Specificity has a ceiling.** Broadly used frameworks like Bootstrap "don't (and shouldn't)" carry the same opinionated, product-specific usage guidance a single product's system can afford (Ch. 3, p. 54).
- **The hierarchy is a convention, not a law.** Teams define the basic/composite/container/layout hierarchy differently; what the book gives is "a general rule," not a fixed taxonomy (Ch. 3, p. 52).
- **Naming metaphors don't scale.** A memorable internal metaphor ("River") can become a source of onboarding friction and is better replaced by a plainer name once new people join (Ch. 2, pp. 43–44).
- **Rollout trade-offs cut both ways.** An incremental rollout risks a period of mixed old and new styles; a large-scale redesign avoids that but "takes more time up front" — neither is presented as categorically better (Ch. 2, pp. 47–48).
- **Type theming is the hard case.** Color is the only dial that changes "without changing anything else"; type cannot be swapped without cascading changes to spacing, because fonts vary in optical sizing and vertical metrics (Ch. 4, pp. 89–91).
- **Density variety is capped on purpose.** Three settings, not more, to avoid "too much variety" (Ch. 4, p. 95).
- **Theming trades control for consistency.** Foreground-only theming is "much simpler, but also more limited" than theming both foreground and background, and theming everything without color groups produced "almost three hundred" unmanageable variables the first time it was tried (Ch. 4, pp. 97–98).
- **Component granularity depends on team shape.** A composite-heavy system like Mailchimp's is hard for an outside team to reuse; a basic-heavy system like Bootstrap generalizes further but offers less opinionated guidance (Ch. 5, pp. 102–104).
- **The ideal governance model is the hardest to run.** The cyclical model "sounds ideal," combining a centralized team's system-wide view with federated teams' context, but making it work is admitted to be "harder than it seems," since a central team struggles to see every product roadmap (Ch. 5, p. 111).
- **Support has a ceiling too.** It is easy to "over-index" on support — walking someone through every step rather than pointing them to documentation and letting them solve the next problem themselves (Ch. 5, p. 112).
- **Systems aren't automatically good.** The Conclusion's closing turn limits the whole book's enthusiasm for systems: a system "reflects the values" of whoever builds it, so an unrepresentative group of contributors produces a system that fails the people left out (Conclusion, pp. 117–118).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter or holds it, then nothing else.

1. **A layered component hierarchy — basic components, composite components, containers, and a top layer the book itself calls "layouts (also known as templates)," which decides how a screen's pieces are arranged** (Ch. 3, pp. 51–52, 58). Record: [ADR-0018](../../adr/0018-templates-are-a-design-system-layer-with-two-named-exits.md), whose four tiers (atoms/molecules/organisms per [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93), plus a Templates layer for page-level arrangement) and [ADR-0003](../../adr/0003-css-layout-primitives-as-native-custom-elements.md)'s design tokens on CSS custom properties predate this read-back.
2. **Theming as a token/role/value system, with semantic rather than descriptive token names** (Ch. 4, p. 88). Record: `app/src/lib/styles/tokens.css`'s own header, which restricts every step name to `src/lib/components/**` and `src/lib/styles/**` so "a route names a purpose, never a size," and [#417](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/417), the ticket that set the token API.
3. **Color grouped by a small number of named background categories rather than hundreds of per-element variables, with a warning against "three hundred customizable values"** (Ch. 4, pp. 98–101). Record: `tokens.css`'s Material Design 3 role set — `surface`, `on-surface`, `outline`, `primary`, `error`, and their variants — a fixed vocabulary rather than a per-component palette.
4. **Theming as an environmental variation — asking "where" a product is used — illustrated by Shopify Polaris's dark mode, built for a point-of-sale screen used "at arm's length" under bright light** (Ch. 4, pp. 84–85). Record: [docs/design/brief.md](../../design/brief.md)'s dark-mode section ([#409](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/409)) and `tokens.css`'s `prefers-color-scheme`/`[data-theme]` blocks.
5. **A type scale built from a base font size and steps, and a spacing scale built from a small base unit** (Ch. 3, pp. 65–68). Record: `tokens.css`'s eight-step type scale and ten-step, 4px-based spacing scale, both fluid `clamp()` steps on what the file calls "the ramp" ([#531](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/531), [#540](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/540)).
6. **Bringing an existing pattern into the system only past a stated bar — Vox's criterion was value and use by three or more of eight brands** (Ch. 2, pp. 35–36). Record: ADR-0018's own extraction bar — "one consumer stays a raw exception; two identical consumers earn a variant or a Template" — citing [#196](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/196), [#187](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/187), and [#189](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/189).

Record: ADR-0018's `style-guide.spec.ts`, which fails CI for any component with no matching `/style-guide` page, and its rule that each Template gets "its own style-guide page rendered with realistic placeholder content."

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, or company the book leans on, checked in September 2026. WebSearch was unavailable for the whole of this check (the session's search budget was already exhausted), so every entry below was checked by WebFetch against a specific known URL rather than a search; a figure with no working URL found in that time is unchecked and named at the end.

- **Vox Media's cross-brand consolidation of eighty-one HTML content components on the Chorus platform, reduced by five criteria into a shared "product card" used by Eater, the Verge, and Polygon** (Ch. 2, pp. 35–36, 39–42). Changed, at the platform and the company level. Vox Media wound down Chorus in December 2022 amid an advertising slump and moved its own titles to WordPress VIP; by 2025–2026 the company itself split, with Vox and New York Magazine sold to James Murdoch's Lupa Systems and the Verge, Eater, SB Nation, PopSugar, the Dodo, Thrillist, and Punch sold to Penske Media Corporation, which then sold Polygon on to Valnet in May 2025 with most of its staff laid off (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vox_Media). The platform and the single company the shared component shipped under no longer exist.
- **Shopify Polaris, including its Retail point-of-sale surfaces** (Ch. 4, pp. 84–85; Ch. 5, pp. 102–104). Holds. Polaris is still published as Shopify's current UI framework, and its live reference still names POS UI extensions for the smart grid, cart, and post-purchase screens (shopify.dev/docs/api/polaris), so the point-of-sale environment the dark-mode example rests on still exists. Dark mode itself is unchecked; the fetched documentation excerpt did not surface it.
- **Mailchimp as the composite-heavy, single-product design system, contrasted with Bootstrap's basic-heavy, many-team one** (Ch. 5, pp. 102–104). Changed in ownership, not in kind. Intuit acquired Mailchimp on November 1, 2021, for about $12 billion and renamed the platform Intuit Mailchimp, but the product continues to operate (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mailchimp). Bootstrap remains actively maintained, currently at version 5.3.8 (getbootstrap.com).
- **GOV.UK's design-system working group, its review/recommend/advocate responsibilities, and its published "Community" section and contribution criteria** (Ch. 5, pp. 109–113). Holds, with a naming shift: the live site now frames this as a "community" rather than a "working group," but it still publishes contribution criteria and a research- and GitHub-issue-driven review process open to anyone (design-system.service.gov.uk/community).
- **The New York City subway signage project by Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda, and its graphics manual** (Ch. 1, pp. 7–9). Holds, with a date correction. The Transit Authority engaged Vignelli and Noorda's firm in spring 1966, and the manual was not finished until 1970, so the project runs past "the 1960s" framing and into the following decade (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massimo_Vignelli).
- **Brasília, designed for "five hundred thousand people" and now home to a much larger population** (Ch. 2, pp. 21–22). Partly unchecked. The planned core, the Plano Piloto, never exceeded roughly 500,000 and today holds about 214,000; the book's "two million" matches no single current count, sitting under the Federal District's roughly 2.8 million and well under the wider metro's roughly 3.5 million (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brasília). The point that the built city outgrew its plan still holds; the specific figure does not match a 2026 count.
- **Intercom's "conversations, not transactions" principle** (Ch. 1, p. 14). The company by that name is gone. Intercom renamed itself Fin in May 2026, and Salesforce completed its acquisition on September 10, 2026, two days before this read-back (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercom_(company)). Whether the principle survived the rename is unchecked.
- **John Gall's *Systemantics* and the law that a working complex system evolves from a simple one** (Ch. 5, p. 114). Holds and has grown. The book has run to three editions since 1975, and Gall's law is now cited specifically as a caution against over-architecting systems built with AI coding agents (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gall_(author)).
- **Lyft's internal variations of "pink"** (Ch. 3, p. 70). Holds as a live example. Lyft operates in 2026 with pink still its central brand color, including a "Lyft Pink" membership tier (lyft.com). The specific count of variations is unchecked; no source outside the book was found for it.
- **Alla Kholmatova's *Design Systems*, credited for grouping components by purpose** (Ch. 2, p. 34). Holds. The book is still sold in its original 2017 form by its publisher, with no newer edition found (smashingmagazine.com/design-systems-book).
- **Not checked:** the Guardian's item/slice/container hierarchy (Ch. 3, pp. 58–59) — theguardian.com could not be reached; Airbnb's standard-versus-Plus listing comparison (Ch. 4, pp. 78–85) — the Airbnb and TechCrunch pages tried returned no usable page; Gmail's density setting (Ch. 4, pp. 94–96) — Google's help and mail pages redirected to a login wall; Spotify's "TUNE" acronym (Ch. 1, pp. 18–19) — not reached in the time available; Pinterest's Andreas Pihlström and his quoted account (Introduction, p. 1) — the page tried returned an error; Paula Scher's Public Theater identity (Ch. 4, pp. 75–77) — not reached; Sprout Social's Ben Lister and its ambassador program (Ch. 5, p. 110) — Sprout Social itself is confirmed still independent and public as of September 2026, but Lister's quote and the program were not separately checked; Etsy's ambassador program (Ch. 5, p. 109) — Etsy remains active through 2026 but sold Depop and cut staff that August, and the ambassador program specifically was not checked; Donella Meadows's *Thinking in Systems* (Ch. 5, p. 116) — the page tried did not resolve.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **The book's own two dominant examples are the author's own two employers.** Author Yesenia Perez-Cruz was Senior Director of Product Design at Vox Media, where she led the design system the book draws on repeatedly (Vox.com versus the Verge's levers, the "river"-to-"Story Feed" rename, the Chorus eighty-one-component audit, the Vox Media color-groups story, the Verge's masthead reuse, Vox Media's newsletter-module consolidation), and she went on to lead Shopify's Polaris team, the book's other most-detailed example (basic-heavy governance, the Retail dark-mode case) (abookapart.com/products/expressive-design-systems). Two employers, not an independent sample, supply most of the book's evidence.
- **The most detailed case is reported as an outcome with no measurement.** Vox Media's consolidation of eighty-one components into a shared product card by five stated criteria (Ch. 2, pp. 35–36, 39–42) is told as a narrative of the audit and the criteria; no before-or-after figure for maintenance cost, engagement, or consistency is given, so a reader can confirm that the consolidation happened but not that it worked better than what it replaced.
- **Key counts carry no audit trail.** Lyft's internal "pink" variations (Ch. 3, p. 70) and Vox Media's "almost three hundred" color variables (Ch. 4, pp. 97–98) are both given as bare numbers, with no citation to the underlying audit a reader could check independently of the author's account.
- **Cross-domain analogies stand in for evidence.** The NYC subway signage manual, Brasília's planning failure, Levittown against Concord Park, Stewart Brand's building-layers model, and Gall's law of working systems are all reached for to illustrate an abstract claim about software design systems (unity versus cohesion, planning without real use, layered rates of change, evolving from something simple), but none of these domains is itself a software design system, and no case connects an outcome in one of these domains to an outcome in a product's design system — the resemblance argues the point, not a measurement.
- **"Harmony" is asserted more than it is demonstrated.** The book names harmony as a third quality alongside unity and cohesion and calls it the target of the whole book (Ch. 3, p. 50), but where unity and cohesion are each given a clear failing case (the subway system before Vignelli and Noorda), every harmony example in the chapter is a positive one; no case shows a system with unity and cohesion that still lacks harmony, so the quality's own failure mode is never shown.
- **The book calls itself non-linear and not a step-by-step guide, then repeatedly gives numbered procedures.** The Introduction states the book is "not a step-by-step guide" (p. 5), yet it prescribes a six-step principles workshop (Ch. 1, pp. 16–17), a multi-step ecosystem-mapping process (Ch. 2, pp. 24–27), and Vox's own five-criteria extraction bar presented as a repeatable method (Ch. 2, pp. 35–36) — procedural in form, whatever the Introduction disclaims.
