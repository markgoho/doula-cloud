## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that a technological shift has ended the Industrial Revolution's employment model and opened an "Entrepreneur Revolution" in which one person, working through a small distributed team, can build a "global small business" that trades worldwide, earns seven-figure profits from a micro-niche, and is not tied to any location (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 13–15, 28–31). The author defines an entrepreneur as a person who spots an opportunity and turns it into a commercial success (Introduction, PDF p. 9). His claim is that the constraint on doing this is not capital, skill, or market conditions but a mindset installed by a school system built for factories, and that the mindset can be changed deliberately, by ten prescribed actions and by a set of frameworks for opportunity, product, team, and success (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 22, 27–28; Chapter 2, PDF p. 52; Chapter 3, PDF p. 56). The evidence offered is the author's own career from a university dropout at 19 to several companies, the clients of his accelerator business, and six named case studies (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 20–21; Chapter 5, PDF p. 127; Case Studies, PDF pp. 185–190).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a promise of easy money: entrepreneurship is described as a bumpy road that stretches a person past their means and their competence, and the reader is told not to start unless they accept being stretched, being resourceful, and being held accountable (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 52–55). It is not a case for becoming a billionaire; the author says explicitly that most people do not need to build a massive business to be living in the Entrepreneur Revolution, and that a lifestyle business with a healthy income qualifies (Chapter 4, PDF p. 92). It is not a book about brain science, and the author disclaims accuracy on that front in favor of usefulness (Chapter 2, PDF p. 36). It is not a claim that ideas matter: the author's position is the reverse, that implementation is the whole of the value (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 140–143). And it is not a book to be read once; the introduction and the closing page both ask the reader to re-read it and to look for an unnamed "ingredient" the author deliberately withholds, and he never names it outright, the closing chapter's statement that love is at the core of the Entrepreneur Revolution being the nearest the book comes (Introduction, PDF pp. 11–12; Chapter 8, PDF pp. 177, 180; PDF p. 184).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction (PDF pp. 9–12)

The conditions are described as the best in history for almost anyone to start a successful business, because the tools are cheap and global: a farmer in rural India is said to have more information and computing power than NASA had at the Voyager launches, and a teenager more tools for building a global enterprise than Coca-Cola had when it went international (Introduction, PDF p. 9). A London business is offered as the shape of the thing, with a developer in Uzbekistan, support in the Philippines, an accountant in India, and a salesperson in South Africa, none of whom have met (PDF p. 9). The introduction also sets up the book's one withheld device: an unnamed ingredient the reader is asked to hunt for across two or three readings, which the author says he will not identify (PDF pp. 11–12).

### Chapter 1: The Entrepreneur Revolution is unfolding (PDF pp. 13–35)

The historical argument runs agricultural age, Industrial Revolution, Entrepreneur Revolution, each ended by a technology that beat the previous era's source of advantage (PDF pp. 13–14). The mechanism offered for technology's effect is a lag: the telephone was invented in the late 1800s and took off in the 1920s, commercial air travel began in the 1920s and spread in the 1950s, television arrived in the 1930s and became a 1950s phenomenon, computers appeared in the late 1960s and sold in volume in the 1980s, and the author generalizes a twenty- to thirty-year gap between a technology and the systemic change it causes (PDF pp. 15–16). The recent chain is Tim Berners-Lee and the World Wide Web in 1989, Google making the web searchable in 1998, social media in 2004, the iPhone in 2007, cloud computing in 2008, and generative AI reaching the mainstream with over 100 million people starting to use ChatGPT in 2023 (PDF pp. 16–17).

Three inherited ideas are then dismantled one at a time: work hard now for a reward later, which the author answers with the claim that retirement systems are unlikely to stay solvent and that a great life is made of great days (PDF pp. 22–25); work is not meant to be fun, answered with the claim that hard work now signals functional rather than valuable work, because the value of anything functional trends to zero (PDF pp. 25–26); and work hard to prove how smart you are, answered with the claim that school punishes exactly the behaviors business rewards, and that the smart operator is the one with fast access to answers rather than the one holding them (PDF pp. 26–28). The chapter's central object is the global small business, defined as under 50 staff, unbound by geography, built on a micro-niche, carrying strong intellectual property and media, outsourcing or automating everything that is not its own way of creating value, and often generating seven-figure profits with a handful of people (PDF pp. 28–31). The chapter closes by dividing people into those surfing the wave and those clinging to the rocks, and argues that AI in particular will drive a wedge between people it helps create and people it keeps consuming (PDF pp. 32–33). A case study, Tom the fitness trainer, illustrates the shift: he moves to fewer, higher-paying clients and builds an online football-fitness community with three collaborators, ending the year at half the training clients paying twice as much (PDF pp. 34–35).

### Chapter 2: Awaken your entrepreneur mind (PDF pp. 36–55)

The chapter's model has three parts, offered as useful rather than neurologically accurate: the **reptile**, which sees danger and scarcity, cannot reason or plan, and is stimulated by gambling, pornography, junk food, and get-rich-quick schemes; the **autopilot**, the functional worker that repeats tasks and believes it can only access the resources it has been told about; and the **entrepreneur**, which has empathy, logic, and long-range reasoning, and believes any resource on the planet can be influenced regardless of who owns it (PDF pp. 36–42). The structural claim is that the lower parts can shut down the higher ones, so a person is only in the entrepreneur mind when they feel safe, uncluttered by tasks, and inspired (PDF p. 45). From this the author builds a claim about society: that the Industrial Revolution's institutions were arranged to keep people fed enough not to riot and busy enough not to think, and that mainstream media does the same work today (PDF pp. 45–46).

Seven ideas are named as reptile fantasies, each paired with the entrepreneur's version: passive income, retirement, multiple streams of income, big wins, entitlements, providers, and financial freedom (PDF pp. 43–44). Two written activities carry the chapter. The first asks the reader to name a non-greedy income for the next twelve months, then tells them that anything under a billion dollars is too small to be unselfish, because a figure that only satisfies the reader and their family is by definition about the reader and their family (PDF pp. 47–48). The second asks what the reader does for a living and argues that answering with a job title is two centuries of conditioning, since a job is not what keeps a person alive (PDF pp. 50–51). The chapter's contrarian line is that the poor mindset is the greedy one, because a person who believes money is scarce and earned by hard labor is easily bought, while a person with a rich mindset is indifferent to the money and interested only in the vision (PDF pp. 49–50). It closes with three admission requirements, stated as conditions rather than encouragements: a willingness to stretch, a willingness to get resourceful, and a willingness to be held accountable, the last of which needs an external party, because the autopilot brain will not supply it (PDF pp. 52–55).

### Chapter 3: Ten challenges to spark your entrepreneurial talents (PDF pp. 56–81)

Ten prescribed actions, each introduced with the author's own story of doing it. **Make three calls**: begin something before knowing how it will work, and make three calls to people who will either advance it or decline; the story is a teenage dance party that unfolded call by call into a nightclub venue, $4,000 of free radio advertising for naming rights, and over $1,000 of retail prizes (PDF pp. 57–59). **Get your reptile brain a bank account**: move 10% of all earnings into a separate account that is never spent on business or risk, kept in cash, property, shares, and metals, as a mental safety net; the author moves the surplus above roughly three months of living costs into a plain share fund (PDF pp. 59–61). **Stop spending time with people who bring you down** (PDF pp. 61–63). **Carry cash**: carry $1,000, or a day's target earnings, to recalibrate what the brain treats as a lot of money; the author's version was $2,000 in a bulldog clip, and he credits it for a jump from $1,200 a month to $12,000 a month in his first year working for a mentor (PDF pp. 63–65). **Take someone new out to lunch** each week and pay, as a way to build a network (PDF pp. 65–67). **Tune out from the news** for at least 90 days, on the definition that news is anything interesting that you cannot influence, which the author extends to podcasts about politics and videos about the economy (PDF pp. 67–69). **Keep an entrepreneur journal**, with a starter list of nine prompts (PDF pp. 70–71). **Plan inspiring holidays first**, blocking at least eight weeks at the start of the year and reverse-engineering the work around them, on three grounds: the brain relaxes about downtime, time away produces the big ideas, and a departure date creates a deadline (PDF pp. 71–73). **Get structured**: meet an accountant and a lawyer before the money arrives, because a wealth structure has to exist before there is wealth, and because these advisors answer instructions rather than volunteer strategies (PDF pp. 73–74). **Get your entrepreneurial team in place**: five roles around the table on day one, a visionary, a design specialist, a salesperson, a generalist, and an outside mentor, none of whom need be full-time employees (PDF pp. 75–76). The chapter ends by telling the reader to look underneath any challenge that feels uncomfortable for the belief making it so (PDF pp. 77–78), and with a case study, Sarah, who leaves a large corporation for a seven-person startup, spends two years there, and only then starts her own company with seed funding from her former employer (PDF pp. 80–81).

### Chapter 4: Finding your opportunity (PDF pp. 82–104)

The chapter's framework is **CAOS**: concept, audience, offer, sales, presented as four experiments rather than four plans (PDF p. 82). **Concept** is the "entrepreneur sweet spot", the intersection of something the reader is passionate about, something that solves a problem, and something that makes money; two out of three is stated as insufficient, with a named failure mode for each pair (PDF pp. 82–83). Passion is found through a "theme", a lifelong pattern the reader is asked to surface as a rant from one of four sentence stems; the examples are Richard Branson shaking up stale industries across 150 Virgin companies and Oprah Winfrey combining mass media with daily inspiration (PDF pp. 84–86). Problem-solving is framed as unmet need rather than a better version of something that already works, and the reader's approach to it is called intellectual property in a non-legal sense, surfaced by writing: blogs, case studies, brochures, and books, because writing slows the thinking down enough to see it (PDF pp. 88–91). Making money is treated with a financial forecast that is only directionally correct, plus three decisions: serve an affluent buyer rather than a frugal one, sell to a business rather than a consumer because lifetime value is higher and a business is incentivized to spend rather than pay tax on profit, and check the total addressable market and the share of it needed to hit the reader's target (PDF pp. 92–93).

**Audience** is separated from market, and the author says market size is mostly irrelevant: Apple had no PC market in 1977, YouTube no user-generated video market, Airbnb no market for renting a stranger's bedroom (PDF p. 94). The instruction is to build the audience before the product, with the Tesla CyberTruck reveal as the model: a prototype, a story, a stated wait of years, a $100 holding deposit, and the resulting list used as proof to raise the money for the factory (PDF pp. 94–95). Four signal campaigns are named: a waiting list, an online discussion group, an introduction event of 30 to 60 minutes, and an online assessment or quiz (PDF pp. 95–96). The stated conversion rule of thumb is that roughly 1 in 20 of an audience buys at once, so an audience needs to exceed 200 people for every 10 sales wanted at launch (PDF p. 96). Within the audience, the instruction is to ignore the larger crowd without money and talk to the minority who can spend, with the observation that small-budget customers typically make more demands than large-budget ones (PDF pp. 96–97).

**Offer** is defined as a package the customer can see and buy, assembled from whatever delivers the outcome, including other people's products; the iPhone with its 18 major and 200 second-tier suppliers, and a pizza restaurant's candlelit dinner, are the two illustrations (PDF pp. 97–98). The prescribed artifact is a brochure built before anything expensive: survey 150 people in the audience, interview at least 30, compile a specification list, return to 10 to 30 with basic diagrams, then write 500 to 1,500 words, have it designed, and print 50 copies (PDF p. 99). Property developers, technology companies, and event promoters are cited as selling things that do not yet exist from a brochure, with the caveat that money taken for an unbuilt product should be set aside for refund (PDF p. 100). **Sales** is presented as unavoidable and founder-led: the author sold ScoreApp face to face to well over 1,000 business owners at a 30-day free trial and $39 a month, and says the meetings produced the language, the objections, and the product changes that observing from a website would not have (PDF p. 101). The measurement instrument is the "Weekly LAPS Dashboard", tracking leads, appointments, presentations, and sales each week, improved by a list of three small changes per stage rather than a single fix (PDF pp. 102–103). The closing figure is a lead-to-sale conversion the author puts at under 5% across the data he sees, so roughly 20 warm leads are needed per sale, illustrated by his own presentation to 40 ideal prospects that produced 12 trials and 8 clients (PDF pp. 103–104).

### Chapter 5: Building your global small business (PDF pp. 105–128)

The chapter opens with a model, meaning a financial forecast over 24 to 60 months and a business plan, ideally built with an outside consultant who is not, in the author's phrase, drunk on the big picture (PDF pp. 105–106). A product is then defined as a consistent way of achieving an outcome the customer wants, so Rolex sells status rather than timekeeping against a $20 Casio, and a service business is told to make its work repeatable and to give it a name; two accountants are contrasted, one selling tax advice and one selling three named products (PDF pp. 107–108). Four product strategies are named as failures for a small business: **OOPS**, only one product or service, too dependent on brand and workable only at Zippo or Tabasco scale; the **J curve**, too dependent on capital, losing money for three to five years, the restaurant and the software company being the examples; the **one-stop shop**, too dependent on systems, with Amazon and Walmart as the scale at which it works; and the **brokerage model**, too dependent on time, which the author ran himself at seven-figure revenue and found unsaleable because he owned no asset (PDF pp. 108–114). The brokerage model is nonetheless recommended as the best way to start, because it teaches sales, marketing, and administration without product risk (PDF pp. 114–115).

The chapter's centerpiece is the **Ascending Transaction Model**, four product types with rules. **Gifts** are free, ask nothing back, not even contact details, must be meaningful, and must be cheap enough that giving them cannot bankrupt the business; the examples are sporting events sponsored by Rolex, Qantas, Credit Suisse, and Kia, and iTunes and Google's free apps (PDF pp. 116–117). **Products for prospects** are a low-cost first step exchanged for accurate contact details, giving a quick win preferably within seven days without cannibalizing the core product; the Porsche Experience Center's 90-minute track drive is the named example, and webinars, memberships, discussion groups, and quizzes are the small-business version (PDF pp. 117–118). The **core offer** is what the business is famous for, must be a remarkable solution, must never be sold at break-even or a loss, must be de-commoditized so it cannot be price-compared, and, absent funding, usually generates over $1,000 of revenue per customer per year (PDF pp. 118–120). **Products for clients** are sold to people who already bought the core offer, should aim to double the profitability of the business, must be genuinely different rather than more of the same, and must be a logical next step; subscriptions, memberships, software, insurance, and support are named as the best of them (PDF pp. 120–121).

Three pieces of "glue" link the four: a **lead capture process** between the gift and the product for prospects, with several ways to collect details; **sales conversations**, which the author says should not happen at all until the person has experienced a product for prospects; and a **servicing process** after the core sale, whose rule is to strategically undersell, hold surprises back, and never send a half-hearted newsletter, because a mediocre attempt at delight annoys rather than delights (PDF pp. 122–125). The claimed effect of the whole ecosystem is results the author reports from his client base: a personal trainer from £50k to £200k a year, a consultant from £80k to £500k in 20 months, and a charity from $2.5m to $4m in donations (PDF p. 127). The stated implementation time is six to twelve months, and the stated diagnostic value is binary: either the business takes off or the reader learns the idea is missing something fundamental, and until the model is in place there is no way to tell which (PDF pp. 127–128).

### Chapter 6: Your core team (PDF pp. 129–154)

Team structures are taken from the British Army: a **scout team** of two, one market-facing and one operational, to test an idea and build a minimum viable product; a **fire team** of four, a lead, a salesperson, an operator, and an administrator, to run a launch or a funding round and find product-market fit; a **section** of eight with two senior people, one outward-facing and one task-facing, to build a go-to-market approach; and a **platoon** of 30 to 50 under a leadership team of three to six, to execute that strategy and dominate a niche (PDF pp. 129–133). Above 150 people the author says the organization stops being entrepreneurial and needs governance, which he defers (PDF p. 133).

Culture is then handled through seven maxims. **You get what you pitch for, and you are always pitching**, illustrated by Toby Milden pitching his way from a school that told him he might peel eggs to consulting for FTSE100 companies, and by Jeremy Gilley pitching a fixed day of peace from 1999 to a United Nations resolution in 2001; the negative version is that repeating a complaint installs it just as reliably (PDF pp. 134–135). **Influence comes from output, not confidence**, supported by Whitney Houston, Kurt Cobain, John Candy, and Amy Winehouse as influential and insecure, and by output counts for the Beatles, Steven Spielberg, Oprah Winfrey, and Steve Jobs (PDF pp. 135–137). **Income follows assets**, where an asset is anything still valuable if the owner were hit by a bus; the author's own case is a business he considered selling for £300k, valued at £4m twelve months after his team spent a year documenting what it knew (PDF pp. 137–138). **Get known by the success of your clients**, with video case studies as the mechanism (PDF pp. 138–139). **You are in partnership with everyone who touches your business**, contrasted against companies that stretched supplier payment terms during the pandemic, with Waitrose as the counter-example (PDF pp. 139–140). **Ideas are worthless, implementation is everything**, argued from Pret A Manger as a dull idea executed well and Microsoft's 2002 tablet against Apple's 2010 iPad as a good idea executed poorly, and extended into a claim that the word creative has been wrongly detached from making things, with imaginative proposed for people who only have ideas (PDF pp. 140–143). **Resources go to the resourceful**, whose mechanism is that commitment precedes resourcefulness rather than following it (PDF pp. 143–144).

The **value creation cycle** has five stages: ideas, ranked by impact and cost; testing, by survey, quiz, introduction event, mock-up brochure, or waiting list; a beta version, deliberately cheap and possibly a white-labeled competitor product, aimed at product-market fit; a commercial version, which the author warns will feel disappointing because it barely pays a wage and is where most people restart the cycle instead of pushing through; and a remarkable and scalable version, which takes considerably more energy than everything before it (PDF pp. 144–147). The chapter closes with a seven-level journey. Three levels of work: newbie, worker, and self-employed, where the self-employed person carries the old job plus every job they never considered. Three levels of play: key person of influence, campaign-driven enterprise, and global small business; the campaign-driven enterprise is flagged as making large money but collapsing the month the promotions stop. And a bonus level, the Making a Difference Enterprise, which the author says cannot be reached early: people who skip to it turn altruism into functional work and end up resenting the cause (PDF pp. 147–152). A case study, Adrian, runs a web agency for thirteen years, opens a waiting list that reaches 700 registrations, launches software at $150 a month, and crosses 2,000 paying clients and $300,000 a month of revenue by year three with a team of ten (PDF pp. 153–154).

### Chapter 7: Three mindsets for an AI-enabled world (PDF pp. 155–167)

Artificial intelligence is presented as a general purpose technology on the scale of steam, steel, and the combustion engine, and the chapter's premise is that it will do at marginal cost much of what lawyers, doctors, engineers, architects, animators, editors, auditors, accountants, and developers do (PDF p. 155). The historical analogy is Isaac Singer's mass-produced sewing machine dropping a dress shirt from 14 hours of a tailor's work to about an hour, and dropping the skill needed with it (PDF p. 155). Three shifts follow. **Vitality versus functionality**: vital is given two senses, irreplaceable and life-force, and the instruction is to automate yourself up rather than out, which the author sets directly against the common advice to keep your personality out of the business so it can run without you (PDF pp. 156–158). **Context versus content**: a large language model has read everything and wants for nothing but a goal, so the scarce thing is the context a person supplies, and spotting the problem worth solving becomes more valuable than knowing solutions to solved problems; the personal version is an inner compass, argued through Marcus Aurelius declining the excesses available to an emperor (PDF pp. 159–161). **Creating versus consuming**: AI has two powers, to make people consume more than they intended and to make them create more than they could, and the author says the only way to beat the first is to remove the apps from the phone (PDF p. 161). The consuming side is argued from lottery winners, whom the author says mostly become depressed, and from the Forbes list of self-made billionaires, few of whom are retired; the creating side from Steve Jobs choosing to keep building after a cancer diagnosis (PDF p. 162). The chapter ends with a snowboarding metaphor: a rider who leans back loses control, and leaning in to disruption is what gives stability, so an exit strategy sought before the business is genuinely valuable is treated as leaning back (PDF pp. 165–167).

### Chapter 8: The very essence of your success (PDF pp. 168–184)

The chapter opens by rejecting the usual trait list. Self-belief, determination, passion, hard work, and decisiveness are dismissed not as false but as non-discriminating: the author says he can find all of them in an Uber driver working 70 hours a week and a children's entertainer at another party (PDF pp. 168–169). Three keys are offered instead. **Luck**, which the author says every successful person has had, and which can be cultivated by recognizing the luck already present; the long personal passage is a day in Mumbai between a slum school and a five-star hotel's water features, and the practical version is a series of choices ranked by how lucky they are, an event over a repetitive day at the office, a book over television, a disgruntled customer taken for coffee over a complaint ignored (PDF pp. 169–173). **Reputation**, named as the most prized asset, measured by the first page of a search for your name, argued through Tiger Woods losing over $25m in contracts and $100m in career earnings, and through quoted lines from Warren Buffett and Richard Branson; the author's own case is that his first book led to media, paid speaking, offered equity, and the publisher approaching him for this one (PDF pp. 173–175). **Leverage**, which the author says must be gated to be worth anything: YouTube, the Kindle Store, Spotify, ChatGPT, and Google are all named as real leverage that confers no advantage because everyone has it, while celebrity endorsements, venture capital, distribution deals, genuine mentoring, and access to unique data are gated, and reputation exists largely to qualify a person to ask for them (PDF pp. 175–177).

The closing argument is the book's withheld ingredient. Three ages are set out: the **age of hands**, ended when machines beat human dexterity; the **age of heads**, in which Carnegie, Rockefeller, J. P. Morgan, Onassis, then Walton, Kamprad, Gates, and Ellison out-thought their rivals, ended when computers beat human thinking; and the **age of hearts**, arriving now alongside a "technology of intimacy", which is what the author says social networks actually are (PDF pp. 178–180). The stated conclusion is that all the doing innovation and all the thinking innovation have been done, so what is left is to come into an industry with more love than anyone else, and that the companies that do well will be the ones that discuss love in the boardroom (PDF pp. 180–182). The author predicts the same displacement as the previous transitions: people whose heads are no longer needed and whose hearts were never trained (PDF p. 181).

### Case Studies and back matter (PDF pp. 185–201)

Six entrepreneurs are profiled, each in the pattern of a passion turned into a niche product ecosystem: Adèle Thèron and the naked divorce method, Jeremy Harbour doing deals from a boat in Dubai after selling his telecommunications business at 34, Pat Lynes and the consultancy Sullivan & Stanley, Seb Bates and The Warrior Academy with over 30,000 students after a base-jumping accident, Sara Milne Rowe and Coaching Impact with a book on sleep, hydration, exercise, and diet, and Jacqui Sharples, an engineer turned fitness trainer serving corporate women in their 30s (PDF pp. 185–190). What Next? is promotional and reprints the opening of the author's earlier book *Oversubscribed*: price is set by demand and supply and a business can manufacture its own, illustrated by two bidders taking a charity auction for an hour of Gary Vaynerchuk's time from £500 to £4,000 each in a room of 400, and by Facebook paying $19 billion for WhatsApp with Google named as the rival bidder; the general rule is that a business is profitable only when oversubscribed, and that some people therefore have to miss out (What Next?, PDF pp. 194–199).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

- **The founder.** The book's own framing says entrepreneurship is about the team rather than the founder, that founders get too much credit, and that high-performing teams exist because the risk-taking is shared by collaborators and not by whoever registered the company (Chapter 1, PDF p. 18). There is also no self-made millionaire in the author's account: everyone so described is surrounded by people who get things done (Chapter 3, PDF p. 76).
- **The brain model.** The three-part brain is offered as useful to entrepreneurs rather than accurate to brain scientists, with an apology in advance for oversimplifying (Chapter 2, PDF p. 36).
- **The billion-dollar activity.** Immediately after telling the reader that anything under a billion is too small, the author states his own answer differently: the most money he can receive as a result of adding value and being true to his purpose, and if that turns out to be a small amount while he stays self-governing and free, he will accept that too (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 48–49).
- **How much money is enough.** Research is cited for money making people happier only up to a point, after which how the money is made matters more; writing down millions or billions as a happiness target is called a recipe for lifelong disappointment, and a lifestyle business with a healthy income and freedom is stated to be enough to be living in the Entrepreneur Revolution (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 91–92).
- **Who should not start.** The three admissions are stated as conditions, not encouragements: do not embark on the journey without a willingness to stretch, to get resourceful, and to be held accountable (Chapter 2, PDF p. 52). Entrepreneurship is explicitly not a ticket to riches, and anyone gloating online about an easy life is said to have paid for it somewhere (Chapter 2, PDF p. 54).
- **Not starting with a business.** The reader is told they do not need to begin by starting a business; the author's own examples begin with a mentor, a small campaign, a sales job, or work alongside a founder, and he says confidence is usually built in small steps first (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 78–79). The book's second case study is a woman who joins a seven-person startup for two years before founding anything (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 80–81).
- **Not reckless.** On the first challenge the author says he is not telling the reader to be reckless, only that it is impossible to know how things will unfold before beginning (Chapter 3, PDF p. 58).
- **What lawyers and accountants can and cannot do.** The structure challenge carries its own limit: these advisors mostly cannot advise on avoiding tax or obligations, especially after the fact, because it exposes them to risk, so the reader has to know what to instruct them to do (Chapter 3, PDF p. 74).
- **Every failing product strategy has a case where it works.** OOPS works behind a brand the size of Zippo or Tabasco (Chapter 5, PDF p. 108). The J curve is very profitable and very valuable for anyone who survives the dip, and is called an advanced strategy for entrepreneurs who can access capital rather than a bad one (PDF p. 111). The one-stop shop works with bulletproof systems, which is what Amazon and Walmart have (PDF pp. 111–112). And the brokerage model, whose flaw is that it builds no asset, is nonetheless named as by far the best way to start out in business, because two years of it teaches sales, marketing, and administration at low risk (PDF p. 114).
- **The core-offer price floor is conditional.** The figure of over $1,000 of revenue per customer per year carries an explicit condition: unless the business has funding (Chapter 5, PDF p. 120). Losing small money is allowed on gifts and on products for prospects, and only the core offer must never be sold at break-even (PDF pp. 118, 120).
- **The model is slow.** The Ascending Transaction Model is said to take time, with clients typically needing six to twelve months to get it right, and it is explicitly not an overnight phenomenon (Chapter 5, PDF p. 128).
- **Partnership does not mean permanence.** The maxim about treating everyone as a partner is qualified in the same section: a poor-performing staff member can still be fired and a supply deal can still end when the alignment is gone (Chapter 6, PDF p. 139).
- **The commercial version is supposed to disappoint.** The value creation cycle warns that traction is harder than expected and that the commercial version often barely pays a wage, and names restarting the cycle at that point as the common mistake rather than a signal (Chapter 6, PDF p. 146).
- **The levels can be crossed quickly.** Levels must not be skipped, but the author says a person need not spend years or decades at each one the way most people do (Chapter 6, PDF p. 152).
- **Automation is not the disagreement.** On vitality, the author agrees with the advice he is arguing against on its substance: functional tasks that could be automated or systematized should not be done by the reader. His disagreement is only about the purpose, which he says is to become more irreplaceable rather than less needed (Chapter 7, PDF p. 156).
- **Luck is not only cultivated.** Before the passage on making yourself luckier, the reader is told they are already born lucky if they have an education, money for books, and time to read (Chapter 8, PDF p. 170).
- **Leverage comes with rejection.** A first approach to a gatekeeper is expected to go unanswered, and the author labels that not a refusal but a not-this-time (Chapter 8, PDF p. 176).
- **Market size cuts both ways.** Alongside the claim that market size is mostly irrelevant, the author says some markets are huge and still very unlikely to be entered, naming a new luxury watch brand and a burger restaurant chain (Chapter 4, PDF p. 94).
- **Money taken for an unbuilt product.** The brochure-first instruction carries a refund rule: if payment is taken for something not yet secured, set the money aside so it can be returned (Chapter 4, PDF p. 100).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter it raises. This book is a mindset and product-strategy book rather than a SaaS operations book, so most of it meets nothing in this repo: the brain model, the ten challenges, the seven maxims, the seven levels, the three ages, and the three AI mindsets have no counterpart in an ADR, a ticket, or a screen, and they are absent here for that reason rather than by omission.

1. **A waiting list is named first among four campaigns that test whether an audience exists, before anything is built** (Chapter 4, PDF p. 95). Record: [ADR-0014](../../adr/0014-the-waitlist-lives-outside-our-stack.md), which states that the pre-launch teaser "collects an email address and a first name, sends one confirmation email carrying the pilot-group offer, and broadcasts once in January 2027", and that the list is kept in Buttondown rather than in Postgres behind the BFF, on sending-domain reputation and on the build list underneath a public write endpoint.

Record: [ADR-0016](../../adr/0016-teaser-analytics-are-cookieless-and-the-channel-rides-on-the-form.md): "A subscriber's channel is carried by a hidden field on the form, not by the analytics", because Pirsch cannot identify a person by design and Buttondown never sees the address bar the form was on; the teaser carries a hidden `metadata__source` input filled from `utm_source`.

4. **Roughly 1 in 20 of an audience buys at once, so an audience has to exceed 200 people for every 10 sales wanted at launch, and the lead-to-sale rate the author sees across industries is under 5%** (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 96, 104). Revisit after January."

10. **The ninth challenge is to meet an accountant and a lawyer before the money arrives, because the book says a wealth structure has to exist before there is wealth, and that an entrepreneur pays the minimum tax legally required and no more** (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 73–74). All three are no."

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, company, or example the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes it. Where the book reports a figure from an outside source, the book's number is compared against that source's own number rather than against the direction of travel.

- **Northern Rock: Richard Branson's involvement in the failing bank is said to have helped to turn it around (Chapter 8, PDF p. 174).** Wrong as stated, on the sequence. Wikipedia's Northern Rock article records that "The bank was nationalised at 00:01 on 22 February 2008" after the private bids, Virgin's among them, failed; Virgin Money did not acquire the bank until much later, when "On 17 November 2011, it was announced that Virgin Money was going to buy Northern Rock plc for £747 million", completing 1 January 2012 with a further £73 million of deferred consideration (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Rock). The reputation the book credits with the rescue did not win the 2007 bid, and the state owned the bank for nearly four years in between. <!-- spelling:ignore: "nationalised" is inside a verbatim quotation of the Wikipedia article cited on this line -->
- **Pret A Manger: hundreds of stores, and the business "worth tens of millions of pounds" (Chapter 6, PDF p. 141).** Understated by roughly two orders of magnitude, and already so before this edition. JAB Holding acquired Pret in September 2018 for £1.5 billion, and the chain is recorded at 717 locations across 19 countries as of September 2025 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pret_a_Manger). The example's point, that a dull idea executed well becomes valuable, survives; the valuation printed beside it does not.
- **The CyberTruck: over a million people are said to have put down a "holding deposit" of $100 each, adding to more than $100 million (Chapter 4, PDF p. 94).** Partly checked, and the checkable half is smaller. Wikipedia records the deposit as "a refundable US$100 deposit" from November 2019, and the only company-stated reservation figure as Elon Musk's announcement of "250,000 preorders in the five days since the unveiling" on November 26, 2019 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_Cybertruck). The million figure has no company statement behind it in that source; later totals circulated from crowdsourced reservation trackers rather than from Tesla, so the million and the $100 million derived from it are unchecked.
- **ChatGPT: more than 100 million people are said to have started using it in 2023 (Chapter 1, PDF p. 17).** Holds, slightly late on the date. Wikipedia records the release as November 30, 2022, and that "ChatGPT gained one million users in five days and 100 million in two months", which places the hundred-million mark at the end of January 2023 rather than across the year (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChatGPT).
- **Cloud computing is dated to 2008 and social media to 2004 (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 16–17).** Both are late. Wikipedia records that "On March 14, 2006, AWS launched Amazon S3 cloud storage, and that was the first AWS' IaaS that was generally available", with EC2 following in August of the same year (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Web_Services). On social media it records that "SixDegrees.com, launched in 1997, is often regarded as the first social media site", and that "Social media started in the mid-1990s with the invention of platforms like Classmates.com, and SixDegrees.com" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_media). The chapter is dating mass adoption rather than invention, which is consistent with its own lag argument, but it labels both as births.
- **Steve Jobs is credited with "317 patents" (Chapter 6, PDF p. 136).** Out of date. Wikipedia states that "Since his death, he has been granted 141 patents; Jobs holds over 450 patents in total" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Jobs). The book's figure is close to the count at his death and roughly 130 short of the current one.
- **Oprah Winfrey is credited with "4561 episodes" of her talk show (Chapter 6, PDF p. 136).** Exact. Wikipedia's article on the show states that it "ran in syndication for twenty-five seasons from September 8, 1986, to May 25, 2011, in which it broadcast 4,561 episodes" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oprah_Winfrey_Show).
- **Jeremy Gilley began pitching a day of peace in 1999 and by 2001 saw a unanimous United Nations resolution fixing 21 September, now celebrated by over 100 million people a year (Chapter 6, PDF p. 134).** The founding and the resolution hold; the two numbers around them do not check out cleanly. Peace One Day was "founded in 1999 by British documentary filmmaker and actor Jeremy Gilley" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_One_Day), and the fixed date came from General Assembly "Resolution 55/282" of "7 September 2001", "sponsored by the United Kingdom (giving credit to Peace One Day) and Costa Rica" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Day_of_Peace). Neither source states the vote was unanimous. The organization's own reported figures are of a different kind from the book's: for 2016, "2.2 billion people were exposed to the Peace Day message, 940 million were fully aware of the day, resulting in around 16 million behaving more peacefully". The book's 100 million sits between the awareness figure and the behavior figure and matches neither, so it is unchecked.
- **ScoreApp sold face to face as a 30-day free trial and then "$39 per month", cancelable at any time (Chapter 4, PDF p. 101).** The price holds. ScoreApp's live pricing page lists Starter at $39 a month, or $30 billed annually, above Business at $99, Pro at $149, and Enterprise from $799, with a free tier of one live scorecard and ten responses a month (scoreapp.com/pricing). What has changed is the entry point: the book's trial is now a free plan, and the company's site positions the product around AI-powered scorecards rather than quizzes.
- **ScoreApp as "the global leader for quiz marketing" (Chapter 1, PDF p. 21) and as "a leading-edge marketing software" (About the Author, PDF p. 193).** The company does not make the global-leader claim on its own home page today, which describes the product as helping a business attract, engage, and convert an audience (scoreapp.com). No independent market-share source was found, so the leadership claim is unchecked.
- **Rethink Press as a niche publisher that has become "the UK's top producer" of non-fiction books (Chapter 1, PDF p. 21).** Not a claim the company makes. Rethink Press's own site states "With more than 1000 business books published to date" and describes its services and categories without any market-position claim (rethinkpress.com). The top-producer ranking is unchecked.
- **Warren Buffett as a billionaire working into his 90s "without giving a thought to retirement" (Chapter 7, PDF p. 157).** Overtaken. Wikipedia records that on May 3, 2025 Buffett asked the board to appoint Greg Abel as his successor, and that the company announced on May 5, 2025 "Abel as president and CEO, effective January 1, 2026, with Buffett remaining chairman" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Buffett). He was 94 at the announcement. The narrower point, that he did not leave the business, is preserved by the chairmanship.
- **Rupert Murdoch, same claim, same page (Chapter 7, PDF p. 157).** Already overtaken when this edition was published. Wikipedia records that "In September 2023, Murdoch announced he would be stepping down as chairman of Fox Corp. and News Corp." at the age of 92 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Murdoch), which is the year before the 2024 edition.
- **Cathy Burke as the current CEO of the Hunger Project Australia (Chapter 7, PDF p. 157).** No longer true, and not true at publication. The organization's own team page states that "Philippe joined The Hunger Project Australia as Chief Executive Officer in May 2022" and lists no Cathy Burke (thp.org.au/teams). The revenue growth attributed to her, from a few hundred thousand dollars to millions, is unchecked.
- **The British Army is said to have been functioning for "about 400 years" (Chapter 6, PDF p. 129).** Overstated on either reckoning. Wikipedia gives the British Army's founding as 1 April 1707, at the Acts of Union, which is 319 years ago (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Army); the English standing army from which it descends dates to the Restoration of 1660, 366 years ago. Neither reaches 400. The unit sizes the chapter takes from the army, a four-person fire team, an eight-person section, and a 30-to-50-person platoon, are not covered by that article and are unchecked there.
- **Most lottery winners are said to "become depressed and unhappy", offered as "a widely known statistical fact" (Chapter 7, PDF p. 162).** Contradicted by the research. Wikipedia's lottery article reports a National Bureau of Economic Research study of large Swedish winners who "tended to retain their wealth over a period of 10 years, often kept their jobs but took more vacation, and maintained or increased their happiness and mental health", and separately records that the familiar claim that 70% of people who receive a large sum lose it within a few years "is false, and is falsely attributed to the National Endowment for Financial Education (NEFE)" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lottery).
- **Facebook paid $19 billion for WhatsApp with Google as the other bidder pushing the price up (What Next?, PDF p. 196).** The price holds, the rival-bidder story is not supported by the source. Wikipedia records the acquisition at approximately US$19 billion on February 19, 2014, structured as "$4 billion in cash, $12 billion in Facebook shares, and an additional $3 billion in restricted stock units granted to WhatsApp's founders". On Google it records something different in kind and four years earlier: "In 2010 Google made multiple acquisition offers for WhatsApp, which were all declined" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WhatsApp). No source was found for two companies bidding each other up in 2014, so the mechanism the anecdote illustrates is unchecked on this example.
- **Vidal Sassoon as "the world's highest paid hairdresser ever" (Chapter 4, PDF p. 84).** Not stated by the source. Wikipedia's article makes no such ranking; the financial figure it does carry is that "Sassoon's 1982 sales of hair products had topped $110 million, with 80 percent of revenues coming from the US", and it records that he had divested his business interests by the early 1980s and that Richardson-Vicks bought the company in 1983 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vidal_Sassoon). The superlative is unchecked.
- **Tiger Woods losing "over $25m in lost contracts" in the short term and "$100m in career earnings" (Chapter 8, PDF p. 174).** Unchecked. Wikipedia's article on Woods states only that "During this period, several companies ended their endorsement deals with Woods" and prints no figures for the losses (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_Woods); no primary study reporting those two numbers was reached.
- **Not checked.** Ecademy.com and its 500,000-plus members, the sale of SOTechnology to a publicly listed US AI company, and the state of BookMagic.ai (Chapter 1, PDF p. 21); the £50k-to-£200k trainer, the £80k-to-£500k consultant, and the $2.5m-to-$4m charity attributed to the Ascending Transaction Model (Chapter 5, PDF p. 127); the author's £300k-to-£4m valuation swing (Chapter 6, PDF p. 138); Toby Milden's career (Chapter 6, PDF p. 134); Ali Abdaal's team of ten making millions in revenue (Chapter 4, PDF p. 92); Esther Perel as the world's most expensive couples therapist (Chapter 4, PDF p. 97); Isaac Singer's sewing machine cutting a dress shirt from 14 hours to about an hour (Chapter 7, PDF p. 155); Richard Branson's 150 Virgin companies (Chapter 4, PDF p. 85) — Wikipedia's Virgin Group article gives revenue of "£16.6 billion (2019)" but states no company count, and says an exact count is hard to determine because many ventures are brand licenses rather than holdings (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Group); the 18 major and 200 second-tier iPhone suppliers (Chapter 4, PDF p. 98); Spielberg's 20 screenplays, 50 films, and close to 200 productions (Chapter 6, PDF p. 136); the six case-study businesses, including Sullivan & Stanley, for which the URL tried did not resolve, and The Warrior Academy's 30,000-plus students, whose site returned no readable content (Case Studies, PDF pp. 185–190); and the Gary Vaynerchuk auction, which is the author's first-hand account with no outside record (What Next?, PDF pp. 194–196).

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Correlation presented as cause.** The clearest case is the author's own: a business he considered selling for £300k is valued at £4m twelve months after his team spent a year producing documents, media assets, posters, and workbooks, and the documents are credited with the swing, with no other change in the business named and no counterfactual offered (Chapter 6, PDF p. 138). The Ascending Transaction Model results are the same shape: a trainer from £50k to £200k, a consultant from £80k to £500k in 20 months, and a charity from $2.5m to $4m in donations, each attributed to the model alone (Chapter 5, PDF p. 127). Carrying $2,000 in a pocket is credited with a tenfold jump in monthly earnings in the author's first year, in a year that also included a new mentor, a new employer, and a new peer group (Chapter 3, PDF pp. 63–65).
- **Figures with no source.** The audience arithmetic, 1 in 20 buying and 200 people needed per 10 sales, carries no citation (Chapter 4, PDF p. 96). The lead-to-sale figure of under 5% is sourced only to the author having access to a lot of data across many industries, with no dataset, period, or industry named (PDF p. 104). The core-offer threshold of over $1,000 per customer per year (Chapter 5, PDF p. 120), the 10% into the reptile account (Chapter 3, PDF p. 60), the eight weeks of holiday (PDF p. 72), and the twenty- to thirty-year lag between a technology and its systemic effect (Chapter 1, PDF p. 16) are all rules of thumb from experience presented as findings.
- **Dated and incorrect facts.** Four are established in section 7: Branson's involvement is credited with turning Northern Rock around when the bank was nationalized after the Virgin bid failed and Virgin Money bought it nearly four years later (Chapter 8, PDF p. 174); Pret A Manger is valued at tens of millions of pounds six years after it sold for £1.5 billion (Chapter 6, PDF p. 141); the British Army is given about 400 years when its founding date is 1707 (Chapter 6, PDF p. 129); and Cathy Burke is named as the current CEO of the Hunger Project Australia two years after that post passed to someone else (Chapter 7, PDF p. 157). Two more are loose rather than wrong, and section 7 carries the sources: cloud computing is dated to 2008 and social media to 2004, both years after the thing being dated (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 16–17).
- **An example overtaken while the edition was in production.** Rupert Murdoch is offered as a billionaire still working with no thought of retirement, and he announced he was stepping down as chairman of both companies in September 2023, the year before this edition was published (Chapter 7, PDF p. 157). Warren Buffett, on the same page, followed in 2025.
- **An activity the reader cannot pass.** The income exercise instructs the reader to name a figure that is not greedy or selfish, then tells them that anything under a billion proves they are too greedy and selfish, on a redefinition of greed introduced only after the answer is written (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 47–48). The reader who follows the stated instruction is guaranteed to fail it, and the author's own answer, given two pages later, is a figure he explicitly declines to name and would accept as small (PDF pp. 48–49).
- **Internal tension.** That activity also sits against Chapter 4, which calls writing down millions or billions as a financial target a recipe for lifelong disappointment (PDF p. 92). Market size is called mostly irrelevant to an entrepreneur one page after the reader is told to size the total addressable market and the share of it they need (Chapter 4, PDF pp. 93–94). The global small business is under 50 staff in Chapter 1 (PDF p. 28) and between 5 and 50 in Chapter 6 (PDF p. 150). Entrepreneurs are said never to retire and often to stop working weeks before they die (Chapter 2, PDF p. 44), a claim the book's own two examples of it no longer support. And the brokerage model is one of four strategies named as not working for a small business and, ten pages later, by far the best way to start out in business (Chapter 5, PDF pp. 113–114).
- **Claims the book's evidence does not reach.** The account of the Industrial Revolution as a system deliberately arranged to keep people out of their higher mind, with social security and pensions as instruments for preventing unrest and mainstream media as the mechanism that keeps it working, is asserted across two pages with no source, no named actor, and no evidence (Chapter 2, PDF pp. 45–46). The three-part brain is used throughout the book as a causal model of behavior and spending while carrying a disclaimer that it is not accurate (PDF p. 36). The claim that most lottery winners become depressed, offered as a widely known statistical fact, is contradicted by the research summarized in section 7 (Chapter 7, PDF p. 162). The claim that the value of anything functional trends to zero (Chapter 1, PDF p. 26) is repeated as a premise in Chapter 7 without ever being shown.
- **A sample with no failures in it.** Every case study is drawn from the author's own circle: the six profiles are clients or friends of his accelerator business, the chapter-closing vignettes are unattributed, and the acknowledgments name the same network of mentors, partners, and clients (Case Studies, PDF pp. 185–190; PDF pp. 191–192). No person who followed the ten challenges or built an Ascending Transaction Model and did not succeed appears anywhere in the book, so the method is only ever observed on people it worked for.
- **Unattributed vignettes beside named profiles.** Tom the fitness trainer, Sarah the corporate employee, and Adrian the web developer are each set in a box labeled Case Study, in the same format and the same typographic treatment as the six named real entrepreneurs at the back, with no statement that they are illustrations rather than people (Chapter 1, PDF pp. 34–35; Chapter 3, PDF pp. 80–81; Chapter 6, PDF pp. 153–154). Adrian's box carries the most specific numbers in the book, a 700-person waiting list, $150 a month, 2,000 clients, and $300,000 a month by year three, which a reader has no way to place.
- **The argument routes through the author's own products.** Eight links point to his companies: the case-study videos, the mindset quiz, the maxims talk, the asset diagnostic, the community, the Key Person of Influence scorecard, and a review-for-a-free-ticket offer (Introduction, PDF p. 12; Chapter 2, PDF p. 52; Chapter 6, PDF p. 144; PDF p. 138; Case Studies, PDF p. 190; Chapter 8, PDF p. 175; What Next?, PDF p. 194). One of the four signal campaigns is illustrated by naming his own software as where to set it up (Chapter 4, PDF p. 96), and the book's final eight pages are an excerpt from another of his books (What Next?, PDF pp. 194–201). The commercial interest is never disclosed at the point of use.
- **A withheld premise.** The reader is told on the second page that a business cannot be built successfully without an ingredient the author will not name, and is asked to hunt for it across repeated readings; the final page repeats the device and still declines to name it, and the nearest the book comes is the closing chapter's statement that love is at the core of the Entrepreneur Revolution (Introduction, PDF pp. 11–12; Chapter 8, PDF pp. 177, 180; PDF p. 184). The stated necessary condition for success is therefore never put in a form that can be examined, agreed with, or argued against.
- **Copyediting.** The book carries a visible cluster of errors in proper nouns and its own terms: Steven Spielberg appears as Stephen, Philippe Starck as Stark, Kendall Jenner as Kendal, Jennifer Lawrence as Jenifer, Isaac Singer as Issac, and Warren Buffett with one t (Chapter 6, PDF pp. 136, 142; Chapter 8, PDF p. 168; Chapter 7, PDF pp. 155, 157). The Industrial Revolution becomes the Industrial Revelation (Chapter 1, PDF p. 27), and the CAOS framework introduced in Chapter 4 is called COAS on the first page of Chapter 5 (PDF pp. 82, 105).
