## 3. The claim

The book's thesis is that a startup is not a small version of a large company, and that what a startup does in its early life is not execute a known business model but search for one (Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF p. 35). The authors define a startup as a temporary organization in search of a scalable, repeatable, profitable business model (Strategy Guide, Introduction, PDF pp. 17 and 34), and Manifesto Rule 12 reads that definition apart: preserve cash while searching, and spend only once the model is found; repeatable means a pattern a sales organization or a website can replay, rather than one-off revenue from a board member's contacts; scalable means that one more salesperson or one more marketing dollar returns more gross profit than it cost (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF p. 88).

Against that they set what they call the new-product introduction model, the product-centered sequence of concept, development, alpha and beta test, and launch, which they say was borrowed from large companies that already knew their customer and their market (Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF pp. 36-42). Its failure is enumerated as nine deadly sins, the first of which is the assumption that the founders know what the customer wants (Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF pp. 41-53).

The remedy is Customer Development, four steps in two halves. The search half is customer discovery, which turns the founders' guesses into facts by testing them on people outside the building, and customer validation, which tries to show the model repeatable and scalable by selling. The execute half is customer creation and company-building (Strategy Guide, Ch. 2, PDF pp. 54-70). Each step ends at a pivot-or-proceed gate, and the model draws its arrows backward as well as forward, so a step whose test fails returns to an earlier step instead of continuing (Strategy Guide, Ch. 2, PDF p. 63).

The Customer Development Manifesto states the process as fourteen rules, opening with the rule that there are no facts inside the building (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 71-87). The business model canvas is the artifact the search runs on: nine boxes of guesses that the founders redraw each time they iterate or pivot, forming a flip book of successive versions (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 75-78), and the canvas is described as a reference for the team and a scorecard of what has been learned (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 238).

What the authors say the claim is not. It is not a substitute for building the product: Manifesto Rule No. 2 pairs Customer Development with agile engineering, so the product is built in parallel rather than deferred (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF p. 72). It is not focus-group research and not an exercise in tallying feature requests and voting on them; the authors say its purpose is to inform the founders' vision, and that a founder may consider the data and still decide to set it aside (Strategy Guide, Ch. 5, PDF p. 124). Nor is it a task for junior staff: the authors say the work must be driven by the founders (Strategy Guide, Ch. 4, PDF p. 97). It does not reduce invention risk: the authors mark out markets where the open question is whether the technology can be made to work at all, name biotech and life sciences among them, and say those are solved by simulation rather than by this book (Strategy Guide, Introduction, PDF pp. 19-20). And it does not cover the execute half: at the close of customer validation the book sends the reader back to the authors' earlier *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* for customer creation and company-building (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 169).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### The Strategy Guide (PDF pp. 13-169)

**Preface, Who Is This Book For, Introduction (PDF pp. 13-34).** The authors date the modern company to 1602 and the first MBA to 1908, and argue that management education produced executives trained to administer companies that already exist, leaving the startup without a corresponding body of practice (PDF p. 13). They name five kinds of startup the process serves, including the scalable startup and the social-enterprise startup, and say it helps the scalable startup most (PDF p. 19). They then name who the book is not for: ventures whose risk is invention rather than customer or market (PDF pp. 19-20). The introduction claims a repeatable path now exists, made of Customer Development paired with agile engineering (PDF pp. 23, 34).

**Chapter 1, The Path to Disaster (PDF pp. 35-53).** The chapter's claim is that the product-introduction model taken from large companies destroys startups, and its evidence is the Webvan case, which the authors say raised $400 million, reached an $8.5 billion market capitalization, and shut down two years after its 1999 IPO (PDF pp. 36-42). The failure is set out as nine deadly sins: assuming the founders know what the customer wants; the flawed step-by-step introduction model; execution instead of learning and discovery; the lack of meaningful milestones for sales, marketing and business development; the business plan with no provision for trial and error; confusing traditional job titles with a startup's needs; executing sales and marketing to plan; presumption of success leading to premature scaling; and management by crisis, which leads to a death spiral (PDF pp. 41-53).

**Chapter 2, The Path to the Epiphany (PDF pp. 54-70).** The chapter presents Customer Development as a parallel process to product development, with four steps in two halves: customer discovery and customer validation, which together form the search; customer creation and company-building, which form the execution (PDF pp. 54-60). Customer Development is described here as the process that organizes the search for the answers that make a business model repeatable and scalable (Introduction, PDF p. 34, states it, and the chapter builds the model from it). The model is drawn as a circular track with recursive arrows, which the authors gloss as an admission that several iterations of each step are expected (PDF p. 63). IMVU is the chapter's worked example of the handoff from search to execution: the founders recruited a professional CEO and moved to the board (PDF p. 70).

**Chapter 3, The Customer Development Manifesto (PDF pp. 71-92).** Fourteen rules. There are no facts inside the building, so get outside (p. 71); pair Customer Development with agile development (p. 72); failure is an integral part of the search (p. 73); make continuous iterations and pivots, a pivot being a substantive change to one of the nine business-model hypotheses (p. 74); no business plan survives first contact with customers, so use a business model canvas as a flip book of successive versions (pp. 75-78); design experiments and test to validate hypotheses, the tests being short, simple, objective pass/fail tests looking for a strong signal (pp. 78-79); agree on market type, because it changes everything (p. 80); startup metrics differ from those in existing companies (p. 83); fast decision-making, cycle time, speed and tempo (p. 84); it is all about passion (p. 85); startup job titles differ from a large company's (p. 86); preserve all cash until needed, then spend (p. 87); communicate and share learning (p. 89); and Customer Development success begins with buy-in from the board and the team (p. 90). Rule 7 defines the four market types: a new product into an existing market, into a new market, or into an existing market re-segmented either as a low-cost entrant or as a niche, plus the clone of a proven model in another country (pp. 80-82).

**Chapter 4, An Introduction to Customer Discovery (PDF pp. 95-115).** Discovery turns the founders' hypotheses into facts, and the chapter's evidence for why is Iridium, which the authors say built a satellite network on a business plan whose customers did not behave as forecast, and which reached its 500,000th customer only in September 2011 after bankruptcy and a long climb back (PDF pp. 96-97). Getting out of the building cannot be assigned to junior staff and must be driven by the founders, repeatedly over weeks or months (PDF p. 97). The chapter enumerates what discovery is not: understanding the wants of all customers, compiling a feature list, or running focus groups (PDF pp. 97-98). It introduces earlyvangelists, the small subset of visionary buyers who have the problem, know they have it, are looking for a solution, have cobbled together a makeshift one, and can find or commit money for a better one (PDF pp. 99-102). Discovery is laid out in four phases, beginning with deconstructing the founders' vision into the nine parts of the business model canvas (PDF p. 112).

**Chapter 5, Introduction to Customer Validation (PDF pp. 116-129).** Validation asks whether the business model is repeatable and scalable, and it is proved by orders or usage rather than by opinions (PDF p. 124). The founders must lead validation as well, because only founders can call a pivot, and anyone else who hears of a serious flaw has neither the authority to pivot nor, usually, the nerve to carry the news (PDF p. 122). Validation runs at different speeds in different channels, faster and with more iterations on web and mobile because the product is bits (PDF pp. 122-123). The first paying customers are earlyvangelists, and the authors treat failure to sell to them as decisive (PDF p. 123). The chapter also contains the counterweight to its own process, under a heading about accountants not running startups (PDF p. 124). Validation likewise has four phases (PDF p. 126).

**Chapter 6, Customer Validation: Pivot or Proceed (PDF pp. 130-169).** The last gate of the search half. The chapter validates the business model and then the financial model, and it closes the Strategy Guide by sending the reader on to the authors' earlier book for customer creation and company-building (PDF p. 169). Fred Wilson is quoted in this chapter on the limits of rules of thumb (PDF p. 141).

### The Web/Mobile book (PDF pp. 185-654)

This is the same argument rebuilt as an instruction manual, with 45 numbered checklists, each carrying a goal, the work, and its pass/fail exit criteria. Many checklists carry a note that a following passage is written for physical-channel startups (for example PDF pp. 252, 265, 284, 361, 404), so the two channel editions interleave in the same text.

**Chapter 1, The Customer Development Manifesto (PDF pp. 190-213).** The same fourteen rules as Strategy Guide Chapter 3, in the same order and at the same wording, at pp. 190, 191, 192, 193, 194, 197, 199, 202, 203, 204, 205, 206, 208 and 209.

**Chapter 2, An Introduction to Customer Discovery (PDF pp. 214-237).** The same material as Strategy Guide Chapter 4, including the Iridium example and the four phases (PDF pp. 234-235).

**Chapter 3, State Your Business Model Hypotheses (PDF pp. 238-374).** The longest chapter in the book, one section per box of the canvas. Market size is estimated as total addressable market, served available market, and target market (PDF pp. 239-244). The value proposition arrives in two parts: the low-fidelity MVP hypothesis and the product vision with an eighteen-month plan (PDF pp. 246-251); and, later, market type and the competitive brief (PDF pp. 287-301). Customer segments distinguish the user, the payer, the influencer, the recommender, the decision-maker, the saboteur, and in a multi-sided market the two sides that each have to be prioritized (PDF pp. 258-268). The channels hypothesis tells web and mobile startups to pick one distribution channel, and cites LendingTree and Zynga as channel examples (PDF pp. 275-283). Customer relationships is the Get/Keep/Grow funnel, where acquisition and activation are distinguished, retention is held to be far cheaper than acquisition, and growth has two components (PDF pp. 302-349). Key resources, traffic partners, revenue and pricing complete the canvas, with pricing asked to recognize the value delivered and the prices competitors charge (PDF pp. 350-374).

**Chapter 4, Test the Problem (PDF pp. 375-407).** Design the tests as pass/fail experiments (PDF p. 382), then build the low-fidelity MVP, which may be a landing page rotated against AdWords copy rather than working software (PDF pp. 383-387). Drive traffic and start counting (PDF p. 392). The chapter then admits the just-do-it scramble, where some startups skip ahead at this stage (PDF p. 394). It closes with gaining customer understanding by using what customers use, and with traffic and competitive analysis (PDF pp. 396-406).

**Chapter 5, Test the Product Solution (PDF pp. 408-438).** The high-fidelity MVP test asks not whether customers like the product but how intensely they want it (PDF pp. 419-421), measured by watching behavior rather than by asking (PDF pp. 422-428), after which the business model is updated again (PDF p. 429).

**Chapter 6, Verify the Business Model and Pivot or Proceed (PDF pp. 439-460).** Three verification questions in turn: has a product/market fit been found (PDF pp. 441-447); does the company know who its customers are and how to reach them (PDF pp. 448-450); and can it make money and grow (PDF pp. 451-455). Then the pivot-or-proceed decision itself, with worked checkpoint examples (PDF pp. 456-460).

**Chapter 7, Introduction to Customer Validation (PDF pp. 464-477).** The same material as Strategy Guide Chapter 5, including the founders-must-lead argument (PDF p. 470) and the four phases (PDF p. 474).

**Chapter 8, Get Ready to Sell (PDF pp. 478-532).** Positioning is crafted first, with a worked example and candidate tag lines (PDF pp. 479-483). Then the acquire and activate plan and its tools (PDF pp. 484-511); the high-fidelity MVP (PDF pp. 512-514); the metrics toolset, opened by John Wanamaker's complaint about advertising waste (PDF pp. 515-523); a dedicated data analytics chief on the senior management team (PDF pp. 524-526); and a formal advisory board (PDF pp. 527-531).

**Chapter 9, Get Out of the Building and Sell (PDF pp. 533-583).** Optimization plans and tools (PDF pp. 541-552), then optimizing acquisition with a diagnosis section for a funnel that is failing (PDF pp. 553-563), then optimizing keep and grow, including cohort analysis of a sales funnel (PDF pp. 564-578), and finally test-selling traffic partners (PDF pp. 579-582). Growth is monitored by the viral growth factor and a second metric, with the caution that not every product spreads virally (PDF pp. 574-576).

**Chapter 10, Develop Product and Company Positioning (PDF pp. 584-605).** Product positioning is formalized by market type in a table (PDF pp. 588-591), then matched to market type, with the note that in a new market company positioning cannot rest on the product category (PDF pp. 592-596), then company positioning is articulated (PDF pp. 597-600) and validated with people outside the company (PDF pp. 601-604).

**Chapter 11, Pivot or Proceed (PDF pp. 606-654).** The team assembles the accumulated data (PDF pp. 607-610), validates the business model (PDF pp. 611-616), then the financial model under the heading metrics that matter, where Fred Wilson's burn-rate guidelines appear and two worked spreadsheet examples run through the numbers (PDF pp. 617-636). The model is re-validated (PDF pp. 639-643) and the pivot-or-proceed question is put (PDF pp. 644-647). The book closes with a simple overview of how to build a web startup (PDF pp. 648-654).

### The Physical Channel book (PDF pp. 687-1095)

The same eleven chapters with the same structure, rewritten for a startup that sells through a direct sales force, distributors, resellers, OEMs, or retail. Its Chapter 1 manifesto opens at PDF p. 691 and carries the same fourteen rules. Where it diverges from the Web/Mobile book it is on the mechanics of selling and of meeting customers face to face: the channel options compared strength by weakness, where a direct sales force is called the most expensive alternative and the hardest to staff and manage (PDF pp. 767-769); a warning against a broad launch or a press push at this stage, against what the authors call peer pressure to do so (PDF p. 788); the Keep and Grow funnels drawn separately for a physical channel (PDF pp. 800-808); preparing for customer contacts and running the problem presentation as a discussion rather than a pitch (PDF pp. 863-878); building the product solution presentation, with the instruction not to ask every question in each solution interview (PDF pp. 897-908); the sales and marketing materials a channel needs (PDF pp. 960-968); hiring a sales closer, and the sales roadmap whose complexity is said to depend on the customer's size, buying cycle and budget as well as the product's price, industry and channel (PDF pp. 989-1006); and getting out of the building to sell, with the selling-strategy map and the access strategy map used to find the route to a buyer (PDF pp. 1015-1038). The financial model is worked through a physical-product spreadsheet, including the guaranteed-sale case where retailers may return unsold stock (PDF pp. 1076-1081).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

**The process is not for invention risk.** Under a heading asking who the book is not for, the authors separate ventures with customer and market risk from ventures with invention risk, where the open question is whether the technology can ever be made to work, and name life sciences and biotech as examples. Those, they say, are solved by simulation tools, and a third category carries both risks (Strategy Guide, Introduction, PDF pp. 19-20).

**The founder may set the customer data aside.** Under a heading about accountants not running startups, the authors say the process is not a giant focus group and the goal is not to tally feedback and vote on features; a great entrepreneur may weigh all the data, listen to instinct, and explain why the feedback is being ignored. In a new market, they add, there is no data at all (Strategy Guide, Ch. 5, PDF p. 124).

**The authors say to put the book down if the business takes off.** Immediately after that, under a heading about lightning striking, the authors say a fast-scaling startup with viral or network-effect drivers may suddenly blast off before the book says it should, name Google, YouTube, Facebook and Twitter, and tell the reader to put the book down and hold on (Strategy Guide, Ch. 5, PDF p. 124).

**The authors allow the just-do-it scramble.** In the middle of the discovery chapter the authors acknowledge investors who encourage a startup to break all the rules and launch a product that does not exist. They say there are times when tossing the book out makes sense, naming a market bubble or an extraordinary customer reaction, and add that an investor holds a portfolio of startups while the founder holds a portfolio of one (Web/Mobile, Ch. 4, PDF p. 394).

**Market type is a late-binding decision.** Unlike decisions about product features, the market-type choice can be deferred until customer creation; the authors ask for a provisional hypothesis during discovery rather than a final answer, while saying the cost of choosing wrong is severe (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 287).

**The burn-rate numbers are guidelines with exceptions.** After setting out Fred Wilson's per-stage burn rates, the authors close the passage by saying there are, in their phrase, zillions of exceptions (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 141).

**Sometimes the metrics do not matter.** The authors allow that on rare occasions investors ignore most metrics and vote to scale rapidly, which they say can happen more often in frothy vertical markets or strong IPO markets, though nine times in ten the metrics matter (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 163).

**Not every product is viral.** After pressing hard on the viral growth factor, the authors note that social media and multiplayer games are viral but that some categories are not the kind of site anyone invites friends to, and they name dating and similar sites (Web/Mobile, Ch. 9, PDF p. 575).

**Velocity is not always the goal.** The authors name two rare exceptions to the pursuit of speed in acquisition: niche vertical sites, and high-value products such as enterprise software or expensive jewelry, where a few high-value customers may be enough (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420).

**Stealth is ruled out, but access control is not.** The authors say Customer Development and stealth are mutually exclusive, and that a founder who thinks the idea too fragile to show without a non-disclosure agreement should not be reading the book. In the same breath they allow an invite-only site to control access while the solution is tested, and say this is not the time for press releases, interviews, blogs or public demos, because the founder does not yet know enough to say what business the company is in (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420).

**The one-channel rule has an exception.** The instruction to test one distribution channel first carries a stated exception: a company using its own website to sell directly to end users tests that in parallel (Physical Channel, Ch. 8, PDF p. 992).

**The pace of validation depends on the channel.** Validation on web and mobile runs faster and with more iterations because the product is bits; a physical channel with prospects on other continents takes far longer. The authors say the underlying principles are identical across channels even though the speed is not (Strategy Guide, Ch. 5, PDF pp. 122-123).

**Early tests do not have to be precise.** The pass/fail experiments are described as short, simple and objective, looking for a strong enough signal rather than a rigorous one, with the authors' example being five of the first twelve customers called on (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF p. 79). And the tests need not be built from real code, hardware or product: a mock-up, a demo or a physical prototype is offered as enough (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF p. 80).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Seventeen places where a claim in the book meets a record in this repo. Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter. Claims with no record deciding them are not listed: among them the business model canvas as a weekly scorecard, Net Promoter Score, the advisory board, the data analytics chief, the viral growth factor, and the burn-rate guidelines.

1. **Getting out of the building cannot be assigned to junior staff and must be driven by the founders, repeatedly over weeks or months** (Strategy Guide, Ch. 4, PDF p. 97). [#994](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/994), interview the pilot agency, gates the calls on a written guide: "An interview guide is on this ticket before any call is made."

2. **Build a customer archetype from what customers say, and keep updating it as more is learned** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 258-259). [#994](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/994) decided what the repo's existing archetypes count as: "The repo's personas are proto-personas by their own README and must not be cited as research."

3. **The first paying customers are earlyvangelists, a small subset of visionary buyers who have the problem, know it, and have cobbled together a makeshift solution; failure to sell to them is treated as decisive** (Strategy Guide, Ch. 4, PDF pp. 99-102, and Ch. 5, PDF p. 123). [#444](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/444) published the pilot terms, which set "the grant (three per Staff member, counted once, no expiry), $20.00 a Credit plus tax beyond it, October 2026 to January 2027, and *nothing changes* at the end."

4. **A completely open site invites competitors, so an invite-only site is offered as a way to control access while the solution is tested** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420). [#444](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/444) decided where those terms live: published unlisted at `doula.cloud/pilot-terms` with `noindex`.

5. **Customer Development and stealth are mutually exclusive, and the authors say this is not the time for press releases, interviews, blogs or public demos, because the founder does not yet know enough to say what business the company is in** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 5, PDF p. 420). [#991](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/991), the go-to-market map, carries the ruling of 2026-09-08: "**No contact with DONA until the teaser is live**."

6. **Customer validation is proved by orders or usage rather than opinions, and the book sets per-category thresholds for how much response counts as enough** (Strategy Guide, Ch. 5, PDF p. 124, and Web/Mobile, Ch. 6, PDF p. 445). [#991](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/991) set the figures and what they are: "Targets on launch day: 150 confirmed waitlist subscribers and 5 pilot Practices… Numbers to be wrong about, not promises."

7. **A repeatable model is not one-off revenue from a board member's relationships or a heroic single-shot effort by the CEO; the test is whether one more customer adds incremental revenue** (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF p. 88). [#991](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/991) records the present position: "the pilot agency is the only real customer contact." [#1265](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1265), a second Practice, was closed as not planned.

8. **Getting a new customer is an expensive process, which is the book's reason for planning retention before acquisition succeeds** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 341). [#991](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/991) fixed the amount available: "one pool of about **$2,500**."

9. **Conferences and trade shows are named as the prime places to meet competitors, collect their literature, talk to their salespeople, and spot trends and talent** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 4, PDF p. 406). [#993](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/993) surveyed where doulas gather under the map's rule that "only a place that can produce a paying customer earns a presence", and [#991](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/991)'s decisions-so-far line records its outcome as five venues at $741 of published paid costs.

10. **A multi-sided market has to optimize the side that generates the cash, and the audience side is verified separately from the paying side** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 9, PDF p. 576). [#991](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/991) drew the boundary: "Parents and referral sources (midwives, birth centers, trainers) are out of scope."

11. **Once the market type is understood a competitive brief follows, and in a new market it is tempting to claim there is no competition and wrong to do so** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 299). [#173](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/173) ran the competitor feature survey into three buckets and fixed its altitude: it "stays at the *whether* altitude — records what other products do, recommends nothing about what Doula Cloud should build."

12. **Product positioning is set by market type, and the book gives a table of the four types with the positioning each one takes** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 10, PDF pp. 588-590). [#991](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/991)'s frameworks table assigns positioning to Dunford, and [#999](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/999) decided the form it takes and what is closed to it: "The positioning is a hypothesis, written as one, with the falsifying signal named beside each claim", and "Settled and not reopened here: the price ($20 per Credit), the audience boundary (no parents, no referral sources), founder voice, and the product name (its own ticket)."

13. **Company positioning answers what the company does for the customer and why to do business with it, and the positioning statement is sought as a few pithy words rather than a feature list** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 10, PDF p. 597, and Ch. 8, PDF p. 480). [#991](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/991)'s frameworks table assigns the message to Miller, and [#1001](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/1001) decided one thing about it outright: "Whether the site shows the $20 price is decided here as a message choice; the price itself is not."

14. **Pricing tactics are listed as value pricing and competitive pricing, so the price is asked to reflect the value delivered and what competitors charge** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 373). [#439](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/439) decided the anchor: "The price is anchored against what a doula charges per engagement, and that figure is **sourced from pilot Practices** rather than assumed."

15. **No business plan survives first contact with customers, so the plan's contents are hypotheses to be tested rather than facts** (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF p. 75). [#243](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/243), the standing questions for the pilot groups, decided how an untested answer is recorded: "A decision blocked on one of these is recorded as **provisional, subject to pilot confirmation** and named here."

16. **Free-to-paid conversion is called sexy but dangerous, and the authors say mapping profitable conversion of free to paid users makes or breaks the company** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 280). [#257](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/257) set the free tier at three Credits and separated it from the pilot's sizing: "the pilot's sizing rides on the founding grant ([#449](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/449)), not the signup bonus." ## 7. What has changed since publication

The book was published in 2012 and leans on companies, tools and figures from the decade before it. Checked below, one entry per example, with the later evidence named. Nine items could not be checked and are listed as unchecked at the end rather than carried silently.

**Webvan, the opening case of Chapter 1** (Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF pp. 36-42). The outcome is confirmed: Webvan took roughly $396 million from venture investors, raised a further $375 million in its November 1999 IPO, and ceased operations in June 2001, about three years after launch. The book's $8.5 billion market capitalization is not what the public record gives for the IPO valuation, which is reported as more than $4.8 billion; the higher figure belongs to a later peak that this check could not pin down. The book already named Peapod and Tesco as grocery deliverers that were working (PDF p. 52); since 2020 online grocery delivery has become a large business under Instacart and DoorDash, and former Webvan executives went to Amazon, which built the same business there. Source: [Webvan, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webvan).

**Iridium, the opening case of the discovery chapter** (Strategy Guide, Ch. 4, PDF pp. 96-97). The bankruptcy is confirmed: Iridium filed Chapter 11 on 13 August 1999, nine months after service launch, and was sold in a 2001 fire sale for $25 million that erased over $4 billion of debt. The company now trades on Nasdaq as IRDM and reported 2.627 million billable subscribers as of 30 June 2026, so the business the book used as a cautionary tale outlived the failure by a wide margin. Source: [Iridium Communications, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iridium_Communications).

**Groupon, cited among the companies that grew faster in a decade than most industrial giants ever did, and for what the book calls its $12 billion pivot** (Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF p. 32, and Ch. 3, PDF p. 74). Groupon's November 2011 IPO was the largest by an internet company since Google in 2004; it lost 80 percent of its value within a year, and by 2023 was reported to have lost 99.4 percent of its value since the IPO, with revenue down from about $3.2 billion in 2014 to $515 million in 2023. Source: [Groupon, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupon).

**Zynga and FarmVille, the book's example of free users converted to payers on a social channel** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 280, and Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF p. 32). The channel the example rests on closed: Facebook ended its special agreement with Zynga in November 2012, and the original FarmVille shut down on 31 December 2020 when Facebook stopped supporting Flash games. Zynga was acquired by Take-Two Interactive for $12.7 billion, closing 23 May 2022. Sources: [Zynga, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zynga); [FarmVille, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FarmVille).

**Alexa, named with Compete as a free traffic-measurement tool for sizing competitors** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 4, PDF p. 401). Amazon announced on 8 December 2021 that Alexa Internet would stop taking new accounts, and retired the service on 1 May 2022, so this tool no longer exists. Source: [Alexa Internet, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexa_Internet).

**Fred Wilson's burn-rate guidelines** (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 141, and Web/Mobile, Ch. 11, PDF p. 620). The source is Wilson's December 2011 post: below $50,000 a month while building product, below $100,000 while building usage, below $250,000 while building the business, from a rule of thumb of about $10,000 a month per person fully burdened. The book departs from its source at the first stage, giving a band of $50,000 to $75,000 a month for building product on both cited pages where Wilson gives a ceiling of $50,000. The figures are nominal 2011 dollars and Wilson scoped them to United States software companies. Source: [Burn Rates: How Much?, AVC](https://avc.com/2011/12/burn-rates-how-much/).

**Net Promoter Score, offered as a way to test whether the problem matters, with a pass mark of at least plus fifty** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 4, PDF pp. 392-393, and Ch. 5, PDF p. 415). The academic record since publication is unfavorable to the strong version of the claim: researchers have found no empirical evidence that the likelihood-to-recommend question predicts growth better than other loyalty questions, and that it does not measure anything the conventional loyalty questions do not, with Keiningham and colleagues' 2007 *Journal of Marketing* study the most cited. Source: [Net promoter score, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_promoter_score).

**The app store as a channel, where the book states that Apple's App Store charges 30 percent of retail pricing** (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF p. 278). The flat 70/30 split the book assumes has been unwound in stages: 85/15 after a subscriber's first year from 2016, 15 percent for developers under $1 million a year from 2020, alternative payment and distribution in the European Union under the Digital Markets Act from 2024, and a 2025 United States court order removing commissions and restrictions on external payment links. Source: [App Store (Apple), Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/App_Store_(Apple)).

**Peapod and Tesco, named alongside Webvan as grocery deliverers that succeeded where Webvan did not, with Peapod credited with more than 10 million deliveries** (Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF p. 52). Peapod continued under Ahold Delhaize, which had bought it in 2001, and by February 2022 the name had been changed to Peapod Digital Labs, so the consumer brand the book names no longer operates under that name. The Tesco half of the same sentence was not checked and is listed below. Source: [Peapod, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peapod).

**Blank's own later work.** The Lean LaunchPad curriculum that came out of this book became the National Science Foundation's I-Corps curriculum in 2011; Blank records it as offered at 100 universities with over 9,500 scientists and engineers trained, 1,380 NSF I-Corps teams launching startups that raised $3.166 billion, and parallel NIH and Energy programs. Source: [Lean LaunchPad posts, steveblank.com](https://steveblank.com/category/lean-launchpad/).

## 8. Where the book is weakest

**The market-share rules of thumb carry no source.** The market-type section attributes a set of rules to military operations research and states them as thresholds: 74 percent share is an effective monopoly; a leader and runner-up above 74 percent combined, with the leader within 1.7 times the second, is a duopoly impervious to attack; 41 percent with at least 1.7 times the next company is the market leader; a biggest player at 26 percent or more makes the market unstable; and below 26 percent the biggest player has no real influence. No study, data set or derivation is offered for any of these thresholds or for the 1.7 multiple, and they are repeated unchanged in the other channel edition (Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 289-292; Physical Channel, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 775-778).

**The failure rate is asserted rather than sourced.** The introduction states that more than nine of ten new products fail, in both established companies and startups, with no citation (Strategy Guide, Introduction, PDF p. 22). The same is true of the claim that for some websites 98 percent of visitors never return (Web/Mobile, Ch. 9, PDF pp. 554-555), and of the common wisdom the authors report that a new customer costs ten times more than keeping an existing one (Web/Mobile, Ch. 9, PDF p. 564), which is presented as received opinion and then used in the argument as though it were measured.

**The Wanamaker framing is wrong by a century.** The metrics chapter introduces John Wanamaker as a 19th-century retailer whose complaint about wasted advertising faces online marketers nearly two centuries later (Web/Mobile, Ch. 8, PDF p. 516). Wanamaker died in 1922, and the book was published in 2012, so the interval is roughly one century, not two.

**Eye-tracking conclusions are stated as fact from no study.** The optimization chapter asserts that the Z pattern is the most common way Americans read a web page and tells the reader where the eye goes, without naming the research it comes from, while the surrounding passage draws design conclusions from it (Web/Mobile, Ch. 9, PDF p. 551).

**The e-book keeps print cross-references that no longer resolve.** This edition prints no page numbers, yet the text sends the reader to specific pages and figures: a checklist item points at line 9 on page 442 (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 159), and the physical-channel selling chapter says the sales roadmap was developed earlier on page 344 (Physical Channel, Ch. 9, PDF p. 1026). A figure captioned 5.3 appears inside Chapter 4 of the Web/Mobile book (PDF p. 380). A reader of this e-book cannot follow any of them.

**The three editions in one file repeat rather than resolve.** The Web/Mobile and Physical Channel books restate the Strategy Guide's manifesto verbatim and each other's chapters with channel substitutions, and the Web/Mobile chapters carry notes saying that the following guidelines are geared for physical-channel startups (for example Web/Mobile, Ch. 3, PDF pp. 252, 265, 284, 361). The result is that a single claim exists in three places with three page numbers and no statement of which is authoritative.

**The venture frame sits uneasily with the audience the book claims.** The book names five kinds of startup it serves, including small business and social enterprise (Strategy Guide, Introduction, PDF p. 19), but its instruction on cash is to preserve it while searching and then to spend like there is no tomorrow (Strategy Guide, Ch. 3, PDF p. 88), its burn-rate guidelines come from a venture capitalist and are denominated in tens and hundreds of thousands of dollars a month (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 141), its metrics chapter treats a VC-ready multiyear profit and loss statement as the eventual output (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 163), and its validation thresholds assume audiences of several thousand prospects and hundreds of activations (Web/Mobile, Ch. 6, PDF p. 445). The authors also allow that in a hot market investors may ignore the metrics entirely and vote to scale (Strategy Guide, Ch. 6, PDF p. 163), which concedes that the arbiter of the process is the investor rather than the customer.

**The counter-cases are placed where they weaken the process least.** The three passages that most limit the method, the founder who may ignore the customer data, the lightning-strike exception, and the just-do-it scramble, are each a short aside inside a long procedural chapter (Strategy Guide, Ch. 5, PDF p. 124; Web/Mobile, Ch. 4, PDF p. 394), and none of them is given a test that would tell a reader which situation applies. The book supplies pass/fail criteria for its own steps and none for its exceptions.

**Examples are chosen after the outcome is known.** The successes the book leans on, among them IMVU, Google, YouTube, Facebook, Twitter, Groupon and Zynga (Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF p. 32; Ch. 2, PDF pp. 54-55 and 70; Ch. 5, PDF p. 124), are named because they succeeded, and the failures, Webvan and Iridium, because they failed (Strategy Guide, Ch. 1, PDF pp. 36-42; Ch. 4, PDF pp. 96-97). No company that followed the process and failed appears, and no company that ignored it and succeeded is weighed against the ones that did, so the evidence cannot separate the method from the selection.
