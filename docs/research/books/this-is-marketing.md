## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that marketing is the act of making change happen, and that the method which now works is empathy and service rather than the interruption and mass reach of the advertising era; what he offers is a compass, a true north, explicitly not a road map and not a step-by-step series of tactics (Ch. 1, PDF p. 13). The method has five steps: invent a thing worth making, build it so a few people particularly benefit, tell a story matching the built-in narrative of that tiny group, the smallest viable market, spread the word, and then show up consistently and generously for years to organize, lead, and earn permission (Ch. 2, PDF p. 21). The change runs through three mechanisms the author names repeatedly: people act according to an internal narrative and adopt a behavior when people like us do things like this (Ch. 9, PDF p. 88); every decision is weighed against status, measured either as affiliation or as dominion (Ch. 11, PDF pp. 111-113); and a pattern is only interrupted when the marketer creates tension that forward motion relieves (Ch. 10, PDF pp. 96-100). Two questions are said to govern every decision, who is it for and what is it for (Ch. 3, PDF p. 27), and the assets to be built are permission, the privilege of delivering anticipated, personal, and relevant messages (Ch. 17, PDF p. 152), and trust, which the author calls as scarce as attention (Ch. 18, PDF p. 159). The scope claimed is wide: a TED talk, asking a boss for a raise, and raising money for a playground are each called marketing (author's note, PDF p. 10).

The author says at length what the claim is not. It is not advertising, which he treats as an optional engine rather than the discipline, and asking how to get the word out is called precisely the wrong question and the last part rather than the first (Ch. 1, PDF p. 15; Ch. 15, PDF p. 136). It is not a battle, a war, or a contest, and involves very little shouting, hustling, or coercion (Ch. 1, PDF p. 14). It is not the shameless kind, the spamming and tricking he blames for driving generous marketers into hiding (Ch. 1, PDF pp. 15-16). It is not being marketing-driven, which he calls a dead end, as against being market-driven (Ch. 3, PDF p. 28). It is not a race to add features for less money (Ch. 3, PDF p. 26), and it is not a quality manual: a reader who cannot yet deliver quality is told this book is not much help (Ch. 6, PDF p. 60). It is not a playbook for making hits, which he says someone will make and it is probably not the reader (Ch. 8, PDF p. 84). It is not a case for authenticity, which he calls a dangerous myth in the marketplace and replaces with emotional labor (Ch. 6, PDF pp. 65-66). And it is not amoral: the author holds the marketer responsible for the impact regardless of what a boss says, and refuses just doing your job as a defense (Ch. 23, PDF p. 197).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

Each chapter below is cited by its number and the PDF page. Chapter titles are the book's own.

### Chapter One: Not Mass, Not Spam, Not Shameful . . . (PDF pp. 13-19)

The chapter's claim is that the "true north" of marketing has flipped, from selfish mass to empathy and service, and that what the book offers is a compass rather than a road map or a step-by-step series of tactics (Ch. 1, PDF p. 13). The material comes from a hundred-day seminar with peer-to-peer coaching at TheMarketingSeminar.com (Ch. 1, PDF pp. 13-14). The internet is named the first mass medium not invented to make marketers happy, unlike television and radio, and is described as a billion tiny whispers rather than a mass audience (Ch. 1, PDF p. 14). Advertising once made money spent equal sales made, so marketers came to treat advertising as the whole of the job, and the author says that stopped being true (Ch. 1, PDF pp. 14-15). Asking how to get the word out is called precisely the wrong question, and being discovered is placed last in the sequence rather than first (Ch. 1, PDF p. 15). Shameless practice, meaning spamming, tricking, and coercing, is set against professions that do not behave that way, and is blamed for driving ethical marketers to hide their best work (Ch. 1, PDF pp. 15-16). The lock-and-key image closes the argument: making products for the customers one seeks to serve is easier than finding customers for products already made (Ch. 1, PDF p. 16).

The evidence is one case study, Penguin Magic (Ch. 1, PDF pp. 17-18). Every trick on the site is demonstrated by a video that withholds the method, which creates tension the purchase relieves; those videos have been seen more than a billion times at no cost of distribution. Professional magicians rarely buy tricks because they need only ten or twenty and their audience changes nightly, while an amateur faces the same friends and family and keeps buying. More than eighty-two thousand product reviews, written by other magicians rather than by general reviewers, drive a stock cycle the author puts at about a month rather than years, across more than sixteen thousand items carried. The company also runs an email list of tens of thousands, three hundred lectures, and nearly a hundred live conventions.

### Chapter Two: The Marketer Learns to See (PDF pp. 20-22)

The evidence offered for the whole book is the author's own record: a 1983 brand-manager job at the software startup Spinnaker where he says he wasted the entire ad budget and the software sold anyway, followed by decades of projects and by work alongside Jay Levinson, Lester Wunderman, and Bernadette Jiwa (Ch. 2, PDF p. 20). Marketing is then given five steps: invent a thing worth making; build it so that a few people particularly benefit; tell a story matching that tiny group's built-in narrative, the smallest viable market; spread the word; and show up regularly and generously for years to organize, lead, and earn permission (Ch. 2, PDF p. 21). The chapter's own executive summary states that ideas that spread win, that marketers use marketing to solve other people's problems rather than using consumers to solve the company's, that status, affiliation, and dominion decide where people go, that persistent and frequent stories to an aligned audience earn attention, trust, and action, that direct marketing and brand marketing are not the same, that people like us do things like this is how culture is understood, that ideas skate through early adopters and leap a chasm, and that culture is strategy (Ch. 2, PDF pp. 21-22). Six numbered claims close the chapter, among them that "Who's it for?" focuses the work and handles the nonbelievers, that a person's own story is entirely true as far as that person is concerned and arguing is foolish, and that what others say about you matters more than what you say (Ch. 2, PDF p. 22).

### Chapter Three: Marketing Changes People Through Stories, Connections, and Experience (PDF pp. 23-29)

The chapter opens with the VisionSpring case study, which is the book's central piece of field evidence (Ch. 3, PDF pp. 23-26). VisionSpring is a social enterprise selling reading glasses in villages, made in bulk at about two dollars a pair and sold for about three, the one-dollar margin covering shipping, local salespeople, and growth. At a table in an Indian village the author found that about a third of the people who needed glasses, knew it, and had cash in their pockets bought a pair. He changed one thing: rather than offering a choice of ten new styles after the eye test, the sample glasses were left on the buyer's face and the line was that these are your glasses, pay three dollars to keep them or hand them back. He reports the share bought doubled. The lesson drawn is desire for gain against avoidance of loss, and that shopping is a pleasure only for people whose being wrong is not fatal; the author's narrative about how to buy glasses is called his own narrative and nothing better (Ch. 3, PDF pp. 25-26).

Three further arguments follow. Utility does not drive purchase: a Land Cruiser bought by someone who never leaves the road, Ludicrous Mode on a Tesla, a three-thousand-dollar car stereo owned by someone with a thirty-dollar clock radio at home, and a most-popular car color that varies by the kind of car (Ch. 3, PDF p. 26). Theodore Levitt's quarter-inch-hole line is quoted and then pushed further, through the shelf and the uncluttered room to the feeling: the author's version ends at feeling "safe and respected" (Ch. 3, PDF pp. 26-27). The tools named are stories, connections, and experiences, and the claim is that most marketers deliver the same small set of feelings by different means, so the thing sold is a road to the emotion (Ch. 3, PDF pp. 27-28). The chapter then distinguishes being marketing-driven, which it calls a dead end, from being market-driven, which is listening to the market and bending it (Ch. 3, PDF p. 28), and closes by rejecting the rational agent of microeconomics, quoting Wikipedia's definition to do it, and naming two mistakes: assuming the people served are well-informed long-term choice makers, and assuming everyone is like you (Ch. 3, PDF p. 29).

### Chapter Four: The Smallest Viable Market (PDF pp. 30-41)

The chapter's frame is four questions. What change are you trying to make, with two named stumbles: picking a grandiose change that has never been done, which the author calls a heavy burden and a convenient excuse, and reverse-engineering a change out of what you were already going to sell and loading it with buzzwords (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 30-31). The counter-example is By the Way Bakery, founded by the author's wife, whose stated change is that nobody is left out, turning hosts from exclusive to inclusive (Ch. 4, PDF p. 31). What promise are you making, since every message takes the form of if you do X you will get Y, a promise rather than a guarantee; slogans are read as promises about status, dominance, respect, belonging, and affiliation (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 31-32). Who are you seeking to change, answered by psychographics rather than demographics: worldviews, a term the author takes from cognitive linguist George Lakoff, and personas such as Bargain Bill, Hurried Henry, and Careful Karla (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 32-34). Two examples carry it: Starbucks and Dunkin' Donuts sold coffee for two decades without selling to each other's customers, the real distinction being internal rather than external (Ch. 4, PDF p. 32); and Ron Johnson, hired to run JCPenney in 2011 out of Apple retail, ended the constant discounts and sales because his own worldview had no room for them, and sales fell by more than 50 percent because he abandoned customers who loved the sport of bargain hunting (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 33-34).

The smallest viable market is then defined as the minimum number of people you would need to influence to make the effort worth it, and mass is rejected on the ground that it means average, offend no one, satisfy everyone (Ch. 4, PDF p. 34). The example is Danny Meyer's Union Square Cafe, which could seat six hundred people a day, so the founder's work was choosing which six hundred; the author says the magic was neither the real estate, in a bad neighborhood, nor a famous chef, whom they did not have (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 34-35). Specificity is called a kind of bravery because it is accountable: it worked or it did not (Ch. 4, PDF p. 35). Lean entrepreneurship's minimum viable product is invoked with the stress put on viable, since shipping something that does not work yet is not allowed, and Steve Blank's customer development is named as the claim that traction with customers, not technology or expensive marketing, separates projects that work from projects that do not (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 35-36). Rejection by the mass audience is met with a rule to resist sanding off the edges, because the work is for the smallest viable audience (Ch. 4, PDF p. 36). Clay Shirky is quoted on Wikipedia making big things possible for love, and the goal of the smallest viable audience is put as finding people who fall in love with where you hope to take them, so that the love becomes part of their identity (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 36-37). A congressional example follows: in a district of 724,000 residents, twenty thousand people voted in the last primary, so five thousand voters decide a contested one, less than 1 percent (Ch. 4, PDF p. 37). Customers are renamed students, in a mentor relationship of enrollment and choice rather than testing and compliance (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 37-38). Thief-detector dye colors a whole swimming pool purple and is invisible in the ocean, so the instruction is to find a pool (Ch. 4, PDF p. 38). Telling a person that the work is not for them is offered as respect rather than rejection, supported by the observation that every best-selling book on Amazon carries some one-star reviews, and by a comedian who bombed in New York before a tour group that spoke no English (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 38-39). The chapter ends with a three-sentence promise template, my product is for people who believe, I will focus on people who want, I promise that engaging with what I make will help you get (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 39-40).

The second case study is the Open Heart Project (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 40-41). Susan Piver, a meditation teacher with a best-selling book, was asked repeatedly by retreat attendees how to find a local teacher, and built an online sangha instead. A few years on it has more than twenty thousand members, most paying nothing and some paying a subscription for daily engagement, run by one full-time staff member, and the author calls it the largest meditation community in the world. Eight numbered reasons close the chapter, among them starting from empathy for a real need, asking how few people could find this indispensable and still make it worth doing, matching the served worldview, making it easy to spread, earning and keeping attention and trust, looking for work to do for members rather than members for the work, creating and relieving tension at each step, and showing up often.

### Chapter Five: In Search of "Better" (PDF pp. 42-58)

The chapter's claim is that better is not a property of the thing. The Beer Advocate website is cited as listing 250 beers that have each earned more than 3,400 ratings, every one of them somebody's favorite, so a marketer who says this is better is wrong and means better for someone (Ch. 5, PDF p. 42). Empathy is set out as a list of things true of everyone, that they do not believe what you believe, know what you know, or want what you want, with the word sonder used for noticing that every other person's inner life is as rich as yours (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 42-43). The nonprofit fundraiser who cannot picture giving a million dollars herself is the worked example: the donation is not for her, and a donor who gives it expects more joy, status, and satisfaction than it costs, which the author generalizes into a claim that every purchase is a bargain in the buyer's own accounting, so withholding the offer is a form of stealing (Ch. 5, PDF p. 43). Better is then argued not to be transitive the way length is: an Hermes bag costs more than a Louis Vuitton and a Coach, which makes it more expensive and not better, and subjective attributes stop being linear at all (Ch. 5, PDF p. 44). With more than 250 motorcycle models for sale in Cleveland, nobody can hold the field in mind, so people remember the one that is best for them, which differs by what they care about (Ch. 5, PDF p. 44). The marketer's job is put as finding a spot on the map with edges, a generous beacon rather than a selfish unique selling proposition (Ch. 5, PDF p. 45).

Dog food is the chapter's second worked example: Americans spent more than twenty-four billion dollars on it in the year before writing, with prices and ingredient claims rising, and the author notes he has never seen a dog buy dog food, so the innovation is for owners and the winning formula is food owners want to buy (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 45-46). The general point drawn is a disconnect between performance and appeal, and that the engineer's best price-performance combination is rarely the market's choice (Ch. 5, PDF p. 46). Early adopters are defined as neophiliacs rather than adapters: they are addicted to the new, forgiving of missteps during discovery and unforgiving after, and cannot be satisfied, only interested (Ch. 5, PDF p. 46). Conspiracy belief is offered as evidence that people choose beliefs for feeling: Roland Imhoff of Johannes Gutenberg University in Mainz is cited for studies in which the same people believe Lady Diana faked her death and was murdered, and that Osama bin Laden was already dead and is still alive, and for an experiment in which American conspiracy theorists were more enthusiastic about an invented German smoke-detector conspiracy when told 19 percent of Germans believed it than when told 81 percent did; Imhoff is quoted on a "deep-seated need for uniqueness" (Ch. 5, PDF p. 47). Humility and curiosity follow, with attention described as something people trade rather than something an ad buys, and the lifeguard who does not have to pitch a life buoy to a drowning person (Ch. 5, PDF p. 48).

The third case study is the musical Be More Chill (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 48-49). Two years after a poorly reviewed New Jersey debut, its cast album reached the Billboard top ten and passed a hundred million streams, with fan fiction, video animatics, and high school productions, and the author calls it the most beloved musical of its time after Hamilton. Charles Isherwood's New York Times review is quoted in fragments, and the point drawn is that the show was not for the critics. A fan in Naples, Italy, drew animation that a viewer in Idaho Falls saw and commissioned more of; a New York meet and greet drew fans from around the world to meet the creators and each other, and an off-Broadway run was coming.

The rest of the chapter is positioning. A teenager's first car is a want rather than a need, and it changes status for the teenager and dominion for the parent (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 49-50). Industrial marketing worked when the customer had three channels and one store, and does not when a million books are published a year and Amazon carries more than five hundred battery chargers, so the surrounded consumer walks away (Ch. 5, PDF p. 50). Positioning is then offered as a service: draw an XY grid, pick two axes of things people care about, and plot every alternative, not yet called a competitor (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 50-51). The diamond-delivery example runs speed against security across an armored car, the postal service, a bike messenger, and a stamp, and the point made is that each option is right for some buyer and that changing the axes changes the whole chart (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 51-52). Twenty-two candidate axes are listed, from speed and price to elitism, danger, and incomplete (Ch. 5, PDF p. 53). The history is named and then departed from: David Ogilvy and Rosser Reeves invented claims to fill a hole in the market in the 1950s, and Jack Trout and Al Ries pushed positioning into cornering the competition, which the author says does not hold up in a hyper-competitive world; his substitute is claims that are true and doubled down on in every action, and claims that are generous, existing as a service (Ch. 5, PDF p. 54). Two music teachers illustrate it, one taking rigor and competition wins as his axes, which obliges him to expel students who are not serious, and another a few blocks away taking the whole student and refusing competitions; the author says they do not compete, they are on the same board, and adds that the rigorous one is not for him (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 54-55). Choosing the popular axes puts you in a crowded quadrant where growth is hard without advertising, so the alternative given is to build your own quadrant from two overlooked axes (Ch. 5, PDF p. 55). The chapter ends on freedom: marketers who believe their industry leaves them no options, the ice delivery man and the milkman as better that stopped being worth paying for, Zillow's 110 million listings against the broker who used to hoard data, and the author's own comparison of eight engineers and millions of dollars to send a million emails in 1994 against nine dollars a month at Feedblitz now (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 56-58).

### Chapter Six: Beyond Commodities (PDF pp. 59-67)

The chapter argues that quality has stopped being a differentiator. The precursor of the FDA was formed in 1906 against products such as Berry's Freckle Ointment and LashLure, which the author says caused more than a dozen forms of blindness; as much as fifty years later product quality was still unreliable, and today FedEx delivers more than 99 percent of packages on time, browsers rarely crash, and air travel has never been safer (Ch. 6, PDF pp. 59-60). Quality in the sense of meeting specifications is therefore called required but no longer sufficient, and the author states plainly that a reader who cannot yet deliver quality is not helped much by this book (Ch. 6, PDF p. 60). The commodity suckout is the consequence: anything findable on Upwork, Amazon, or Alibaba cannot carry a price raise, because the price of everything is a click away (Ch. 6, PDF p. 60). The shoeshine stand is the worked example: nobody needs a shoeshine, so the available stories are looking good, the status of being waited on, and the signifier of what people like him do, and knowing the customer's story is called insufficient without organizing the whole experience around it (Ch. 6, PDF pp. 60-61). Bernadette Jiwa's *Story Driven* is cited for the claim that filling a hole in the market is rearview-mirror behavior driven by scarcity, against a generative posture built on possibility, and her ten things good stories do are reproduced (Ch. 6, PDF pp. 61-62). Claiming a story puts the marketer on the hook to deliver, which the author gives as the reason making average stuff for average people is tempting (Ch. 6, PDF p. 62).

The case study is Stack Overflow (Ch. 6, PDF pp. 63-64). Its predecessor Experts Exchange charged three hundred dollars a year, showed answers to Google's crawler for search traffic, and scrambled them for arriving humans, which the author calls profit via frustration. Joel Spolsky and Jeff Atwood made questions and answers both visible and paid for the site with job advertising, on the reasoning that a site where great programmers ask and answer is the place to recruit them. The design treats three constituencies differently: programmers in a hurry get answers ranked by quality, since a thousand people want an answer for every one who writes one; answerers get a community, a ranking system, and levels that earn reputation and power; job posters get fast self-service with no hard sell. At the time of writing the company is described as profitable with more than 250 employees and millions of visits a week.

The chapter closes with four shorter arguments. Better belongs to users: Google beat Yahoo not on obviously better results or speed but because its box did not make you feel stupid, two links against Yahoo's 183, and DuckDuckGo is now better for some people because it does not track them (Ch. 6, PDF p. 64). Trident Booksellers and Cafe in Boston held its own against Amazon because it serves coffee, which makes a third place, so the author calls it a coffee shop that sells books and the books a souvenir of the connections made (Ch. 6, PDF pp. 64-65). The authentic vulnerable hero is called a dangerous myth: James Brown collapsing on stage every night was stagecraft, the patient therapist is doing his job, and the smiling barista is presenting rather than revealing, since revealing is for family and close friends (Ch. 6, PDF p. 65). Emotional labor is named as the substitute, the work of doing what you do not feel like doing, and a person who must be authentic to do good work is called a fortunate amateur rather than a professional; a faceless corporate email in the second person is contrasted with taking responsibility, where the goal is not to personalize the work but to make it personal (Ch. 6, PDF pp. 66-67).

### Chapter Seven: The Canvas of Dreams and Desires (PDF pp. 68-77)

School and work train people to meet a specification, and the chapter reproduces a U.S. government job description for a grade-6 sewing machine operator, down to the weights handled and the exposure to cuts and bruises, to show a document that is specific about a task and silent about a dream (Ch. 7, PDF p. 68). Money is used the same way: twenty-dollar bills are meaningless and it is what they buy that is worked for, and the same is said of a product, which is offering a new emotional state rather than a widget (Ch. 7, PDF pp. 68-69). Asking people what they want is called unlikely to produce a breakthrough, since the crowd did not invent the Model T, the smartphone, rap, JetBlue, City Bakery, or charity: water (Ch. 7, PDF p. 69). Three confusions are named: mistaking wants for needs, when what is needed is air, water, health, and shelter; being intimately aware of a want and terrible at inventing new ways to meet it, so preferring a familiar solution that works badly; and believing everyone wants the same thing, when early adopters want the new and laggards want nothing to change (Ch. 7, PDF p. 69).

The chapter's centerpiece is a list of about thirty-two dreams and desires the author calls a shared vocabulary, running from adventure, affection, and avoiding new things through peace of mind, revenge, and tension; he says a reader could probably add ten more but not fifty, so a marketer, like an artist, needs few colors (Ch. 7, PDF pp. 70-71). From this list the method is to make assertions about what the audience wants and about how the story and promise will interact with those wants, starting from dreams and fears rather than from machines, inventory, or tactics (Ch. 7, PDF p. 71). The white leather wallet is the worked example: nobody needs a wallet, and the buyer is not buying white or leather but a feeling, which the author says to identify before making the wallet (Ch. 7, PDF p. 72). The real estate broker follows: nobody is happy to call one, the caller is afraid, nervous, stressed about money, and thinking about status, and the author cites figures given to him by the National Association of Realtors that more than 80 percent of people who hire a broker hire the first one who returns the call (Ch. 7, PDF pp. 72-73). Where someone acts unexpectedly, the instruction is to look for their fear (Ch. 7, PDF p. 73).

The chapter then turns the same questions on the reader: what you want is also an emotional state rather than a detail, and three truths of commerce are offered, that independence usually needs owned assets or a reputation, that being well off usually needs delivering enough value that people pay happily, and that pride in the work usually needs avoiding a race to the bottom (Ch. 7, PDF pp. 73-74). Boring is defended against only on the ground that people happy with boring are not looking for you, so the servable audience is the curious, dissatisfied, or bored; two shifts make testing cheap, that a prototype or limited run costs less and takes less time than ever, and that early adopters are easier to find (Ch. 7, PDF p. 74). Always be testing is glossed as always be wrong, most of the time (Ch. 7, PDF pp. 74-75). Scrapbooking is offered as the alternative to radical originality, which the author says has a low return and wears you out: collect the typefaces, prices, offers, images, and interfaces the audience trusts, break them into their parts, and rebuild on top of them (Ch. 7, PDF p. 75). Charging ten times as much is said to require a different extreme, story, or scarcity rather than more of the same, since nobody pays ten times for more words or a bigger order of fries (Ch. 7, PDF pp. 75-76). The closing examples are the Opinicon resort near Ottawa, whose enormous ice cream cones sell for under three Canadian dollars where the author reckons an MBA analysis would price them at about eight, because the cones are a beacon rather than the business; and Stew Leonard's, profiled by Tom Peters with the highest sales per square foot of any store of its kind, which a later generation of owners ran for short-term profit, raising profits for a while and then losing the crowd (Ch. 7, PDF pp. 76-77).

### Chapter Eight: More of the Who: Seeking the Smallest Viable Market (PDF pp. 78-86)

The chapter is about network effects and true fans. Every very good customer brings another, so dead-end, silent, or secretive customers are called not worth the trouble, and the work must be built so it works better when spread (Ch. 8, PDF p. 78). The fax machine spread because it works better when colleagues have one; Bob Metcalfe's Ethernet at 3Com first let three users share a printer, a marginal benefit, and changed only when users shared data and being off the network became painful (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 78-79). Metcalfe's Law is described from its original slide, a straight line for the cost of each added person and a curve for the exponentially greater value (Ch. 8, PDF p. 79). The counter-position is that a miracle is not going to happen: the old-school dream of turning an average product into a hit through public relations, promotion, ad buys, and influence marketing is called expensive failure most of the time, and the substitute is customer traction, tested by how many people outside headquarters use the thing daily, how many insist their friends use it, and who would miss it if it were gone (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 79-80).

Kevin Kelly's 2008 essay is quoted at length for the definition of a true fan, one who buys anything you produce, with the claim that roughly a thousand of them support a living but not a fortune (Ch. 8, PDF p. 80). The author's gloss is that the hard part is the micro market rather than the mass market, and that people bend themselves trying to please anonymous masses before fifty or a hundred people would miss them (Ch. 8, PDF p. 81). Hamilton is handled as the apparent counter-example: for more than a year it was seen by a few hundred people a night, at full Broadway capacity by a few thousand, and in total by less than 1 percent of the U.S. population, with a best-selling soundtrack and companion book each selling a few hundred thousand copies, so the author says hits are now meaningful to a few and invisible to the rest (Ch. 8, PDF p. 81).

The Grateful Dead are the chapter's long example (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 81-83). The author says he has bought 233 of their albums, more than five hundred hours. The band had one top-40 Billboard hit and grossed more than $350 million in concert revenue while Jerry Garcia was alive and another $100 million since, at average ticket prices near twenty-three dollars. Four elements are named: a tiny audience given all their energy; fans encouraged to tape shows rather than radio to reach the masses; a small number of true fans supporting a lot rather than a large number supporting a little; and owning both ends of an XY axis, live concerts against polished records and long jams against short radio hits. Three requirements follow: extraordinary talent, since 146 concerts in a year cannot be faked; patience, since a typical 1972 show drew five thousand people and the overnight success took more than a decade; and the guts to be quirky while the Zombies, the Doors, and the Turtles outsold them. The author adds that in 1972 this was an accident and today it is the best path. Taylor Swift is then ruled out as a role model: Scott Borchetta of Big Machine has had more than two hundred number-one singles and sold more than thirty million records for Swift, whose tour revenue the author puts near the Dead's, and the lesson drawn is that someone will make hits and it is probably not the reader (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 83-84).

The chapter closes on criticism and refusal. A critic who dislikes the work is right about that and wrong to say nobody will like it, which is how a best-seller carries both one-star and five-star reviews; twelve percent of twenty-one thousand reviews of *Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone* gave it one or two stars, which the author reads as a bimodal distribution of a desired audience and an accidental audience that enjoys not liking the work (Ch. 8, PDF p. 84). Seeking feedback is called asking to be proven wrong, and advice is offered as the substitute, phrased as asking how the work could fit the other person's worldview more closely; negative stories are said to be universalized into what everyone will feel when they are a specific sore spot, the one-star review for a book that arrived late for a baby shower being the example (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 84-85). The final exercise asks why the people who do not choose you are right, filling in a sentence about what they want and believe, on the argument that people decide rationally given what they see, believe, and want; the illustration is a cooking class where a chef sneered at substituting ground turkey for veal, and the author says both were right, the student caring more about the story and the teacher about the memory of the dish (Ch. 8, PDF pp. 85-86).

### Chapter Nine: People Like Us Do Things Like This (PDF pp. 87-95)

The chapter's mechanism is normalization. Marketers are said to overrate their power to change people, because everyone acts in accordance with an internal narrative, and most behavior change is driven by wanting to fit in and by perceived status, both of which usually push toward staying put, so tension is needed (Ch. 9, PDF p. 87). Crickets against beef is the illustration: neither preference is genetic and neither has a clear rational basis, yet one makes many readers squirm, and the author says even an outlier is aligning with the behavior of outliers, and even a sociopath is not unaware of the crowd (Ch. 9, PDF p. 88). The scope claim follows: nobody can change the culture, but each person can change a culture, a pocket of the world, which is the reason given for the smallest viable market (Ch. 9, PDF p. 88).

Three case studies carry the chapter. The blue ribbons (Ch. 9, PDF p. 89): the author's town failed a school budget vote for the first time in memory, and New York state's second vote carries draconian mandated cuts if it fails too; with eight days left, activists tied a hundred blue ribbons to a tree in front of the middle school rather than arguing, flyering, or rallying, thousands of ribbons appeared on dozens of trees, and the budget passed two to one. Gay marriage in Ireland (Ch. 9, PDF p. 92): rather than a rational case about fairness and civil rights, Brighid and Paddy White, both nearly eighty, made a video about their son, which the author reads as letting viewers see themselves as parents, traditionalists, and Irishmen, on the claim that political change is cultural change and culture changes horizontally. The Robin Hood Foundation (Ch. 9, PDF pp. 93-94): $101,000,000 raised in one night in 2015, which the author calls the most effective fundraiser of its kind in history and attributes not to the gala format but to a generation of expectation-building among wealthy hedge fund and Wall Street donors, trading cash for status under the tension of a room containing your peers and your spouse.

Between them run the supporting arguments. Decisions are made against a perception of one's cohort, so normalization creates culture and culture drives choices (Ch. 9, PDF pp. 89-90). "Us" used to mean nearly everyone under mass media, and no longer does: *Mad Men* was regularly seen by 1 percent of the U.S. population despite dozens of New York Times articles in a season, and the author says all of us has gone from everyone to no one, which the long tail is content with (Ch. 9, PDF p. 90). Defining "us" is named as the marketer's first job, and answering it with the people who already give is called backwards (Ch. 9, PDF pp. 90-91). It should be a culture rather than the culture, with two tasks, mapping that culture's worldview and focusing every effort on it (Ch. 9, PDF p. 91). Alex Samuel's observation supplies the ratchet: JetBlue only had to be hipper than American and Delta, while Virgin America six years later had to be hipper than JetBlue, and photography works the same way, so a leap too far leaves the tribe behind (Ch. 9, PDF pp. 91-92). Malcolm Gladwell's distinction between elite and exclusive is used to argue that elite is an external measure that can be withdrawn, the Rhodes Scholarship being elite but not exclusive, while exclusive is internal and controllable, the Hell's Angels being exclusive but not elite, and Harvard Business School and the Navy Seals both (Ch. 9, PDF pp. 92-93). The standing ovation is offered as evidence that venues carry a bias: three people can start one at TED, about fifteen at a Broadway show, and probably nobody at the Mezzrow jazz club (Ch. 9, PDF p. 94). The chapter closes on a tree image, roots in the dreams and desires of the people served, a commodity having shallow roots and a tall tree shading out similar ones, with the warning against showing up with an acorn and expecting a crowd (Ch. 9, PDF p. 95).

### Chapter Ten: Trust and Tension Create Forward Motion (PDF pp. 96-102)

Every offering either matches a pattern or interrupts one (Ch. 10, PDF p. 96). A new cereal for a family already cycling through cereals is a match, and so is a sitcom in an established time slot. An interrupt requires a jolt, and the answer is usually no, because patterns are established, time is precious, and risk is feared: getting someone who has never hired a gardener to hire one, or moving a habitual hundred-dollar donor to five thousand. The consequence drawn is that people whose lives have just changed have no pattern to match, which is why new fathers, engaged women, and recent movers are profitable to market to, and why a brand-new platform is the best time to market an app, since there are no old choices to call mistakes (Ch. 10, PDF pp. 96-97).

Tension is defined as the force on a stretched rubber band, and distinguished from fear (Ch. 10, PDF pp. 97, 99). The classroom example is a student who will not volunteer a question but answers readily when called on, because the social tension supplied by the teacher overcomes inertia (Ch. 10, PDF p. 97). Slack is the extended case (Ch. 10, PDF pp. 97-98): it began as a pattern match, new software for people who like new software, then handed that group a tool for a peer-to-peer pattern interrupt, since the product is better when coworkers use it and the new user's tension is the colleague saying you are missing out, with conversations happening without them that signing in relieves; the author says this horizontal transmission is built into the software rather than accidental. Launching anything also breaks something: a second hotel in Niagara Falls ends the first being the only one, the telephone ends the telegraph's speed, an exclusive party makes outsiders, and a new extreme demotes the one it exceeded, which is the tension of being left behind (Ch. 10, PDF pp. 98-99). Fear is called a dream killer that paralyzes, while tension is the promise of getting through it, and all effective education is said to create tension, because just before learning something you know you do not know it (Ch. 10, PDF p. 99). Examples of manufactured tension follow: the going-out-of-business sale, whose logic the author calls elusive since the store will not honor a warranty; getting into a social app early; a new invoice system to be good at by Thursday; houses on the block selling below expectation; Supreme making only 250 of a sneaker; and a series finale on Sunday. None of those feelings existed before a marketer created them, and the reason given is status (Ch. 10, PDF pp. 100-101). The choice is put as being a cab driver charging by the meter or an agent of change who creates tension and relieves it, with fancy Las Vegas casinos degrading the experience of travelers previously happy in Reno or downtown (Ch. 10, PDF p. 101). The chapter ends by saying the status quo does not shift because you are right, but because the culture changes, and that the engine of culture is status (Ch. 10, PDF pp. 101-102).

### Chapter Eleven: Status, Dominance, and Affiliation (PDF pp. 103-115)

Status is offered as the engine named at the end of the previous chapter. The evidence opens anecdotally with the author's dog Baxter, who gets along with every dog except a new German Shepherd across the street, and with Galapagos penguins, said to fish about two hours a day and spend the rest on social positioning (Ch. 11, PDF p. 103). Decisions that look irrational, a restaurant, a college, a car, a poker champion's bad bet, are said to make sense once status roles are visible (Ch. 11, PDF pp. 103-104). Keith Johnstone's *Impro* is the cited source for status roles, and the worked example is the opening scene of *The Godfather*, where the low-status undertaker Amerigo Bonasera asks a favor the Godfather cannot grant without losing status, and the tension is relieved only when Bonasera returns to his place by kissing the ring (Ch. 11, PDF pp. 104-105).

The case study is Lion Guardians (Ch. 11, PDF pp. 105-106). Conservation biologist Leela Hazzah faced a Maasai rite of passage in which an adolescent male kills a lion singlehandedly, against a regional population the book puts at about thirty thousand lions, down from two hundred thousand a generation or two earlier. The argument made is that rational argument cannot move a deeply held cultural belief, and that the cultural goals behind the rite, binding the community, bravery, patience, and raising a boy's status, are not connected to killing a lion, which the author calls a historical artifact. The team introduced a new rite in which the young demonstrate the same qualities by saving a lion, and the Maasai now find, name, track, and census lions with radio telemetry; the organization's own words about blending traditional knowledge with science are quoted.

Six claims about status follow (Ch. 11, PDF pp. 107-108): it is always relative, with no highest number; it is in the eye of the beholder, so external and internal readings can both be true; it matters only when the person engaged with cares about it; it has inertia, since people work to maintain a status high or low rather than change it; it is learned early and can be shifted quickly by a cohort; and shame is the status killer, used as a lever because it works. Two further points precede them: wanting higher status is not universal, and someone conditioned into a role may fight to keep or even lower it (Ch. 11, PDF p. 107); and status is not wealth, a Pulitzer-winning columnist, a doctor running a prestigious hospital, and a penniless yogi each outranking wealthier people in some circle, though the author says recent decades have lazily tied status to bank balances and follower counts (Ch. 11, PDF p. 107). Frank Sinatra, as chronicled by Gay Talese, is the example of the gap between external and internal status, seen by the world at an apex and by himself as a disrespected skinny kid (Ch. 11, PDF p. 108). Two XY grids map this: external against internal status, with four quadrants the author populates with Oprah Winfrey, impostor syndrome and the diva, artistic drive that can sour into bitterness, and Cinderella before the ball beside the coal miner; and a second grid of moving one's own status up or down against pushing others up or down, populated with the philanthropist and Superman, the person who does not try out because others deserve it more, the antisocial narcissist, and the hard-charging winner (Ch. 11, PDF pp. 108-111).

The chapter's second axis is affiliation against dominion (Ch. 11, PDF pp. 111-115). Tom Hanks is named for affiliation and the fictional Don Corleone for dominion. Affiliation asks who knows you, who trusts you, have you made things better, what your circle is like, and where you stand within the tribe; dominion says this is mine and not yours, asks who has more power, and is satisfied by a side winning even when the speaker is not in charge. The same person can measure differently by setting: a twelve-year-old caring only about beating the opposition on the ball field and about who sits next to him on the bus; a jazz band member counting solos while another keeps the group in sync. Who eats first and who sits closest to the emperor are given as the two forms of the same status question. Pro wrestling is read as a status battle among fans as well as wrestlers, since a hero's rise raises theirs. The author states that modern, urban, internet, arts, and innovation societies are built primarily on affiliation, which is not I am better but I am connected. Fashion is called usually about affiliation, where the leader's value is a signal that everyone else will be in sync. Uber is the named example of a brand built on dominance signals, its early years marked by contentious relationships with local governments, competitors, and drivers, which aligned with some investors, employees, and users. Affiliation signals are the busy trade show booth, who else is at the launch party, who blurbed the book, and whether people are talking; affiliation welcomes abundance because it admires the network effect, while dominion is scarcity. The instruments named are tombstone ads for an investment bank, referrals for a business-to-business seller, and one neighborhood for a local craftsperson. Dominion is vertical and affiliation horizontal, and the chapter ends by saying the customer's worldview, not the marketer's, decides which is in play.

### Chapter Twelve: A Better Business Plan (PDF pp. 116-118)

The shortest chapter proposes a five-section replacement for the conventional business plan, which the author says is often misused to obfuscate, bore, and demonstrate compliance (Ch. 12, PDF p. 116). Truth describes the world as it is, the market, the needs, the competitors, the technology standards, and how others succeeded and failed, taking no positions and running as long as it needs. Assertions is called the heart of it: we will do X and Y will happen, we will build Z for this money in this time, stated as the change expected from the project's arrival. Alternatives says what happens when the assertions do not pan out, and asks how much flexibility the product and team have. People covers who is on the team, meaning attitudes, abilities, and track record in shipping rather than resumes, and extends to who is being served, who the champions are, and what they believe about status. Money covers the amount needed, the spending, cash flow, profit and loss, balance sheets, margins, and exit strategies. The author notes a venture capitalist may not like the format (Ch. 12, PDF pp. 116-117). The chapter closes by naming the shift it expects a reader to have made, from having a marketing problem to asking what change is sought, and by dismissing reverse-engineered mission statements: most marketers share the same why, to be successful, respected, and profitable enough to do it again, and the work of a plan is to make that universal need specific in who and what it is for, the tension created, the status roles engaged, and the story told (Ch. 12, PDF pp. 117-118).

### Chapter Thirteen: Semiotics, Symbols, and Vernacular (PDF pp. 119-127)

The claim is that busy people scan rather than study, and that what they ask while scanning is what this reminds them of, so the logo, the stories, the appearance, and even the room layout at an off-site all carry meaning (Ch. 13, PDF pp. 119-120). The letters C-A-R stand in for a car, Nike is said to have spent billions teaching that the swoosh means human possibility, achievement, status, and performance, and comic sans is named as a designer's symbol of bad taste (Ch. 13, PDF p. 119). Examples of form changing content follow: a newspaper against a tablet, a comic book, or a Bible; a chocolate bar against a chemotherapy drug; a chiropractor's office that feels like a surgeon's; a book that feels self-published; the clicks and pauses before a robocall; and a site built with GeoCities and flashing GIFs. The author says big companies' logos look alike on purpose, to remind a viewer of a solid company (Ch. 13, PDF p. 120). What a professional supplies is a look other people will like rather than one the maker likes, and there is said to be no single professional look, since a summer blockbuster identifies itself in four frames (Ch. 13, PDF p. 121). Don LaFontaine, credited with more than five thousand movie and television voice-overs, is the example of a symbol compounding: his voice reminded audiences of his own earlier work (Ch. 13, PDF p. 121). Symbols are audience-specific: a hoodie signals status in Silicon Valley and may put someone on alert in East London.

The misspellings in advance-fee scam email are read as a deliberate signal to the skeptical and well-informed to go away, so that only the greedy and gullible remain, because the scammer would rather lose a mark at the start than at the end (Ch. 13, PDF pp. 121-122). Wheel flares on expensive cars are given as status flags with no function, since the flare sits more than six inches from the wheel, with aftermarket flares available and the observation that overdoing it lowers status, as with plastic surgery; the Cadillac XTS is said to carry a small flare on each tail light that recalls the Batmobile or the 1955 Lincoln Futura, and Alex Peck is credited with the observation that driving gloves keep a hole for a wristwatch nobody wears any more (Ch. 13, PDF p. 122). The rule drawn is to send a signal that feels like one already trusted and then change it enough to be new and yours (Ch. 13, PDF p. 123). Vernacular is why car ads look alike and why fashion ads in Vogue look nothing like ads in Field & Stream, and a good designer supplies the chance to fit in while a great one breaks the expectation without losing the audience (Ch. 13, PDF p. 123). Apple's 1984 Super Bowl ad by Lee Clow is the example: the author says almost no one watching caught the Orwell references, but media commentators and technology enthusiasts did, the ad needed only a million people to care, and it took thirty years for the idea to reach everyone, which he attributes to semiotics rather than technology (Ch. 13, PDF pp. 123-124).

The case study is Witchsy.com (Ch. 13, PDF p. 124). Penelope Gazin and Kate Dwyer could not get email answered, invented a third, fictional partner named Keith with his own address, and reported to Fast Company that vendors, developers, and potential partners replied faster to Keith, used his name, and were more helpful. The author reads it as evidence that judgments are often biased and inefficient and that denying them does not remove them, and says acknowledging the culture is not the same as fitting in or declining to challenge injustice. Not flying a flag is called as intentional as flying one, and insisting the features are good enough that nothing else matters is called foolish (Ch. 13, PDF pp. 124-125).

The chapter closes on brands and logos (Ch. 13, PDF pp. 125-127). A brand is defined as shorthand for the customer's expectation, the promise they think is being made; Nike has no hotel, and a reader's guesses about what one would be like are the brand. A commodity has no brand, since wheat by the ton or bandwidth by the gigabyte carries only a specification. Verizon and AT&T are called essentially worthless as brands on the test that a customer switched between them would not care. A logo is called the reminder of the promise and meaningless without one; the exercise offered is to list five admired logos and notice that each belongs to an admired brand. The author concedes that a terrible logo can adorn a fabulous brand and that Google, Sephora, and Costco have no memorable logo, and ends by advising a logo that works at different sizes in different media, chosen without many meetings and kept as long as a first name.

### Chapter Fourteen: Treat Different People Differently (PDF pp. 128-134)

The chapter opens on the normal distribution: in any hundred people measured on anything, about sixty-eight cluster near the average, twenty-seven sit further out, and four are extreme outliers (Ch. 14, PDF p. 128). Everett Rogers is cited for the finding that most people like what they have and are not seeking novelty, leaving fifteen or sixteen on the left of the curve as neophiliacs who queue for opening night, upgrade the operating system immediately, and read Vogue for the ads, and an equal number on the right who defend the status quo, still reading Reader's Digest and using a VCR (Ch. 14, PDF pp. 128-129). The author states that a marketer should not spend a minute on anyone off the left of the curve, since a satisfied person cannot be made dissatisfied within the reach of one marketer's time or money, and that such people may be reached one day horizontally, person to person, through earned media (Ch. 14, PDF p. 129).

Enrollment is the next term: there is no mandatory education, teaching against someone's will is nearly impossible, and the alternative is voluntary attention lent in exchange for insight or forward motion (Ch. 14, PDF p. 130). Enrollment is described as mutual and consensual, bought lazily with flashy ads and earned by finding people who want the change and connecting them to one another. Different people are then shown wanting different things: the neophiliac wants to go first, to show the innovation to the crew, and to be rewarded for it; the corporate cog wants to avoid trouble with the boss and to hold an alibi; the social crusader wants a glimmer of hope; the dominion-measuring person wants to win or, failing that, to watch an opponent lose; the affiliation-seeking tribe member wants to be in sync without being made leader. Some want a bargain and a few want to overpay to prove they can, almost nobody wants to feel stupid, and convenience has seduced many out of paying attention while others feel empty when they cannot contribute effort (Ch. 14, PDF pp. 130-131).

Superusers are the chapter's economic argument. The restaurant that keeps the critic's photo in the kitchen is contrasted with the present, where everyone can post a review, and the author rejects the inference that everyone must therefore be treated better, on the ground that treating everyone better is like treating everyone worse given fixed resources, and that while everyone has a platform not everyone uses it (Ch. 14, PDF pp. 131-132). The instruction is to watch what people do, and to adopt back the person adopting your cause, give something to talk about to the person talking, and give resources to the person itching to lead. A chart of customer contribution shows fixed marketing cost per person as a dotted line against gross margin per customer as bars, with only eight of the graphed customers contributing a profit, a shape the author says holds for book buyers, restaurant goers, political donors, philanthropists, and stamp collectors, summarized as the whales paying for the minnows (Ch. 14, PDF pp. 132-133). The closing example is a customer service call from a customer whose record shows years of tenure, high balances, no returns, on-time payment, high-margin purchases, and eight times the average revenue; the author notes a freelancer with six clients knows instantly when the big client calls, while a bank or retail store puts its least-respected and lowest-paid person on the phone, and lists the responses available, driving across town, catching the last FedEx pickup, having the chief executive call a customer who was triple-charged, while conceding it cannot be done for every customer (Ch. 14, PDF pp. 133-134).

### Chapter Fifteen: Reaching the Right People (PDF pp. 135-144)

The chapter first separates goal, strategy, and tactics (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 135-136). The goal is the change sought in the world; the strategy is the long-lasting way of investing toward it, such as earning trust and attention or being seen as the only alternative; the tactics are the dozens or hundreds of steps taken on the strategy's behalf, replaceable when one fails. The author adds that a competitor who learns your tactics can steal them, while one who learns your strategy cannot use it for want of guts and persistence. Two examples carry it: Coca-Cola's goal of more people drinking more Coke, pursued by a strategy of persuading the mass market that Coke was part of a happy culture everyone else was already in, with the content of each ad as a replaceable tactic; and Patagonia's goal of getting a small group of outdoor enthusiasts to express environmental care through its apparel, with a strategy of redefining how that group thinks about environmental impact and clothing quality and creating insiders, and tactics running from recycling clothes to placing stores in brick buildings, held while the strategy has stood for more than thirty years. Patagonia's own language about silent sports is quoted.

Advertising is then treated as an optional engine rather than a necessity (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 136-138). Media, telecommunications, and delivery businesses are all described as selling the attention of the people they ostensibly serve, so an advertiser buys attention instead of earning it, and gains the ability to scale an approach quickly once one works, against the difficulty of finding one that does. An unnoticed ad is said not to exist, and most television advertising is called semiotic noise that reassures a viewer this is a brand its peers know, a tax major companies in competitive markets pay and not a realistic model for anyone else. Online advertising is credited with three properties, psychographic precision, immediacy, and measurability, and then called the most ignored advertising ever created, since running in front of a hundred thousand people without a single click is not unusual; the reason given is that advertising is unearned media, and the recipient was not paid but is expected to pay with attention. On price, a fancy magazine is put at an $80 CPM, under a dime a reader, against $80 for a million people on a second-rate website who will neither remember nor act (Ch. 15, PDF p. 138).

The chapter's central distinction is Lester Wunderman's, whom the author names as the father of direct marketing and says he recruited to the board of Yoyodyne in 1995 (Ch. 15, PDF p. 139). Direct marketing is action oriented and measured; brand marketing is culturally oriented and cannot be measured. Counting Facebook clicks and conversions is direct; a highway billboard for a funeral parlor is brand. Each can produce the other's result as a side effect, and the danger named is confusing them. The growth of Google's and Facebook's revenue is attributed to ads that pay for themselves, with a hundred dollars of advertising generating $125 of profit so the advertiser keeps buying until it stops working. Brand advertisers such as Ford, Absolut Vodka, and Palmolive are said to be unable to build direct campaigns that work, which has made the shift online stressful; Procter and Gamble's model for Tide and Crest is named as falling apart when television brand ads are replaced by digital direct ads, and the local pizza place's move from a Yellow Pages ad with its own tracking phone number to Yelp is called time consuming and risky-feeling (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 139-140). The author says the two paragraphs of instruction that precede this, measure everything for direct and refuse to measure for brand while being consistent and patient, ought to have repaid the price of the book (Ch. 15, PDF p. 140). The online direct chain is then given in four lines: the ad exists to get a click, the click to make a sale or earn permission, the sale to lead to another sale or word of mouth, and permission to lead to education and a sale, with values assigned to each step and no direct-response ads run until they can be (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 140-141). Brand marketing's guide is that everything, from phone answering and hold music to packaging, location, executives' behavior, and packing peanuts, is brand marketing already; the question is whether to spend more and with intent, and the most important lesson given is that there is not enough time or money to build a brand for everyone, so be very specific and then overdo it (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 141-142).

Frequency closes the chapter (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 142-143). People remember what they rehearse, not what they read, hear, or see; Uncle Fred who came to Christmas twenty years running is remembered and his one-time date is not, and photographed events are remembered because the photo is rehearsed. Familiarity becomes trust, and marketers break it because they get bored before the audience gets the message. Jay Levinson is quoted at length on not changing ads until the accountant is tired of them, and the author generalizes it to all storytelling, naming a dip between when the teller gets bored and when people receive the message, with examples of people who give a talk a few times and move on, or start a freelance business that sputters, or raise money and spend it fast. The final section rejects generic search engine optimization: a thousand pages of results make being the first match a delusion, and a locksmith or hotel that wins a generic search profits hugely while everyone else spends on consultants and trickery, which the author calls a pyramid the math cannot support. The alternative given is to build something people search for by name, where Google's interest is aligned, in two steps, make a product worth searching for specifically, then be the one they want to find (Ch. 15, PDF pp. 143-144).

### Chapter Sixteen: Price Is a Story (PDF pp. 145-151)

Price is presented as a marketing tool rather than a way to collect money, on the pair of claims that marketing changes pricing and pricing changes marketing, and that the price should align with the extremes claimed in positioning (Ch. 16, PDF p. 145). The wine list is the opening test, since asking whether someone buys the cheapest or the most expensive bottle says nothing about the wine. Nobody drives the cheapest possible car and few drive a Bugatti around town, and a Porsche Cayenne is called a signal with no utility proportionate to its expense (Ch. 16, PDF p. 145). Price is then shown as the engine of growth through margin, in a baker's arithmetic: at $1.95 of ingredients and overhead per loaf, a two-dollar price earns a nickel, $2.50 earns fifty-five cents, an elevenfold increase, and three dollars earns more than a dollar, more than twenty times the first; the two-dollar baker must sell twenty-one loaves for each loaf the three-dollar baker sells (Ch. 16, PDF pp. 145-146). The margin is then spent visibly, on a clean shop, well-paid staff, a new sign, sponsored baseball jerseys, a handsome bag with every loaf, free butter cookies, and the story that this is the bread the fancy restaurant serves; the author's summary is that apologizing once for the price beats excusing a hundred small slights (Ch. 16, PDF p. 146).

The history of the price tag is credited to the Quakers, who thought charging different people different prices immoral, and its spread to Macy's and Wanamaker's, which needed scale and could not train low-paid staff to haggle (Ch. 16, PDF pp. 146-147). The internet is said to change this, with Tesla telling luxury buyers that the price is the price, and Uber's demand-matched pricing costing its brand billions in trust; the hard part for a small organization is named as the storytelling rather than the mechanics, tested by how it feels to receive a discount nobody else got, to miss one others received, or to face a nearly full Kickstarter tier (Ch. 16, PDF p. 147). Cheap is then read as scared: being cheapest promises the same rather than a change, the race to the bottom is tempting because nothing is easier to sell, and low price is called the last refuge of a marketer out of generous ideas (Ch. 16, PDF p. 147).

Free is given two objections and one structure (Ch. 16, PDF pp. 147-149). A transaction differs fundamentally from encountering a freely shared object, since scarcity, tension, and enrollment all exist only when a purchase is decided on, and without cash flow there is nothing to invest in product, team, or marketing. Free is nonetheless called a different category rather than a lower price, because it scales to infinity: the author asserts Facebook at three dollars a month would have attracted fewer than a million users, and that a paid Top 40 radio would disappear. The resolution offered is to pair free ideas that spread with expensive expressions of them that are worth paying for, illustrated by a chef giving away recipes and appearing on podcasts while charging twenty-four dollars for the pasta on china, and by a free radio song against an eighty-four-dollar concert ticket, with the china and the ticket called souvenirs that are supposed to be expensive.

Trust is then argued to run opposite to intuition (Ch. 16, PDF p. 149). Rather than requiring trust before a risky transaction, people invent trust because they are spending a lot, which the author attributes to cognitive dissonance; boot camp and Outward Bound are the examples of high participation cost producing alignment, and high-end restaurants and hotels surviving bad reviews the commercial one. The stated consequence is that lowering a price does not make a seller more trusted but does the opposite. Generosity is then distinguished from free work, constant discounts, and uncompensated overtime, which the author calls unsustainable and therefore promise-breaking, against generosity with bravery, empathy, and respect (Ch. 16, PDF pp. 149-150).

The case study is the Union Square Hospitality Group's elimination of tipping in 2016 (Ch. 16, PDF pp. 150-151). The group, built around the Union Square Cafe, best reviewed in the New York Zagat guide for more than a decade, and the spun-off Shake Shack, raised prices 20 percent and spent the revenue on parental leave, fair wages, better pay for the back of the house, and an incentive for waitstaff to work together rather than for tips. The author treats it as a marketing problem rather than only a leadership one, and lists the three audiences it has to be explained to: the regular who sees himself as an above-average tipper, the tourist comparing menu prices online without knowing tips are included, and the highest-earning servers whose wages may fall. The conclusion drawn is that the change cannot be for everyone, since a diner who enjoys the status of a flourished large tip loses that thrill, while a diner seeking affiliation may prefer a sincere thank-you to the fear of tipping wrongly, and a diner whose worldview centers on fairness and dignity finds other restaurants harder to patronize; dining being rarely solo, the host gains status and a story to tell about a small choice turning a ratchet on race, gender, and income disparity.

### Chapter Seventeen: Permission and Remarkability in a Virtuous Cycle (PDF pp. 152-158)

The chapter restates the author's 1999 book *Permission Marketing*: the privilege of delivering anticipated, personal, and relevant messages to people who want them, against spam, which he says was free and therefore endless (Ch. 17, PDF p. 152). His evidence is his own company, Yoyodyne, which he says was at one point sending, receiving, and processing more email than anyone on the planet, with permission from every recipient, open rates over 70 percent and an average 33 percent response rate, which he puts at about a thousand times a typical 2018 commercial email; he also notes the argument got him thrown out of the Direct Marketing Association (Ch. 17, PDF p. 152). Real permission is distinguished from presumed or legalistic permission: having an address, an absence of complaints, and fine print in a privacy policy are each said not to be permission, and the working test given is that people notice and ask where you went when you stop showing up (Ch. 17, PDF p. 153). Permission is likened to dating, earned over time rather than asked for at first impression, and its economics are the near-zero cost of each further contact once earned. Social platforms are called a sharecropper's arrangement, because the platform holds the permission and the asset rather than the marketer, and the author says every publisher and author of ideas needs the privilege of contacting people without a middleman (Ch. 17, PDF p. 153). Subscriptions are named an overt act of permission, which is why home-delivery newspaper readers and magazine subscribers are worth more than newsstand buyers (Ch. 17, PDF p. 154). The obligation is that the promise made to get permission is the promise kept until both sides agree to change it, whatever the quarter, product launch, or campaign, and the author names the retailer Woot as an example of a daily sales pitch that was promised and is therefore permitted. A metric is proposed: how many people would reach out or complain if the next send did not go out (Ch. 17, PDF p. 154). DailyCandy is the worked example, a city email newsletter started by Dany Levy after *Permission Marketing* was published, which the author says sold for more than a hundred million dollars, alongside a podcaster's subscriber base and a politician's activist voters (Ch. 17, PDF p. 154). The list is to be protected as an asset more valuable than the office laptops, with the same response to a team member who spams it as to one who walks out with the furniture (Ch. 17, PDF p. 155).

RapCaviar is the chapter's case (Ch. 17, PDF p. 155). Spotify hired Tuma Basa in 2015 as a defensive move against Apple's DJ-curated playlists; his RapCaviar playlist passed three million subscribers within months and nine million within three years, which the author calls the most important asset in the music business, larger than any radio station or magazine, updated every Friday morning with the landscape of hit music changed by the end of the day.

Permission is then said to be earned by showing up with generosity to the neophiliacs (Ch. 17, PDF p. 156). Marvel is the example: to launch a franchise it goes to San Diego Comic-Con rather than to nationwide television advertising, because Comic-Con already has permission from raving fans, and it arrives with a coming attraction, a director interview, and actual news, a year before release, to earn attention rather than sell tickets. The author notes that the share of the movie market present is tiny and says what matters is the quality of the story and the generosity. The second half of the chapter is remarkability, his own earlier term Purple Cow: spreading the word one person at a time is called too expensive and too slow, so the alternative is building something people decide is worth talking about, with the decision belonging to the user rather than the creator (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 156-157). Stunts are excluded as selfish; the reason people talk is that they are really talking about themselves, about their own taste or eye for an important idea, while criticism is a signal sent to friends that someone is to be shunned (Ch. 17, PDF p. 157). Two examples of talk designed in: Chuck Palahniuk's rule that you do not talk about Fight Club, which for the right worldview is an invitation to talk about it, and Alcoholics Anonymous, which the author calls hardly anonymous, since talking about it is built into an active member's practice as a generous act and a shame killer (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 157-158). Ideas are said to travel horizontally now, person to person rather than organization to customer, and the closing line of the chapter is that people spread the word only when it furthers their own goals and lets them tell a story they are proud of (Ch. 17, PDF p. 158).

### Chapter Eighteen: Trust Is as Scarce as Attention (PDF pp. 159-161)

The chapter's premise is a moment when more people are connected and fewer are trusted, which the author attributes to forces preferring dominion over affiliation manufacturing distrust in established channels, to exposed misbehavior among institutions, and to noise, fakes, and rip-offs (Ch. 18, PDF p. 159). Three paths are named for a marketer in that vacuum: ignored, sneaking around, and trusted (Ch. 18, PDF pp. 159-160). Being ignored earns neither trust nor attention; sneaking around can steal attention and a faux trust that does not last; trust is called the only one that repays the investment, and the cycle described runs from trust to enrollment to a promise kept to more trust, with attention letting a story be told uninterrupted and word of mouth following if it resonates. The author qualifies the benefit of the doubt: it is given only when people are already going where they think you are going, with their identity and status on the line (Ch. 18, PDF p. 160).

Trust is then located in action rather than talk, on the claim that people remember what you did long after they forget what you said, with the tests being what was done about a refund for a defective product, about lost data, and about a plant closing (Ch. 18, PDF p. 160). Talking is glossed as a press conference for the masses and not talking as what is done when nobody is watching, one person at a time. Fame is treated as a trust shortcut at small scale: the author asserts that everyone is famous to fifteen hundred people and some to three thousand, that being famous to the right three thousand is plenty for a consultant, designer, or inventor, and that the goal is being known to the smallest viable audience rather than maximizing social media numbers (Ch. 18, PDF p. 161). The chapter closes by separating publicity, meaning clips and write-ups, from public relations, the art of telling a story to the right people in the right way, which turns its back on ink at all costs; the author adds that the race to be slightly famous is fueled by internet connections and that the credit given to the famous will fade once everyone is (Ch. 18, PDF p. 161).

### Chapter Nineteen: The Funnel (PDF pp. 162-182)

The longest chapter opens with a leaky funnel: attention poured in at the top, committed customers out of the bottom, and most people leaking out along the way through diminished trust, a mismatch between offer and belief, a gap between what is said and what is heard, poor fit, distraction, or life (Ch. 19, PDF p. 162). Six repairs are listed: attract the right people, align the promise that brought them in with where they are going, remove steps so fewer decisions are required, reinforce dreams and ameliorate fears, use tension for forward motion, and hand those who made it through a megaphone (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 162-163). The illustration is Casey Neistat's numbers as the author observed them: about a million views on a YouTube video, eighteen thousand clicks through to a Twitch live stream, roughly a thousand comments, and perhaps five who take a further action, which he writes as 1,000,000 to 18,000 to 1,000 to 5, adding that what distinguishes Neistat is not an optimized funnel but a top that fills effortlessly with people enrolled in his journey (Ch. 19, PDF p. 163).

The direct marketing funnel is then worked through in arithmetic (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 163-164). Google and Facebook are said to have taken in more than a hundred billion dollars in 2017, about half of worldwide online advertising. A thousand dollars of ads reaching a million people yields twenty clicks at fifty dollars each; one in ten converts, so an order costs five hundred dollars, which works if lifetime value exceeds it. The author notes that most of the profit flows to the ad seller, which he gives as the reason those two companies are so successful, with Google perhaps making a hundred dollars of profit per sale to the advertiser's ten, and that the advertiser buys anyway because the margin is still positive. He adds that very few organizations do the math carefully, and that where fewer than 5 percent of advertisers measured results when he started, he guesses about 60 percent do now, with thoughtful analysis of the numbers still missing (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 164-165). Two warnings follow: lowering the cost of the first click with a ridiculous promise backfires once people stop trusting inside the funnel, and the better moves are shifting or eliminating steps, letting people engage with the ideas or community before being asked for money, and building new things for existing customers rather than finding new customers for existing things (Ch. 19, PDF p. 164).

Funnel math is then taught twice over (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 165-166). A supermarket customer is worth a dollar or two per trip and about a thousand dollars of profit over five years of twice-weekly hundred-dollar visits at a 2 percent margin, more still if they bring a neighbor, which the author uses to argue that such a store should sponsor an event for new residents and should refund an unripe four-dollar melon without an argument. Slack is the technology version: an early customer's lifetime value is put at fifty thousand dollars or more once coworkers' payments, shut-out competitors, and equity value are counted, and the first thousand right customers are called practically priceless. The cost side is taught with stamps: at fifty cents a letter and a thousand letters per order, an order costs five hundred dollars, which is worth buying at a seven-hundred-dollar lifetime value and not at four hundred, and the author credits that arithmetic for L.L. Bean, Lands' End, and Victoria's Secret. Online, clicks replace stamps, each intermediate click makes the funnel more expensive while removing too many leaves no trust, and the rule given is not to buy ads when the funnel cannot be seen or cannot be afforded (Ch. 19, PDF p. 166). The chapter then says plainly that the funnel is unlikely to be a magical fountain, because lifetime value rarely exceeds the cost of the ads needed to acquire a customer, so most brands that matter are primed by advertising and built by good marketing, growing through evangelism and tribes; attempts at a perpetual motion machine of profit are said to end in bitterness (Ch. 19, PDF p. 167).

Chris Anderson's long tail is the next frame (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 167-171). Number one is said to sell ten times number ten and a hundred times number one hundred; half of Amazon's sales are books outside the top five thousand, and half the volume of streamed music is unavailable in stores. The marketplace does well and the individual does not: selling one or two books a day is called no way to make a living, and a musician with twelve or twenty-four songs on the tail pays no bills. The vivid example is a T-shirt on Amazon reading that it is Easter, Passover, April Fools', and the wearer's birthday, which the author reasons could have sold about four copies, until further searching shows companies making tens of thousands of different shirts to order; his conclusion is that aggregating a chunk of the tail can work while selling one obscure shirt cannot, and that becoming an outlier is a wish rather than a strategy. The way out offered is to divide the market into many curves, each with its own short head and long tail, since hits are hits because people like hits and a hit is what connects a market to itself; the two elements named are making the definitive contribution to a field and connecting the market so it sees that this is the hit that holds it together, with *Rocky Horror* and a named DeWalt hammer drill as short heads in their own markets (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 170-171).

Geoffrey Moore's chasm is credited and explained (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 171-173). There are not enough neophiliacs to sustain a mass business, and the mass market wants a pattern match rather than a pattern interrupt, so the thing that satisfied the early adopters is the thing that annoys the masses, who do not want to trade in DVDs, learn a new platform, or read the news online; the author sends the reader to an Apple store help desk to watch the collision, and says the middle of the curve is barely adapting rather than eagerly adopting. The bridge he names is network effects: there is no reason to tell anyone about a chocolate you like and every reason to tell them about Snapchat, Instagram, or Twitter, because your life improves if they follow. Non-technology examples are a bus trip to a protest, which is more effective and more fun with more people, and a CSA farm share, where more members buy better variety. The bridge is reduced to two questions, what will I tell my friends and why will I tell them, with the note that nobody tells a friend because the marketer wants them to or worked hard. The Gartner Hype Cycle is then read as the same shape: the trigger reaches the neophiliacs, who hype the work, the rest of the market cannot match the hype, and the trough is the moment when the neophiliacs are bored and the mass market is disdainful (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 174-175).

A failure of the author's own is recorded next (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 175-176). HugDug, from his team at Squidoo, let anyone build a page in about four minutes profiling an Amazon product, with half the resulting affiliate royalty going to the builder's chosen charity, which he says was years before Amazon's own charity program and twenty times as generous. The bet was that authors would promote their own books and that Pinterest-style builders would enjoy it. It failed after months: thousands of pages were built, so trial was there, but fewer than six people built more than a dozen pages or promoted them much, tension dissipated after a single visit, a page often sold no books in a month, and people were reluctant to push friends toward a shopping site. The author's stated lessons are that four months was naive for an overnight success, that the incentives were too weak, that no tension dynamic turned early users into ambassadors, and that the story about status and the specificity about who the first customers were both fell short.

Facebook is the case study of crossing the largest chasm (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 177-178). The author says few brands cross fully, naming Starbucks, Heineken, and the bagel as not having made it, and puts Facebook's leap around 2008, when the reason for signing up moved from mild interest to necessity to being the last person not using it. Starting at Harvard is called part of the secret, since an insecure student had an urgent need to know where he stood; each Ivy League school was a local chasm crossed by a neophiliac going first and then a status ratchet, with more friends meaning higher status and the people already there holding an admired status. He describes the final leap to the public as the billion-dollar one, achieved by combining nerdiness with status so that joining or facing social isolation were the options, and says most marketers are unlikely to make that leap because the market is too big and the typical network effect too weak.

Two local crossings close the chapter. A yo-yo arrives at an elementary school with the wrong kid on the wrong day and goes nowhere, then a charismatic fifth-grader starts an exclusive club open to all, brings three spare yo-yos for chosen friends who are leaders themselves, and within two weeks the school is doing it; the author notes yo-yos are a fad that cools as fast as it spreads unless identity and persistence are built in, and names Uggs, black backpacks, and penny skateboards as the same crossing (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 178-179). Water Health International brings a purification kiosk to a village where a billion people's norm is parasite-infested water fetched on a walk of several hours; residents buy a special colored jerry can and pay for daily refills, which is quickly repaid in time, productivity, and medical expense, and the adoption follows the same curve as toys and computers, with the distinctive cans working as a badge and an invitation to talk (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 179-180). The organization sends a representative with a microscope projector to the school, students bring water from home, and the projected parasites send children home to tell their parents; status then does the rest, as respected villagers carry the cans and a household without clean water hesitates to host. The author calls this the original network effect, built on proximity rather than software, and says the village crosses the local chasm within months.

A closing aside treats business-to-business marketing as no different, against the impression of requests for proposal, specification-meeting, price wars, and long cycles (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 180-182). LEED certification from the Green Building Council is the example: at launch only two buildings a day were submitted, a rate that would have taken a hundred years to reach what twelve years produced, and the number leapt just before the housing crisis. The author's explanation is the developer's worldview: if only a few tenants or buyers insist on certification, having it confers status and lacking it invites regret and the fear of a building seen as defective, so there is a race to the top. The chapter ends on the one question he says every business buyer asks, what will I tell my boss, with the marketer out of ideas finishing the sentence with having bought the cheapest one and everyone else finishing it with status, fear, affiliation, belonging, dominion, safety, commitment, or insight.

### Chapter Twenty: Organizing and Leading a Tribe (PDF pp. 183-189)

The chapter opens by denying ownership: the tribe does not belong to the marketer, who therefore does not get to direct its members or use them for his own aims, and the goal offered is that the tribe would survive your leaving and would miss you (Ch. 20, PDF p. 183). Marshall Ganz, named as the Harvard professor who worked with Cesar Chavez and Barack Obama, supplies the three-part narrative: the story of self, which gives standing by describing the transition from who you were to who you became, and which the author distinguishes from catastrophizing or the faux empathy of online vulnerability; the story of us, which explains why the self-story is relevant and what the listener gains from being one of us; and the story of now, the pivot that enlists the tribe under peer opportunity and peer pressure (Ch. 20, PDF pp. 183-184). A three-paragraph worked example runs a person from fifty pounds overweight through competitive figure skating and new friendships to an invitation to the rink today, with rental skates already reserved (Ch. 20, PDF pp. 184-185).

Saul Alinsky's thirteen rules from *Rules for Radicals* are reproduced in full, quoted as the outside source they are, and then rejected: the author says the approach is now used by both sides of nearly any issue and tears at civil discourse, since everyone ends up standing in a burning building (Ch. 20, PDF p. 185). He then writes thirteen reversals of his own, among them putting people to work rather than paying them, helping others write rules that let them reach their goals, criticizing only when it educates, sticking with tactics long after everyone else is bored and stopping only when they stop working, letting pressure cease now and then, making no threats, solving your own problems before finding problems for others, and disagreeing with institutions rather than people (Ch. 20, PDF p. 186).

The rest of the chapter is maintenance. A tribe shares interests, goals, and language and need not have a leader, and the marketer's opportunity is connecting members who are lonely and afraid of being unseen, including by creating cultural artifacts, a costume, code words, a secret handshake, with the note that not everything should be said or made obvious and that commitment and longevity may earn an edge (Ch. 20, PDF p. 187). Nike's Breaking2, the funded attempt at a two-hour marathon, is the example of challenging a tribe, with the author saying that even failing leaves Nike and the tribe ahead. Tribes are said to fade without maintenance: early adopters are always leaving for the next thing, and those who admired the status quo drift back once the tension is gone, so any tribal behavior not energetically maintained loses half its activity over some unknown half-life, and the alternative given is reinvestment, with the best marketers described as farmers rather than hunters (Ch. 20, PDF pp. 187-188). The closing story is Zig Ziglar selling pots and pans door to door in the 1960s: while most of his company's three thousand representatives filled a car with samples, took the easy sales in a town, and drove on, Ziglar took a room for weeks, kept running demonstration dinners, and met someone in the middle of the curve five, six, or seven times in a month, which the author says is what that person needs before deciding; days with no sales were acceptable because volume after the local chasm repaid the time (Ch. 20, PDF pp. 188-189).

### Chapter Twenty-One: Some Case Studies Using the Method (PDF pp. 190-193)

Three short applications. Getting an agent (Ch. 20's method applied at Ch. 21, PDF pp. 190-191): Brian Koppelman is credited with the observation that an agent fields calls rather than working as a full-time sales representative, so the method is making work magical enough that agents and producers come looking, and the author adds that it works best when the work is not fully polished, because the best work creates an imbalance in the viewer that only telling someone else relieves, which raises the asker's status when they ask whether you have seen it.

Tesla (Ch. 21, PDF pp. 191-192): the Model S is described as breaking the cars luxury neophiliacs already owned, in the sense that those cars stopped being fun to own or worth bragging about, and the author states that nobody who bought one of the first fifty thousand Teslas needed a car. Elon Musk is credited with changing a story that undid a group's status as early adopters, technology enthusiasts, environmentalists, and supporters of audacity at once, by positioning the car at three extremes, fastest, safest, and most efficient of its size. Existing car companies are said to have had the knowledge and the resources and not the self-image, since car companies like them do not take risks like that, and their customers felt the same; the author notes that concept cars exist to normalize innovations years ahead.

The National Rifle Association (Ch. 21, PDF p. 192): the author calls it controversial and says that as focused nonprofit and political marketers it has no peer, with about five million members, less than 2 percent of the population, used to change the focus of thousands of lawmakers. The mechanisms he names are comfort with saying it is not for you, activating members and making it easy to talk to friends, and intentionally creating people like us with statements he calls at their best viciously divisive; a Pew study is cited for gun owners being more than twice as likely as nonowners to contact government officials about their issues. He states that the NRA is not his version of better and that it clearly resonates with those it serves. The chapter closes on marketing to a boss, described as no different: she wants what she has always wanted, sees through her own experience, knows who her people like us are, and pursues status, safety, and respect, so an approach built on price, features, false urgency, or authority without responsibility is unlikely to work (Ch. 21, PDF pp. 192-193).

### Chapter Twenty-Two: Marketing Works, and Now It's Your Turn (PDF pp. 194-195)

A two-page chapter. Perfect is said to close the door by asserting that this is the best that can be done, and to forbid trying, since seeking perfection and missing is a failure; better opens the door (Ch. 22, PDF p. 194). Good enough is defended as a chain rather than an excuse: engagement, then trust, then seeing, then learning, then a promise, then enrollment, which is what better requires, with the instruction to ship the work and then improve it (Ch. 22, PDF p. 194). The closing page is on help, generous to offer and an act of trust to ask for, with the observation that where neither is done everyone is on defense and nothing can be made better (Ch. 22, PDF p. 195).

### Chapter Twenty-Three: Marketing to the Most Important Person (PDF pp. 196-200)

The final chapter answers whether marketing is evil (Ch. 23, PDF pp. 196-197). The author's position is that marketing works, which is why the question matters, and that some marketers are evil rather than the craft: he names persuading children to start smoking, cynically manipulating a political process, lying in ways that cause disastrous side effects, and selling an ineffective potion where an effective medicine exists. Against those he sets polio vaccination, handwashing before surgery, and electing someone who improves a community, and credits Josiah Wedgwood with inventing marketing a few centuries ago. A Time magazine piece about his blog is quoted asking tongue in cheek whether marketing is evil, and he amends the question to whether marketers are. His test is that marketing works for society when marketer and consumer both know what is happening and are both satisfied with the outcome; selling cosmetics is allowed because the process rather than beauty brings the joy, and swindling someone out of a house for a commission is not. Doing your job is explicitly refused as a defense, and he notes that he is not the one who decides, that customers and their neighbors are, and predicts that public marketing will defeat the kind that depends on the shadows.

The closing section turns the book's method on the reader (Ch. 23, PDF pp. 197-200). Questions about the noise in one's head and the hesitation before saying that you made this are called marketing questions, since leaving them unanswered lets less gifted people who show up as professionals run circles around you. Failure is assigned to the work rather than the person: the empathy may have been missing, the axes wrong, the edges unreached, the story wrong for the person or the day, and the craft can be improved, as a pot breaking in the kiln is not evidence of a bad person. The argument that opened Chapter Five returns as the ending: a donor gives only when the value exceeds the cost and a buyer buys only when the thing is worth more than the price, so not marketing a change you could contribute is called stealing from the person who needed to learn from you. The last pages allow that the thing being marketed may satisfy no real demand and rest on no good strategy, in which case the instruction is to blow it up and start over, and the book ends on the marketing a person does to themselves, since a story told enough times becomes true.

### A Marketing Reading List, worksheet, and acknowledgments (PDF pp. 201-204)

The reading list, given in no particular order, runs to about forty titles and includes Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm*, Chris Anderson's *The Long Tail* and *Free*, Claude Hopkins, David Ogilvy, Trout and Ries's *Positioning*, Steve Blank's *The Four Steps to the Epiphany*, Gladwell's *The Tipping Point*, Bernadette Jiwa, Zig Ziglar, Steve Krug, Simon Sinek, Fred Reichheld, Osterwalder and Pigneur, and four of the author's own books, of which he names *All Marketers are Liars* as the most on point (PDF pp. 201-202). The worksheet is fourteen questions on one page, beginning with who it is for and what it is for, and running through the audience's worldview and fears, the story and whether it is true, the change sought, how status changes, how the early adopters and neophiliacs will be reached, why and what they will tell their friends, where the network effect is, what asset is being built, and whether the maker is proud of it (PDF p. 203). The acknowledgments credit Michael Schrage for the germ of the change idea, Bernadette Jiwa for the work on stories, and Tom Peters broadly, and name dozens of others including the students and coaches of TheMarketingSeminar.com (PDF p. 204).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope of the book.** A reader who cannot yet deliver quality that meets specification is told the book is not much help, since quality is called required but no longer sufficient (Ch. 6, PDF p. 60). Nobody can change the culture; each person can change a culture, a pocket of the world (Ch. 9, PDF p. 88).
- **Rationality.** The rejection of the rational agent is hedged: averaged over a large enough group, the author allows that glimmers of that behavior might appear, while saying he would not bet on it (Ch. 3, PDF p. 29). Grouping people into stereotyped clumps is said to work often but not always (Ch. 2, PDF p. 22).
- **The smallest viable market.** The grandiose change is not ruled out, only called a heavy burden and a convenient excuse; the author says he is a fan of game-changing home runs before the "But" (Ch. 4, PDF p. 30). A person who understands the offer and declines is not a failure of the marketing: it is not for them, not at that price or with that structure, and the author says that is fine (Ch. 5, PDF p. 43).
- **Better.** Better is not transitive, so more expensive is not better and subjective attributes are not linear at all (Ch. 5, PDF p. 44). The dog food chapter says its own purpose is not to help anyone market dog food (Ch. 5, PDF p. 45). The critic who dislikes the work is called correct about that (Ch. 8, PDF p. 84).
- **Positioning.** The Ogilvy and Reeves method of inventing claims to fill a hole, and the Trout and Ries method of cornering a competitor, are called fine and then said not to hold up in a hyper-competitive world, which is why the author substitutes claims that are true and generous (Ch. 5, PDF p. 54). Two music teachers on the same board are both right (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 54-55).
- **Innovation's distance.** A leap too far leaves the tribe behind, since everything sits in a hierarchy between yesterday and tomorrow (Ch. 9, PDF pp. 91-92). The rule for a new signal is to feel like one already trusted and then differ enough to be new (Ch. 13, PDF p. 123).
- **Authenticity.** The archetype is called a myth with "a few exceptions" that prove the rule, and the author allows that the professional's presented version may overlap with how the person feels in the moment (Ch. 6, PDF p. 65). A person whose best work requires being authentic is called a fortunate amateur rather than wrong (Ch. 6, PDF p. 66).
- **Professionals and amateurs.** Hiring a professional designer is advised, with the admission that every once in a while an amateur happens on a vernacular that reminds the right people of the right story (Ch. 13, PDF p. 121).
- **Logos.** A terrible logo can adorn a fabulous brand, and several of the best brands, Google, Sephora, and Costco among them, are said to have no identifiable or memorable logo (Ch. 13, PDF p. 126).
- **Status.** Wanting higher status is not universal: some people fight to maintain or even lower theirs (Ch. 11, PDF p. 107). Status matters only when the person being engaged with cares about it (Ch. 11, PDF p. 107). Status is not wealth (Ch. 11, PDF p. 107). The benefit of the doubt is given only where identity and status are already on the line (Ch. 18, PDF p. 160).
- **The right of the curve.** People satisfied with what they have are not written off permanently: the author says you may reach them one day, horizontally, person to person, through earned media, but not right now (Ch. 14, PDF p. 129).
- **Superusers.** The advice to treat different people differently ends with the concession that it cannot be done for every customer (Ch. 14, PDF p. 134).
- **Advertising.** Advertising is called an optional engine, not something the author says to avoid: he states plainly that the difficulty of finding an approach that works does not mean a reader should not try (Ch. 15, PDF p. 137). Television brand advertising is called a tax that major companies in competitive markets have to pay and not realistic for anyone else (Ch. 15, PDF p. 137). Full-page ads, network television, and sponsoring a tournament or a podcast are allowed as possibly the best use of the money (Ch. 15, PDF p. 141). Brand ads carry one condition: a marketer who cannot afford to be consistent and patient is told not to buy them (Ch. 15, PDF p. 140). Direct ads carry another: do not run them until a value can be assigned to each step (Ch. 15, PDF p. 141), and where the funnel cannot be seen or afforded, the funnel is fixed before the ads are bought (Ch. 19, PDF p. 166).
- **Price.** Cheap is called scared with an explicit exception for a seller who has found an extraordinary new way to deliver the thing (Ch. 16, PDF p. 147). Free is argued against on two grounds and then said to be worth considering in other situations, and the paired structure of free ideas and paid expressions is the author's own answer to the paradox (Ch. 16, PDF pp. 147-149).
- **The Union Square change.** The elimination of tipping is said not to be for everyone: a diner who enjoys the status of a large visible tip loses that, and the author puts the refusal in the restaurant's mouth (Ch. 16, PDF p. 151).
- **Permission.** Permission is said not to require formality, only obviousness, and not to be a one-way broadcast, since different people can be given different formats and frequencies (Ch. 17, PDF pp. 153-154).
- **Remarkability.** Whether something is remarkable is explicitly not the creator's decision (Ch. 17, PDF p. 157).
- **The funnel.** The funnel is called unlikely to be a magical fountain, because lifetime value rarely exceeds the cost of the ads needed to win a customer, and self-running passive funnels are called rare (Ch. 19, PDF p. 167). Lowering the cost of the first click by overpromising is said to backfire (Ch. 19, PDF p. 164).
- **The long tail.** Aggregating a chunk of the tail can work; selling one obscure item cannot, and becoming an outlier is called a wish rather than a strategy (Ch. 19, PDF p. 170).
- **The chasm.** The author says the Facebook-scale leap to the whole mass market is unlikely for most marketers, because the market is too big and the typical network effect not that strong, and redirects to local chasms (Ch. 19, PDF p. 178). His own HugDug project is recorded as a failure, with four reasons of his own naming (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 175-176).
- **Tribes.** The tribe is said not to belong to the marketer, and would probably survive his leaving (Ch. 20, PDF p. 183). Movements fade rather than take on a life of their own, so the half-life is treated as the normal case (Ch. 20, PDF pp. 187-188). Nike's Breaking2 is offered as worthwhile even if it fails (Ch. 20, PDF p. 187).
- **The NRA example.** The author states that the NRA is not his version of better while presenting it as a role model for focus (Ch. 21, PDF p. 192).
- **Plans and purpose.** A plan that does not work is said not to mean a person is without purpose, only that one route has been ruled out (Ch. 12, PDF p. 118). Perfect is rejected and good enough is defended as a starting point rather than a shortcut (Ch. 22, PDF p. 194).
- **Ethics.** Being able to market something is said not to mean it should be marketed, and the author notes that he is not the one who decides what is evil (Ch. 23, PDF p. 197).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each example, tool, or figure the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure. This session's web-search budget was exhausted before the last group below could be reached, so those are listed together with that reason.

- **Stack Overflow as a profitable company with more than 250 employees and millions of visits a week (Ch. 6, PDF p. 63).** The company changed hands and the traffic collapsed. Prosus bought it on June 2, 2021 for $1.8 billion, and questions submitted by users fell 78 percent between December 2024 and December 2025, against a peak of 200,000 questions a month in 2014, a decline attributed to the site's moderation culture and to competition from generative AI tools (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_Overflow). The employee count and the weekly visit figure were not re-checked against a first-party source and are unchecked. The design argument, three constituencies given three different stories, is unaffected.
- **Be More Chill as the hit musical you cannot see, with a hundred million streams and an off-Broadway run coming (Ch. 5, PDF pp. 48-49).** It went further and then closed. Previews began at Broadway's Lyceum Theatre on February 13, 2019 and the show opened March 10, closing on August 11, 2019 after 30 previews and 177 performances, on a transfer the lead producer estimated at $9.5 million; the cast recording has since passed 350 million streams (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Be_More_Chill_(musical)). The spread the book describes was real; the Broadway run it enabled lasted five months.
- **DailyCandy sold for more than a hundred million dollars (Ch. 17, PDF p. 154).** The price holds and the asset does not. Comcast bought it for $125 million in a deal finalized September 12, 2008, and NBCUniversal shut it down in 2014 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DailyCandy).
- **The NRA with about five million members, less than 2 percent of the population (Ch. 21, PDF p. 192).** The figure is the organization's own claim and the organization has since been through court. It claimed nearly 5 million members as of December 2018, a figure Wikipedia notes has not been independently confirmed, and now self-reports about 5.5 million; it filed for Chapter 11 in Texas on January 15, 2021, dismissed on May 11, 2021 as not filed in good faith, and in February 2024 a Manhattan jury found NRA leaders liable for financial misconduct and corruption (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Rifle_Association). The Pew finding that gun owners are more than twice as likely to contact officials is unchecked.
- **Slack as the fastest-growing product of its kind (Ch. 10, PDF p. 97).** The growth ended in an acquisition: Salesforce announced the purchase on December 1, 2020 for $27.7 billion and closed it July 21, 2021 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slack_Technologies). The network-effect mechanism the chapter describes is untouched by the ownership change.
- **Nike's Breaking2 as a challenge worth setting even if it fails (Ch. 20, PDF p. 187).** It did fail, twice over on ratification. Eliud Kipchoge ran 2:00:25 on May 6, 2017, unratified because of rotating pacemakers, and 1:59:40.2 at the INEOS 1:59 Challenge in 2019, also unratified; the first ratified sub-two-hour marathon was Sabastian Sawe's 1:59:30 at the 2026 London Marathon, run in Adidas shoes (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breaking2).

The worldview reading the book gives is the same one that source gives; the number is not. See section 8.

The Economist traced breakthrough technologies in 2024 and found that perhaps a fifth move from innovation through excitement and despondency to widespread adoption, and an analysis of Gartner Hype Cycles since 2000 found that few technologies travel an identifiable one; the model is also described as not scientific in nature (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hype_cycle).

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **A figure the later sources do not support.** JCPenney's sales are said to have fallen by more than 50 percent under Ron Johnson (Ch. 4, PDF p. 33); the worst quarter on record for that company was a 32 percent same-store fall, and the full-year decline was smaller still, as section 7 records. The book's reading of why it happened matches the later account; the number does not.
- **Figures with no source at all.** Almost every quantity in the book is stated without a citation: the statistics from the National Association of Realtors are described only as given to the author (Ch. 7, PDF p. 72); the twenty-four billion dollars of dog food (Ch. 5, PDF p. 45); everyone being famous to fifteen hundred people and some to three thousand (Ch. 18, PDF p. 161); the sixty-eight, twenty-seven, and four out of a hundred and the fifteen or sixteen neophiliacs (Ch. 14, PDF p. 128); Yoyodyne's rates at about a thousand times a typical commercial email (Ch. 17, PDF p. 152). The Facebook counterfactual, that a three-dollar monthly fee would have held it under a million users (Ch. 16, PDF p. 148), is an unobservable stated as a fact.
- **A splice of two different distributions.** Chapter Fourteen opens with the normal distribution, sixty-eight percent within one standard deviation, and then attaches Everett Rogers's adoption categories to the same picture as though they were the same curve (Ch. 14, PDF pp. 128-129). They are not: Rogers's segments are defined by adoption order with their own fixed shares, and the standard-deviation arithmetic the chapter prints is doing no work in the argument that follows.
- **One case generalized.** The VisionSpring afternoon is the book's foundational demonstration, and it is one table, one village, one day, with the before and after shares given as a third and double a third, no count, no control, and no repetition reported (Ch. 3, PDF pp. 24-25). The blue ribbons are credited with turning a failed school budget into a two-to-one pass in eight days, with no other change in that window named (Ch. 9, PDF p. 89). Robin Hood's $101 million is attributed to peer pressure rather than to the gala on the author's reading alone (Ch. 9, PDF pp. 93-94).
- **Correlation presented as cause.** Ron Johnson's worldview is given as the cause of the JCPenney collapse (Ch. 4, PDF pp. 33-34) in a year that also carried a rebrand, a store redesign, and a staff reorganization the book does not mention. Facebook's crossing is attributed to the status dynamics of insecure Ivy League students (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 177-178) with no comparison to the contemporaries that started elsewhere and failed. Stew Leonard's decline is attributed to a later generation's focus on short-term profit with no other factor considered (Ch. 7, PDF pp. 76-77).
- **Framing that nothing can contradict.** People like us do things like this is presented as covering conformity and its opposite at once, since an outlier is said to be aligning with the behavior of outliers and even a sociopath is not unaware of the crowd (Ch. 9, PDF p. 88); no observed behavior falls outside it. The same shape appears in the claim that everyone is right, every time, given what they see, believe, and want (Ch. 8, PDF p. 86), and in the claim that nothing is considered before status roles (Ch. 11, PDF p. 108).
- **Internal tension between tension and shame.** Tension is distinguished from fear on the ground that the marketer cares about the person (Ch. 10, PDF p. 99), and is then built from being left out, left behind, uninformed, or impotent (Ch. 10, PDF pp. 100-101) and from making a villager without clean water feel socially isolated and stupid (Ch. 19, PDF p. 180). The book separately names shame as the status killer and as a lever people use because it works (Ch. 11, PDF p. 108), and rejects Alinsky's rules as manipulation that kills a tribe (Ch. 20, PDF p. 185). The test that separates the admired case from the rejected one is the marketer's own intent, which the book supplies no way to check from outside.
- **Advice with no boundary.** Chapter Fifteen instructs the reader to measure everything for direct marketing and to refuse to measure brand marketing (Ch. 15, PDF p. 140), and then defines brand marketing as everything from hold music to packing peanuts (Ch. 15, PDF p. 141), which leaves no line at which measuring resumes. The author says those two paragraphs ought to have paid for the book (Ch. 15, PDF p. 140).
- **No method under the central instruction.** The smallest viable market is defined as the minimum number of people worth the effort (Ch. 4, PDF p. 34) with nothing offered for computing what is worth the effort. The book says funnel math flummoxes people and that thoughtful analysis of the numbers is what is missing (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 165, 164), and prints no benchmark of any kind. The closing worksheet asks fourteen questions and supplies no procedure for answering one of them (PDF p. 203).
- **The author is his own evidence and his own product.** The material is drawn from a hundred-day seminar whose site the book names on its first chapter page and again at the end of the front matter (Ch. 1, PDF pp. 13-14; PDF p. 12); the permission chapter's evidence is his own company (Ch. 17, PDF p. 152), the failure chapter's is his own project (Ch. 19, PDF pp. 175-176), the change example is his wife's bakery (Ch. 4, PDF p. 31), and the reading list names four of his own books, one of them as the most on point (PDF p. 201). The overlap is visible rather than hidden, and it is never weighed.
- **Claims the evidence does not reach.** That spam does not scale (Ch. 17, PDF p. 152) is asserted rather than shown, in a chapter whose only numbers come from the author's own company twenty years earlier. That being market-driven lasts (Ch. 3, PDF p. 28) has no case attached. That the credit given to the famous will fade once everyone is famous (Ch. 18, PDF p. 161) is a prediction with no mechanism.
- **Numbers and examples that have moved.** The Be More Chill future, the RapCaviar subscriber curve, the Hamilton reach, Swift's tour revenue, Stack Overflow's traffic, and the hundred billion dollars of 2017 advertising were true or close to true in 2018 and are not the current picture; section 7 carries the later figures. Witchsy, DailyCandy, the Cadillac XTS, Experts Exchange's business model, and the Big Machine arrangement no longer exist in the form the book describes.
- **An endorsed model that later analysis does not support.** The Gartner Hype Cycle is called a brilliant meta-analysis of how the culture changes and is used to explain the chasm (Ch. 19, PDF p. 174); section 7 records the later finding that few technologies travel an identifiable one.
- **Figures the book rounds into rhetoric.** The chapter on the long tail says half of Amazon's sales are books outside the top five thousand and half of streamed music is unavailable in stores, without a source or a date (Ch. 19, PDF p. 168), and then estimates the sales of one novelty T-shirt from three invented conditional probabilities and reports the product as four copies sold (Ch. 19, PDF p. 169).
