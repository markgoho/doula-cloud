# Refactoring UI, read back

## 1. Source

*Refactoring UI*, Adam Wathan and Steve Schoger, version 1.0.2. Self-published and sold from refactoringui.com; the PDF carries no copyright page, no publisher, no ISBN and no printed date — its front matter is a cover, a blank page and a three-page Contents, nothing else. The first release was December 2018 and version 1.0.2 followed in 2019 (Goodreads and Open Library metadata, checked 2026-09-10; the book itself says neither). Read 2026-09-10 from the `pdftotext -layout` extraction of the 218-page PDF, front to back, with the PDF opened for every chapter because the book is mostly figures and the text alone is not the book.

**Page rule.** The book prints page numbers, and this file cites the printed page. Printed page = PDF index, offset zero, found by opening PDF page 8, which prints "8" in its running header, and PDF page 25, which prints "25"; the Contents on PDF pages 3–5 lists "Limit your choices" at 24 and "Ditch hex for HSL" at 119, and both land on those PDF indices. Three kinds of page carry no printed number and are cited by their index anyway: the front matter (PDF 1–5), the nine part-title pages (6, 29, 55, 87, 118, 149, 173, 191, 215) and the first page of every chapter, which prints the chapter title instead of a number — so a reviewer opening p. 41 for "accessibility pitchfork" finds a title, not a "41". The book has no numbered chapters. It has eight named parts (Starting from Scratch, Hierarchy is Everything, Layout and Spacing, Designing Text, Working with Color, Creating Depth, Working with Images, Finishing Touches) and a closing "Leveling Up", each part holding titled chapters of two to eight pages, 49 chapters in all before "Leveling Up". A citation names the part and the page: "(Working with Color, p. 121)".

## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, chapter by chapter, with its own exceptions, what has changed since 2018, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR, ticket or file that decided it, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the authors' frame, is that making an interface look good does not take "innate artistic talent" but a small number of concrete, learnable moves, the largest of which is visual hierarchy, which they call the most effective tool for making something feel designed (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 30). The reader is a developer working without a designer (Leveling Up, p. 216), and the method is to replace open-ended choice with systems defined in advance — for font size, weight, line height, color, spacing, width, height, shadows, radius, border width and opacity — so that choosing among a few visibly different options becomes easy (Starting from Scratch, pp. 27–28). The authors say systems are a "recurring theme" of the book and that later chapters build each one "in finer detail" (Starting from Scratch, p. 28).

The authors say what the claim is not. It is not accessibility guidance: the chapter on labels opens by waving off "the accessibility pitchfork" and declaring that it is not about forms (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 41). It is not a science of color: the reader is told to attend to how colors feel to them (Starting from Scratch, p. 19), that picking a base shade has "no real scientific way" (Working with Color, p. 130), that the palette method "is not a science", and to trust their eyes over the numbers (Working with Color, p. 132). The wording of an interface is admitted to be not a visual technique "per se" and is included anyway because words matter as much as, or more than, color or typeface (Starting from Scratch, pp. 22–23). And the constraints it teaches are not absolute: the last chapter of advice ends by saying constraints are powerful and a little freedom is sometimes what a design needs (Finishing Touches, p. 214).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Starting from Scratch (pp. 6–28)

**Start with a feature, not a layout (pp. 7–9).** Designing "the app" first means designing the shell — top nav or sidebar, container or full-width, where the logo goes — before the features exist that would decide it, because an app is, to the authors, a collection of features (p. 7). The example is a flight-booking service begun as five fields and a button (p. 8), and the closing remark is that the shell may never be needed: "it worked for Google" (p. 9).

**Detail comes later (pp. 10–12).** Typefaces, shadows and icons are deferred in the earliest stage; the authors cite Jason Fried of Basecamp designing on paper with a thick Sharpie because it makes fussing over details impossible (p. 10). "Hold the color": designing in grayscale forces spacing, contrast and size to carry the hierarchy, and color is added afterward (pp. 10–11). Sketches and wireframes are "disposable" and are left behind once a decision is made (p. 12).

**Don't design too much (pp. 13–16).** Every edge case is hard to imagine in the abstract — 2000 contacts, where the error goes, two events at once (p. 13) — so the authors work in short cycles: design a simple version, build it, fix what the working interface reveals, then design the next feature (pp. 14–15). "Be a pessimist": do not imply functionality you are not ready to build; the example is a comment system that never ships because attachments were drawn in on day one (pp. 15–16).

**Choose a personality (pp. 17–23).** Personality is set by a few concrete factors: a serif for an "elegant or classic" look, a rounded sans for playful, a neutral sans for plain (pp. 18–19); color by how it feels, with blue "safe and familiar", gold "expensive", pink "fun" (pp. 19–20); border radius, where small is neutral, large is playful and none is "serious or formal", and mixing them "almost always looks worse" (pp. 21–22); and language, with a less personal tone reading as official and a casual one as friendly (pp. 22–23). The way to decide is to look at the sites the intended audience already uses, without borrowing so much from direct competitors as to look "second-rate" (p. 23).

**Limit your choices (pp. 24–28).** Unconstrained decisions are "a paralyzing curse" — 12px or 13px, 10% or 15% shadow opacity — and three buttons in three nearly identical blues make the point (p. 24). Define systems in advance: 8–10 shades of a color, a restrictive type scale (p. 25). Then choose by elimination: guess a value, try its two neighbors, and the middle wins when both outer options look wrong (pp. 26–27). The list of things to systematize runs from font size to opacity (pp. 27–28), with the note that it need not all be defined ahead of time, only approached "with a system-focused mindset" (p. 28).

### Hierarchy is Everything (pp. 29–54)

**Not all elements are equal (pp. 30–31).** A brokerage dashboard is shown twice with the same colors, font and layout; de-emphasizing secondary and tertiary information makes the second "immediately more pleasing" (p. 31).

**Size isn't everything (pp. 32–35).** Font size alone produces primary content too large and secondary too small; weight and color do the same job with a "more reasonable" size (pp. 32–33). Two or three text colors — dark for primary, gray for secondary, lighter gray for tertiary — and two weights, 400 or 500 for most text and 600 or 700 for emphasis, are "usually enough" (p. 34). Weights under 400 are for large headings only; to de-emphasize, use a lighter color or smaller size, not a thinner weight (pp. 34–35).

**Don't use grey text on colored backgrounds (pp. 36–38).** What light gray achieves on white is reduced contrast, and on a colored background the same gray looks wrong (pp. 36–37); white at reduced opacity looks "washed out", even disabled, and lets a pattern show through (pp. 37–38). The authors hand-pick a color of the same hue with adjusted saturation and lightness, `hsl(183, 70%, 84%)` on a teal panel in the figure (p. 38). <!-- spelling:ignore: the book's own chapter title, quoted verbatim -->

**Emphasize by de-emphasizing (pp. 39–40).** When the active nav item will not stand out, soften the inactive items instead (p. 39); when a sidebar competes with the content, remove its background color (p. 40).

**Labels are a last resort (pp. 41–45).** A naive "label: value" card gives every fact equal weight (p. 41). Format often identifies data on its own — an email address, a phone number, a price — and context does the rest, as with a department under a name (pp. 41–42). Labels can be folded into values: "12 left in stock", "3 bedrooms" (pp. 42–43). Where a label is needed, as on a dashboard of similar figures, it is "supporting content", made smaller, lower-contrast or lighter (pp. 43–44). On information-dense pages such as a phone's specifications the reader is scanning for the label, so the label is the darker element and the value "slightly lighter" (pp. 44–45).

**Separate visual hierarchy from document hierarchy (pp. 46–47).** Semantic heading tags carry browser default sizes that suit articles and, the authors say, encourage bad decisions in application UIs; a "Manage Account" `h1` at 24px is shown against the same title at 16px (pp. 46–47). Section titles behave more like labels than headings, may be small, and in the extreme may be present in markup for accessibility and hidden visually (p. 47).

**Balance weight and contrast (pp. 48–51).** Bold reads as emphasized because it covers more surface area, about 90px² against 50px² in the figure (p. 48). Solid icons are "heavy" beside text, and since an icon's weight cannot be reduced, its contrast is: a softer color, `hsl(212, 20%, 68%)` against the text's `hsl(212, 20%, 13%)` (pp. 48–49). The reverse holds for a 1px border that is too subtle when light and too harsh when dark: keep the light color and make it 2px (pp. 50–51).

**Semantics are secondary (pp. 52–54).** A listing card with red Delete, blue Edit and green Publish buttons is the trap (p. 52). Every page has "one true primary action", a few secondary and a few tertiary; primary gets a solid high-contrast fill, secondary an outline or low-contrast fill, tertiary a link style (pp. 52–53). A destructive action that is not the page's primary action gets a secondary or tertiary treatment, and the "big, red, bold" styling belongs on the confirmation step where it is the primary action (p. 54).

### Layout and Spacing (pp. 55–86)

**Start with too much white space (pp. 56–59).** White space on the web is added until things stop looking "actively bad", which yields the minimum; the authors start with far too much and remove it (pp. 57–58). "Dense UIs have their place": a sports-stats dashboard is the example, and the instruction is that density be a deliberate decision rather than the default (p. 59).

**Establish a spacing and sizing system (pp. 60–64).** A rule that everything be a multiple of 4px does not decide between 120px and 125px; a useful scale respects the relative difference between neighbors, since 12px to 16px is a 33% jump while 500px to 520px is 4% (pp. 61–62). The authors keep any two values in a scale at least about 25% apart (p. 62). The example scale starts at 16px "because it divides nicely" and is the browser default, and runs 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 96, 128, 192, 256, 384, 512, 640, 768 (pp. 62–63). Designing in the browser makes sticking to the scale easier than dragging in a tool (p. 63).

**You don't have to fill the whole screen (pp. 65–71).** 960px was once the desktop width; a designer with 1200–1400px of canvas need not use it, and a design that needs 600px takes 600px (p. 65). Sections need not be full-width because the nav is (p. 66). "Shrink the canvas": for a responsive application, start at about 400px and design the mobile layout first, then widen and adjust whatever felt like a compromise (pp. 67–68). A narrow form that looks unbalanced in a wide page can split its supporting text into a second column rather than widen its inputs (pp. 68–70). "Don't force it" the other way either: a design that needs space takes it (p. 71).

**Grids are overrated (pp. 72–78).** A 12-column grid gives fluid percentage widths, and handing every layout decision to it does harm (pp. 72–73). A 25% sidebar grows on wide screens and collapses on narrow ones; a fixed sidebar width "optimized for its contents" with the main area flexing is the fix, and the same holds inside components, where a percentage is used only for something that is meant to scale (pp. 73–75). A login card at six columns then eight columns ends up wider on medium screens than large ones; give it a max-width and shrink it only when the screen is narrower than that (pp. 76–78).

**Relative sizing doesn't scale (pp. 79–82).** A 2.5em headline over 18px body is 45px; at 14px body on a small screen it is 35px, "way too big", where 20–24px is right, so the authors conclude there was no real relationship to encode (pp. 79–81). Large elements "need to shrink faster" than small ones (p. 81). The same applies within a component: button padding defined in em scales the whole button like a zoom, whereas padding that grows more than proportionally at large sizes and shrinks more at small ones makes a large button "feel like a larger button" (pp. 81–82).

**Avoid ambiguous spacing (pp. 83–86).** Without a visible separator, a label 20px below the previous input and 20px above its own does not read as connected; 20px between groups and 10px within them does (pp. 83–84). The same fault appears above article headings, in bulleted lists whose gaps equal their line height, and in horizontal action rows (pp. 85–86). The rule is more space around a group than within it (p. 86).

### Designing Text (pp. 87–117)

**Establish a type scale (pp. 88–93).** Most interfaces use "way too many font sizes"; a product page is shown using 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 17, 18 and 26px (p. 88). A modular scale by ratio — 4:5, 2:3, the golden ratio — yields fractional pixels that browsers round differently and "too limiting" jumps for interface work; a rounded 3:4 scale gives 12, 16, 21, 28 and leaves a designer wanting sizes between them (pp. 89–90). The authors pick values by hand: 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36, 48, 60, 72px (p. 91). Em units are ruled out for the scale because nested ems compute to values off the scale — 0.875em inside 1.25em is 17.5px — so the scale is written in px or rem (pp. 92–93).

**Use good fonts (pp. 94–98).** The safe choice for UI is a neutral sans-serif "like Helvetica", or the system stack `-apple-system, Segoe UI, Roboto, Noto Sans, Ubuntu, Cantarell, Helvetica Neue` (p. 94). Families with fewer than five weights are skipped as a rule of thumb, "not always true"; filtering Google Fonts to 10+ styles cuts out 85% of the directory and leaves under 50 sans-serifs (pp. 95–96). Headline faces have tighter spacing and shorter x-heights, so condensed, short-x-height faces are avoided for body UI text; Futura PT and Proxima Nova are the figure (p. 96). Popularity sorting is "the wisdom of the crowd", and inspecting well-designed sites finds faces the safe routes miss (pp. 96–98).

**Keep your line length in check (pp. 99–101).** 45–75 characters per line, reached with a width of 20–35em; wider "can sometimes work" but is "risky territory" (pp. 99–100). Paragraph width is capped even when the surrounding content area is wide, `max-width: 34em` in the figure (pp. 100–101).

**Baseline, not center (pp. 102–104).** Mixed sizes on one line, a card title beside small actions, look wrong vertically centered once the text is close together; aligning by baseline uses a reference the eye already perceives, `align-items: baseline` in the figure (pp. 102–104).

**Line-height is proportional (pp. 105–108).** 1.5 is a starting point, not a constant. Line height and line length rise together — 1.5 for narrow text, "as tall as 2" for wide (pp. 105–106) — and line height and font size move inversely: 1.75 against 1.25 for small text, and a line height of 1 is called fine for a large headline (pp. 107–108).

**Not every link needs a color (pp. 109–110).** The authors want a link inside prose to look clickable; in an interface where "almost everything is a link", the same treatment is overbearing, so most links get only weight or a darker color, and ancillary links show an underline or color "only on hover" (pp. 109–110).

**Align with readability in mind (pp. 111–114).** Text is aligned with the direction of its language, left for English (p. 111). Centering suits headlines and short blocks; anything over two or three lines is left-aligned, and a block too long to center is shortened (pp. 111–112). Numbers in a table are right-aligned so the decimals line up (p. 113). Justified text needs `hyphens: auto` or it gaps, and even then the choice against left alignment is called a matter of preference (pp. 113–114).

**Use letter-spacing effectively (pp. 115–117).** The default is to "trust the typeface designer" (p. 115). Two exceptions: tighten a body-optimized family such as Open Sans when it is used for headlines, `-0.05em` in the figure, but never the reverse, since headline faces seldom work small (p. 116); and loosen all-caps text, `0.05em`, because uniform-height letters have fewer distinguishing shapes (p. 117).

### Working with Color (pp. 118–148)

**Ditch hex for HSL (pp. 119–122).** Colors that look alike look nothing alike as hex; HSL names hue in degrees (0° red, 120° green, 240° blue), saturation as 0% gray to 100% vivid, and lightness as 0% black to 100% white with a pure hue at 50% (pp. 119–121). HSL is not HSB: 100% brightness in HSB is white only at 0% saturation (p. 121). Because, in the authors' words, browsers "only understand HSL", it is their "weapon of choice" for the web (p. 122).

**You need more colors than you think (pp. 123–128).** Five-swatch palette generators produce a garish chat app (p. 123). A real palette has three groups: grays, which are almost everything in an interface, 8–10 shades starting from a dark gray because true black looks "unnatural" (pp. 124–125); one or two primary colors with 5–10 shades, ultra-light ones for tinted alert backgrounds and dark ones for text (p. 126); and accent colors for new-feature highlights and semantic states — red for destructive confirmation, yellow for warning, green for a positive trend — plus more for categorizing chart lines, calendar events or tags, so a complex UI may need as many as ten colors at 5–10 shades each (pp. 126–128).

**Define your shades up front (pp. 129–132).** Preprocessor `lighten` and `darken` produce "35 slightly different blues"; the fix is a fixed set (p. 129). Pick the base as the shade that would work as a button background (p. 130), the darkest as text and the lightest as a tinted background — an alert component exercises both (pp. 130–131) — then fill the gaps: nine steps named 900 to 100, with 700 and 300 as compromises between their neighbors, then 800, 600, 400 and 200 (p. 131). Grays follow the same process from the edges (p. 132). The section ends with "It's not a science" and a warning against adding new shades once the system exists (p. 132).

**Don't let lightness kill your saturation (pp. 133–138).** In HSL the same saturation reads paler near 0% and 100% lightness, so saturation is raised as lightness leaves 50% (pp. 133–134). Yellow and blue at identical HSL lightness differ in perceived brightness; the authors give the formula √(0.299r² + 0.587g² + 0.114b²)/255 and plot it around the wheel, with minima at red, green and blue and maxima at yellow, cyan and magenta (pp. 134–135). Brightness can therefore be changed by rotating hue toward 60°, 180° or 300° to lighten and 0°, 120° or 240° to darken, which keeps yellow's dark shades "warm and rich" rather than brown (pp. 136–137), within a limit of 20–30° of rotation (p. 138).

**Greys don't have to be grey (pp. 139–141).** True gray is 0% saturation, but most useful grays are saturated: blue for cool, yellow or orange for warm, and the saturation again rises at the light and dark ends to hold the temperature (pp. 139–141). How much is "completely up to you" (p. 141). <!-- spelling:ignore: the book's own chapter title, quoted verbatim -->

**Accessible doesn't have to mean ugly (pp. 142–145).** WCAG asks 4.5:1 for normal text under about 18px and 3:1 for larger, shown as a table of gray values with their grades (p. 142). White text on colored badges needs a dark fill to pass, and dark fills steal attention; the authors "flip the contrast" to dark colored text on a light tint of the same hue, which passes at AAA in the figure (pp. 143–144). Colored secondary text on a colored panel is harder; rotating its hue toward a brighter hue — cyan, magenta, yellow — raises contrast without approaching white (pp. 144–145).

**Don't rely on color alone (pp. 146–148).** Red-green colorblind readers cannot tell a rising metric from a falling one by color; icons are added (pp. 146–147). Chart series distinguished by hue fail under blue-yellow color blindness; distinguishing them by lightness of one hue works because "light and dark" survives (pp. 147–148). Color, the chapter ends, supports what a design already says and is never the only channel (p. 148).

### Creating Depth (pp. 149–172)

**Emulate a light source (pp. 150–157).** Raised and inset elements follow one rule, "light comes from above", shown on a door's raised panels and a cabinet's inset ones (pp. 151–152). A raised button gets a lighter top edge by inset shadow, hand-picked rather than semi-transparent white because white "can suck the saturation out", and a small sharp dark shadow below, `0 1px 3px` (pp. 153–155). An inset well gets a lighter bottom edge and a dark inset shadow at the top; text inputs and checkboxes take the same treatment (pp. 155–156). "Don't get carried away": photo-realism makes interfaces "busy and unclear" (p. 157).

**Use shadows to convey elevation (pp. 158–162).** Small tight shadows sit near the surface, large blurred ones near the reader, and whatever feels closer draws more focus (p. 158). Buttons get small shadows, dropdowns medium, modals large (pp. 159–160). An elevation system of about five shadows, from smallest to largest with linear steps, is enough (pp. 160–161). Shadows also signal interaction: an item lifts when grabbed to drag, and a button loses shadow when pressed (pp. 161–162).

**Shadows can have two parts (pp. 163–166).** Polished shadows are two: a larger softer one cast by direct light and a tighter darker one from the ambient-light gap under an object (pp. 163–165). As elevation rises the tight shadow fades, until at the highest elevation it is nearly or entirely gone (pp. 165–166).

**Even flat designs can have depth (pp. 167–169).** Without shadows, lighter surfaces read as closer and darker as farther, so an element lighter than its background feels raised and one darker feels inset (p. 168); solid, unblurred, vertically offset shadows keep a flat look while lifting a card (p. 169).

**Overlap elements to create layers (pp. 170–172).** A card straddling two backgrounds, an element taller than its parent, carousel controls hanging over an edge (pp. 170–171); overlapping images get an "invisible border" in the background color so they never touch (p. 172).

### Working with Images (pp. 173–190)

**Use good photos (pp. 174–175).** Bad photos "will ruin a design"; hire a photographer or buy good stock, with Unsplash named as free (pp. 174–175). Placeholder images that will be swapped for phone photos later "never works" (p. 175).

**Text needs consistent contrast (pp. 176–180).** A headline fails on a photo because the photo, not the text, is the problem; the fixes are a semi-transparent overlay, `hsla(0, 0%, 0%, .55)`, lowering the image's contrast with brightness compensated, colorizing it with a single hue by multiply, or a large-blur no-offset text shadow that reads as a glow (pp. 176–180).

**Everything has an intended size (pp. 181–186).** Icons drawn at 16–24px look "chunky" at three or four times that size; a small icon is enclosed in a colored shape instead (pp. 181–182). A full screenshot shrunk 70% turns 16px text into 4px; take it at a smaller layout, crop to a region, or draw a simplified version (pp. 183–185). Shrinking a 128px logo to a 16px favicon "all turns to mush", so the favicon is redrawn simplified at target size (pp. 185–186).

**Beware user-uploaded content (pp. 187–190).** Uploaded images are shown in fixed containers cropped with `background-size: cover` rather than at intrinsic aspect ratio (pp. 187–188), and an inner box shadow, or a semi-transparent inner border, stops a light photo bleeding into a light background where a solid border would clash (pp. 189–190).

### Finishing Touches (pp. 191–214)

**Supercharge the defaults (pp. 192–194).** Bullets become icons, checkmarks generically or a padlock for security features; a testimonial's quotation marks become a visual element; links get weight, color or a thick overlapping underline; checkboxes and radios take a brand color in their selected state (pp. 192–194).

**Add color with accent borders (pp. 195–197).** A colored rectangle — across a card top, under an active nav item, down the side of an alert, under a headline, across the whole layout — takes no graphic design talent and makes a design feel "designed" (pp. 195–197).

**Decorate your backgrounds (pp. 198–202).** A changed background color on a panel or page section, a gradient of two hues within about 30°, a subtle repeating pattern from Hero Patterns or one along a single edge, a geometric shape or a simplified world map, all kept at low contrast (pp. 198–202).

**Don't overlook empty states (pp. 203–205).** A feature designed with rich sample data ships as "No contacts found." (pp. 203–204). For anything dependent on user content the empty state is "a priority, not an afterthought"; it gets an illustration and an emphasized call to action, and supporting UI such as tabs and filters is hidden until content exists (pp. 204–205).

**Use fewer borders (pp. 206–209).** Borders separate, but too many feel "busy and cluttered"; the alternatives are a box shadow, which works when the element differs in color from its background, two background colors, or extra spacing (pp. 206–209).

**Think outside the box (pp. 210–214).** A dropdown can be sectioned, multi-column, with icons and supporting text; a table can merge columns that need not sort independently and carry images and color; a set of radio buttons that matters can become selectable cards (pp. 210–214).

### Leveling Up (pp. 215–218)

Two habits: ask of any admired design what its designer did that the reader would never have thought to do — an inverted date picker, a button inside an input, a two-color headline (pp. 216–217) — and rebuild favorite interfaces from scratch without the developer tools, which surfaces tricks such as tighter heading line height, uppercase letter-spacing and combined shadows (pp. 217–218).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the authors limit their own claim, admit a counter-case, or say a rule does not apply.

- **Systems.** Not everything has to be defined ahead of time; a "system-focused mindset" and adding systems as decisions arise is enough (Starting from Scratch, p. 28). Once a palette exists, "little tweaks" are expected, and the only firm line is against adding new shades often (Working with Color, p. 132).
- **Density.** Airy interfaces "almost always feel cleaner", yet "Dense UIs have their place", the example being a dashboard showing a great deal at once; the condition is that density be deliberate (Layout and Spacing, p. 59). A design that needs a lot of space takes it, and nobody is obliged to fill space they do not need (Layout and Spacing, p. 71).
- **Labels.** The chapter that calls labels a last resort concedes that a label is sometimes really needed, for scannable similar data on a dashboard (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 43), and reverses the emphasis entirely on information-dense pages where the reader hunts for the label, with the data kept dark enough because it is "still important information" (pp. 44–45). The whole chapter is fenced off from forms in its first line (p. 41).
- **Weights.** Two weights are "usually enough" and weights under 400 "can work for large headings" (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 34). Fewer than five weights as a quality signal "isn't always true" (Designing Text, p. 95).
- **Grids and relative units.** Grids "can be useful" and bring a "sense of order"; the objection is to outsourcing "all" layout decisions to one (Layout and Spacing, p. 72). Relative units such as em are called "nothing inherently wrong"; the objection is to believing the relationship stays fixed across screen sizes (Layout and Spacing, p. 79). Em is ruled out for a type scale (Designing Text, p. 92) and recommended for a paragraph measure two chapters later (Designing Text, p. 99).
- **Line length.** Wider than 75 characters "can sometimes work", with 45–75 as the safe range (Designing Text, p. 100).
- **Line height.** 1.5 "is not necessarily untrue" as a starting point; the rule is that it varies with width and size (Designing Text, p. 105).
- **Alignment.** Center alignment "can look great" for headlines and short, independent blocks (Designing Text, p. 111). Justified text can work on the web for a formal look and, hyphenated, the choice is called "a matter of preference" (Designing Text, pp. 113–114).
- **Links.** The de-emphasis advice is for interfaces where "almost everything is a link"; a link in a block of prose is still made to "stand out and look clickable" (Designing Text, p. 109).
- **Letter-spacing.** The default is to leave it alone; only headline tightening and all-caps loosening are exceptions, and tightening never rescues a headline face at small sizes (Designing Text, pp. 115–117).
- **Fonts.** The system stack is admitted to be "not the most ambitious" choice and is offered for a reader who does not trust their taste (Designing Text, pp. 94–95).
- **Color.** Color psychology "isn't super practical" on its own, and much of the choice comes down to what looks good to the designer (Starting from Scratch, p. 20). Picking a base shade has "no real rules", not even a starting lightness (Working with Color, p. 130). Hue rotation for brightness "works best in small doses" and stops at 20–30° (Working with Color, p. 138). How far to tint grays is "completely up to you" (Working with Color, p. 141).
- **Depth.** Simulating light can produce interfaces that are "busy and unclear" if pushed toward photo-realism (Creating Depth, p. 157). Flat design, defined as designing without shadows or gradients, is treated as a legitimate style that conveys depth by color and solid shadows instead (Creating Depth, pp. 167–169). Five shadows are "usually plenty" (Creating Depth, p. 160).
- **Borders.** A box shadow as a border works best when the element is not the same color as its background (Finishing Touches, p. 207). A heavier border is the answer when a soft one is too subtle and a dark one too harsh (Hierarchy is Everything, pp. 50–51).
- **Semantic states.** Accent colors for semantic states are to be used "pretty sparingly" (Working with Color, p. 128). A destructive action is not automatically "big, red, and bold" (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 54).
- **Decoration.** Background patterns and shapes are kept at low contrast so they do not interfere with content (Finishing Touches, pp. 200–202). Gradients are limited to two hues within about 30° (Finishing Touches, p. 199).
- **Personality.** Borrowing from the audience's other sites is encouraged; borrowing "too much from direct competitors" is not (Starting from Scratch, p. 23).
- **Constraints.** The last advice chapter ends by calling constraints "powerful" and a little freedom sometimes necessary (Finishing Touches, p. 214).
- **Scope.** The authors say they crammed in every good idea they could think of and that more always remains to learn (Leveling Up, p. 216). The book cites no study and names WCAG as its only outside standard (Working with Color, p. 142).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool or claim the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source was reached in this pass that confirms or refutes it; the reason is given each time.

- **"Browsers only understand HSL" (Working with Color, p. 122).** True in December 2018 and no longer. `oklch()`, `oklab()`, `lab()` and `lch()` reached Baseline in 2023 — Safari 15.4 (March 2022), Chrome and Edge 111 (March 2023), Firefox 113 (May 2023) — along with `color-mix()` and relative color syntax (web.dev, "Baseline 2023"; MDN, `oklch()`; caniuse, "mdn-css_types_color_oklch"). The authors' own Tailwind CSS v4 (January 2025) rebuilt its default palette in OKLCH and P3 rather than hex, moving the vivid ends of every scale (tailwindcss.com, "Tailwind CSS v4.0"). The chapter's mechanism — hue, saturation, lightness as separate axes — is what OKLCH offers with a perceptual lightness axis in place of HSL's, which is the very gap the chapter "Don't let lightness kill your saturation" (pp. 133–138) works around by hand.
- **Preprocessor `lighten` and `darken` as the road to "35 slightly different blues" (Working with Color, p. 129).** The functions the book warns against are being removed: Dart Sass 1.79.0 (September 2024) deprecated the global `lighten()`, `darken()`, `saturate()`, `desaturate()` and `adjust-hue()` in favor of `color.adjust()` and `color.scale()`, with removal scheduled for Dart Sass 3.0 (sass-lang.com, "Breaking Change: Color Functions"). CSS itself now does the same job without a preprocessor through `color-mix()` and relative color syntax (MDN, `oklch()`), so the on-the-fly shade the book argues against is easier to produce, not harder.
- **WCAG's 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large (Working with Color, p. 142).** Holds. WCAG 2.2 (October 2023) keeps both floors at level AA, and 2.x AA remains what laws and procurement cite. WCAG 3 is still a Working Draft; APCA, the perceptual contrast method once expected to replace the ratio, was removed from the WCAG 3 drafts in 2023 and the draft states the algorithm is yet to be determined (Adrian Roselli, "WCAG3 Contrast as of April 2026"; Eric Eggert, "WCAG 3 is not ready yet").
- **The perceived-brightness formula √(0.299r² + 0.587g² + 0.114b²) (Working with Color, p. 134).** The book gives it no source. It is the "P" of Darel Rex Finley's HSP color model, published at alienryderflex.com/hsp.html, where the weights are described as reverse-engineered from Photoshop's RGB-to-grayscale conversion; the chroma.js library adopted it as `color.brightness()` (github.com/gka/chroma.js, pull request 41). The formula still stands as Finley published it.
- **The Google Fonts "10+ styles" filter cutting out 85% of the directory and leaving under 50 sans-serifs (Designing Text, p. 96).** Unchecked: the filter is inside the live Google Fonts application and could not be re-run from here, and no source for the 2018 directory size was reached in this pass. What is known is that the directory has grown to about 1,900 families in 2026, 705 of them sans-serif by one count (serbyte.net, "Google Fonts Directory", 2026; photutorial.com, "Fonts statistics"), so whatever the filter leaves today, it is not the 2018 number.
- **The system font stack `-apple-system, Segoe UI, Roboto, Noto Sans, Ubuntu, Cantarell, Helvetica Neue` (Designing Text, p. 94).** Still in circulation nearly verbatim; the maintained "Modern Font Stacks" list gives `-apple-system, BlinkMacSystemFont, "Segoe UI", Roboto, Oxygen-Sans, Ubuntu, Cantarell, "Helvetica Neue", sans-serif` and notes that the CSS keyword `system-ui` now names the platform UI font directly on every modern operating system, with `-apple-system` kept for older Apple devices (github.com/system-fonts/modern-font-stacks; modernfontstacks.com). The book's stack omits `system-ui` and a generic `sans-serif` fallback.
- **16px as the default font size in every major browser (Layout and Spacing, p. 62; Designing Text, p. 89).** Holds; nothing found suggests any major browser changed its default.
- **Unsplash as free photography (Working with Images, p. 175).** Still free to use commercially, but under Unsplash's own license rather than CC0 — that change was June 2017, before the book — and since Getty Images bought Unsplash in March 2021 the site also sells a paid tier, Unsplash+, with indemnified files (Wikipedia, "Unsplash"; picdefense.io, "Unsplash License for Commercial Use").
- **Hero Patterns as the source of a repeating background (Finishing Touches, p. 200).** Live at heropatterns.com, free, more than 100 SVG patterns, maintained by Steve Schoger, one of the book's two authors (heropatterns.com; github.com/sschoger/hero-patterns). The book does not say the site is the author's own.
- **`hyphens: auto` for justified text (Designing Text, p. 114).** Now unprefixed in Chrome from version 88 (January 2021) on every platform, after years of `-webkit-hyphens` only; it still needs a `lang` attribute and a dictionary for that language, or it does nothing (caniuse, "CSS Hyphenation"; chromestatus.com, "CSS Automatic Hyphenation").
- **Favicons redrawn at 16px because a shrunk logo turns to mush (Working with Images, pp. 185–186).** Partly overtaken. SVG favicons are supported in Chrome 80+, Firefox 41+, Edge 80+ and Safari 26 on macOS; the sources reached disagree on iOS Safari, one listing Safari 26 there too and one, dated late 2024, saying it renders none, so a raster fallback is still in use (caniuse, "SVG favicons"; testmuai.com, "SVG Favicon: Browser Support"). The advice to simplify a mark for the tiny size is not affected by the format.
- **`background-size: cover` for cropping user images (Working with Images, p. 188).** The technique still works. Whether the `<img>`-element equivalent, `object-fit`, has changed the usual practice since 2018 is unchecked: no source was reached in this pass.
- **Shadows as the elevation language, with buttons, dropdowns and modals at rising heights (Creating Depth, pp. 158–166).** Unchecked against later design-system practice: a fetch of Material Design 3's elevation page returned only its title, and no other source was reached in this pass.
- **960px as the former desktop layout width and 1200–1400px canvases (Layout and Spacing, p. 65).** Unchecked; a historical framing with no figure that later evidence would confirm or refute.
- **Weights under 400 as too thin for UI text (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 34).** Unchecked against the spread of variable fonts since 2018: no source was reached in this pass.
- **"It worked for Google" (Starting from Scratch, p. 9).** Unchecked; the claim is that a search box without a shell succeeded, and no later evidence bears on it.
- **The book itself.** Still sold at refactoringui.com as a 218-page PDF, at $99 for the book and videos or $149 with a component gallery, palettes, a font showcase and icons, claiming "over 30,000 copies"; the page names no version or date (refactoringui.com, fetched 2026-09-10). The 218 pages match this file's PDF, and no revision after v1.0.2 (2019) was found.
- **Not checked, because nothing outside the book bears on them:** the constructed before-and-after figures, every one drawn by the authors; the 25% minimum step in a spacing scale (Layout and Spacing, p. 62); the 20–30° hue-rotation ceiling (Working with Color, p. 138); five shadows as enough (Creating Depth, p. 160); and the 45–75 character range (Designing Text, p. 99), which the book gives without a source.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Every claim rests on the authors' eye.** The evidence for each rule is a pair of figures the authors drew, one marked with a red cross and one with a green check, and the reader is asked to see that the second "is immediately more pleasing" (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 31). No user was watched, no study is cited, and the book says as much of its color method, "It's not a science" (Working with Color, p. 132). The one outside standard named anywhere is WCAG (Working with Color, p. 142).
- **Numbers without a source.** The 25% minimum gap between adjacent scale values (Layout and Spacing, p. 62), the 45–75 character line (Designing Text, p. 99), the 20–35em width that reaches it (p. 99), the 20–30° hue-rotation ceiling (Working with Color, p. 138), the "5-10" shades per color (p. 126), the "8-10" grays (p. 125), five shadows (Creating Depth, p. 160), and the ~18px boundary for large text (Working with Color, p. 142) are all stated as fact and none carries a citation. The perceived-brightness formula (p. 134) is another author's, uncredited; section 7 names him.
- **Accessibility is fenced off, then leaned on.** The labels chapter opens by dismissing "the accessibility pitchfork" (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 41) and goes on to advise showing data with no visible label at all (pp. 41–42) and hiding section titles visually (p. 47), with no word on how a screen reader or a magnifier user meets either. The selectable-card radios (Finishing Touches, p. 214) and the sectioned dropdown (p. 211) carry no note on keyboard or assistive use. Two chapters later the book adopts WCAG's contrast ratios as its only external rule (Working with Color, p. 142) and adds a chapter on color blindness (pp. 146–148); the book's own scope statement and its practice do not agree.
- **Internal tension on units.** Em is ruled out for a type scale because nested values fall off it (Designing Text, pp. 92–93), recommended for a paragraph measure because it tracks the font size (p. 99), and called "nothing inherently wrong" as a class (Layout and Spacing, p. 79). The reader is left to work out that the objection was to inheritance, not to the unit.
- **Internal tension on labels.** "Labels are a last resort" (Hierarchy is Everything, p. 41) is followed within three pages by a case where the label is the emphasized element (pp. 44–45); the rule survives only as a matter of what the reader "scans for", which the chapter title does not say.
- **The examples are marketing pages more often than applications.** The book opens by telling a developer to design a feature before the shell (Starting from Scratch, p. 7), but the figures that follow are landing-page heroes (pp. 17–22), pricing cards (Finishing Touches, pp. 195, 198), testimonials (p. 193), a "features" section (Working with Images, pp. 181–182) and newsletter sign-ups (Finishing Touches, p. 202; Leveling Up, p. 217). The chapters on photography, overlays, colorized images, background decoration and accent borders (Working with Images, pp. 174–180; Finishing Touches, pp. 195–202) have no application-interface example at all.
- **Some advice is a period piece.** Designing the mobile layout first at "a ~400px canvas" (Layout and Spacing, p. 67), 960px as the remembered desktop width (p. 65), shadows as the elevation vocabulary (Creating Depth, pp. 158–166), HSL as the only browser color model (Working with Color, p. 122) and preprocessor color functions (p. 129) describe the web of 2018; section 7 has what moved.
- **The authors' own products appear without disclosure.** Hero Patterns (Finishing Touches, p. 200) is Steve Schoger's site and is introduced as a third-party resource, with a link and nothing else. The book nowhere mentions Tailwind CSS, the framework refactoringui.com credits the same two authors with creating (section 7), so a reader cannot tell from the book whether the worked scales — spacing (Layout and Spacing, p. 63), 100–900 shade names (Working with Color, p. 131), type sizes (Designing Text, p. 91), five shadows (Creating Depth, p. 161) — are the book's advice or a product's defaults.
- **The book's strongest chapter proves the least.** "Not all elements are equal" (Hierarchy is Everything, pp. 30–31) is the thesis in two figures — the same dashboard, same colors, same font, same layout, hierarchy the only change — and it is the only chapter whose comparison holds everything else constant. Most later pairs change several things at once (the heart-rate card on p. 44 changes size, weight, color and case together), so a reader cannot recover which change did the work.
- **"Don't rely on color alone" stops at icons and lightness.** The chapter (Working with Color, pp. 146–148) treats red-green and blue-yellow color blindness and offers an icon or a lightness ramp; it says nothing about text labels, patterns, or what a monochrome print or a high-contrast mode does to a lightness ramp, and the rest of the book's status badges (pp. 143–144) are colored pills whose only non-color signal is their text.
- **No date, no edition history, no errata.** The PDF prints no copyright page, no version note beyond the file name and no changelog, so a reader holding v1.0.2 cannot tell what changed from the December 2018 release or whether any claim was corrected.
