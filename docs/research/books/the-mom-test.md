## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that customer conversations are "bad by default" and that the fix is in the questions rather than in the person answering them: a founder who asks about the other person's life instead of about the idea, asks for specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future, and talks less than they listen, gets facts that even a mother cannot distort (ch. 1, pp. 8, 13). The three rules are collectively the Mom Test, named for the test case of an interviewee with every reason to be kind (ch. 1, p. 13). The measure of a useful early conversation is whether it produced concrete facts about the customer's life and world view, and the demonstration is a pair of annotated dialogues about the same idea in which the second yields two candidate customer segments and a named channel risk while the first yields a compliment (ch. 1, pp. 9–12). Later chapters add the machinery around the rules: three kinds of bad data with a repair for each (ch. 2, p. 24), a test for whether a question is important at all (ch. 3, p. 41), a casual format in place of the scheduled meeting (ch. 4, p. 57), commitment and advancement as the way to cut through compliments once a product exists (ch. 5, pp. 63–64), routes to finding people to talk to (ch. 6, p. 76), customer slicing to a who-where pair (ch. 7, p. 97), and a prep-notes-review process so the learning does not stay in one founder's head (ch. 8, p. 100).

The author is explicit about what the claim is not. It is not a treatment of Customer Development, which he calls a bigger concept and assigns to Steve Blank's *4 Steps to the E.piphany* and *The Startup Owner's Manual*, keeping the lowercase term "customer conversation" for his own subject (Introduction, p. 7). It is not an argument that talking to customers is worth doing, since he assumes the reader already agrees and describes the book as more how than why (Introduction, p. 7). It is not a theoretical treatment but a practical handbook, concerned with finding people and taking notes (Introduction, p. 6). It is not a promise of a large business: he says he cannot teach the reader to make a business huge (Introduction, p. 6). It is not written from the position of someone who was already good at meetings; the author describes himself as a techie rather than a salesperson, introverted and naturally bad in meetings, who fumbled through from scratch (Introduction, p. 6). It is not a method of building by committee, and the chapter that could be read that way answers the objection directly by dividing the ground, leaving the problem to the customer and the solution to the founder (ch. 1, p. 23). And it is not an obligation: the book states that talking to customers is a tool, and that a founder who will not be helped by it should skip it (ch. 8, p. 108).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction and front matter (pp. 3–7)

The opening image is an archaeological dig: the truth about a business is buried and fragile, a blunt question smashes it, and a timid one never reaches it (Introduction, p. 3). The reader is named by situation rather than by role: someone who has read about Customer Development or Lean Startup and does not know how to start; a salesperson inside a young company with no business model yet; a mentor or investor; someone in love with an idea who has not quit a job yet; a founder whose investors want evidence of a real problem (Introduction, p. 4). The problem the book claims to fix is that the advice to talk to customers is well intentioned and unhelpful, because bad conversations are not merely useless, they produce a false positive that makes a founder over-invest cash and time (Introduction, p. 5). The evidence offered is the author's own failure at his first company, Habit, a social advertising business where he says he spent months talking to customers full time and learned only afterward that he had been doing it wrong (Introduction, p. 5). Three reasons are given for the book existing at all: the author is a techie rather than a salesperson and had to work out the unspoken rules of a meeting from scratch, though he says he closed deals with Sony and MTV; understanding how it goes wrong has to come before doing it right; and the book is a practical handbook rather than a theoretical one, covering how to find people and how to take notes (Introduction, pp. 5–6). The scope is drawn tightly: this is not a summary or re-interpretation of Customer Development, which the author assigns to Steve Blank's *4 Steps to the E.piphany* and *The Startup Owner's Manual*, and the book uses lowercase "customer conversation" to keep the distinction visible (Introduction, p. 7).

### Chapter One, The Mom Test (pp. 8–23)

The chapter opens by widening the folk advice: it is true that you should not ask your mother whether your business is a good idea, but the reason is not your mother, it is the question, which invites everyone to lie at least a little (ch. 1, p. 8). Finding the truth is the founder's responsibility rather than the interviewee's, and the tool is a better question (ch. 1, p. 8). The evidence is a pair of dialogues about the same idea, a cookbook app for the iPad, annotated line by line with what each speaker is actually thinking. In the failing version the son announces he has an idea, leads with a yes-or-no question about the iPad, supplies the price himself, piles on features, and reads his mother's closing politeness as validation (ch. 1, p. 9). The author's reading is that the botched version costs more than no conversation at all, because a fistful of false positives removes the caution that ignorance supplies (ch. 1, p. 10). In the passing version the son asks how the iPad is treating her, what the last thing she did on it was, where she hears about apps, where the cookbooks on her shelf came from, and what the last cookbook she bought for herself was; the answers produce a marketing channel constraint, a gift market, and two candidate customer segments (ch. 1, pp. 10–11).

The measure the chapter proposes is whether a conversation produced concrete facts about the customer's life and world view (ch. 1, p. 12). One conversation is said to have moved a generic premise, old people like cookbooks and iPads, into two specific segments each needing a different product, plus named risks to address before committing (ch. 1, pp. 12–13). The stated reason it worked is that the idea was never mentioned, and the general claim is that the mistake is almost always to mention the idea too soon rather than too late (ch. 1, p. 13). The three rules are then given: talk about their life instead of your idea, ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future, and talk less and listen more (ch. 1, p. 13). The chapter's rule of thumb is that conversations with customers go wrong unless the founder does the work of fixing them (ch. 1, pp. 13–14).

The second half is a graded set of fourteen questions, listed together first as an exercise (ch. 1, p. 15) and then discussed. Asking whether the idea is good is called awful, because only the market can answer it and everything else is opinion; the repair offered is to ask a construction company to show how it manages suppliers today, what it loves and hates, what it tried before, whether it is actively searching, and where money is being lost, and then to decide for yourself (ch. 1, p. 16). Asking whether someone would buy a product that did X is bad because the answer is almost always yes (ch. 1, p. 16). Asking how much they would pay for X is called exactly as bad, and more dangerous because a number feels rigorous (ch. 1, p. 16). Adding a number to the hypothetical, would you pay X for a product that did Y, is said not to help, and the repair is to ask what they do now, or, if far enough along, to ask for a deposit or a pre-order (ch. 1, pp. 19–20). Asking what their dream product would do is rated only sort-of-okay, valuable for the motivations and constraints behind the requests rather than the requests themselves, since people know their problems but not the solutions (ch. 1, p. 17).

The good questions are those that reach for a past fact or a motive. Asking why they bother moves from the perceived problem to the real one; the example is a group of finance staff who asked for better messaging tools and turned out to want certainty that everyone was on the latest version, so the answer resembled Dropbox rather than a chat tool (ch. 1, p. 17). Asking what the implications are separates a problem someone will pay to solve from one they will tolerate, and doubles as a pricing signal; the counter-example is an interviewee who described a workflow in capital letters and then admitted that throwing interns at it was working fine (ch. 1, pp. 17–18). Asking to be talked through the last time it happened is preferred because actions beat opinions, watching beats being told, and a full walkthrough answers several questions at once, including which tools a product would have to integrate with (ch. 1, p. 18). Asking what else they have tried tests a future promise against a past search; the example is a man who said he would definitely pay and, pressed, had never once searched for a solution that in fact existed (ch. 1, p. 19). Asking how they deal with it now yields a price anchor, and the two anchors in the example differ by a hundred times, a hundred pounds a month for a workaround against a hundred and twenty thousand pounds a year of agency fees, which the author frames as a choice about what to present yourself as replacing (ch. 1, p. 20). Asking where the money comes from is called a must-ask in business-to-business work, because the person in the room is often not the budget owner and the purchasing process eventually becomes a repeatable sales roadmap (ch. 1, p. 20). Asking who else to talk to is to end every conversation, and a refusal is itself data, either about the meeting or about how little they care (ch. 1, p. 22). Asking whether there is anything else you should have asked is called a crutch, useful while you do not know the industry and discarded later (ch. 1, p. 22).

The chapter closes by noting that none of the good questions asks what to build, answering a standing objection that talking to customers means building by committee: the questions are about the customer's problems, cares, constraints, and goals, the founder takes the visionary leap to a solution, and confirmation comes later through commitment and advancement (ch. 1, p. 23). The division is stated as a trade, in which the problem belongs to them and the solution to the founder (ch. 1, p. 23).

### Chapter Two, Avoiding bad data (pp. 24–40)

Bad data is sorted into three kinds, compliments, fluff, and ideas, with the corresponding repairs of deflecting, anchoring, and digging (ch. 2, p. 24). The danger is asymmetric: false negatives kill a live idea, and false positives, which the author calls more dangerous, confirm a dead one (ch. 2, p. 24).

On compliments, the argument is that they are almost certainly untrue, not necessarily on purpose, and that even a true one carries no information, because professional judges of the future are wrong more often than right (ch. 2, p. 25). The stated exception is an industry expert who has built a very similar business (ch. 2, p. 25). Three annotated conversations follow: one in which the compliment is taken at face value and the team asks six months later why there are no customers; one in which the founder apologizes for slipping into pitch mode and recovers workflow data, discovering that two full-time staff are already spent on the problem; and one with a clean negative result, where the interviewee says the problem is not a big deal and the author calls that a conversation to be happy with (ch. 2, pp. 25–27). The symptoms are given both in the meeting and back at the office, the office ones being the reports that a meeting went well or that everybody loves the idea (ch. 2, p. 28). The rule of thumb calls compliments the fool's gold of customer learning (ch. 2, p. 28).

Fluff is sorted into generic claims, future-tense promises, and hypothetical maybes (ch. 2, pp. 28–29). The deadliest is the promise to definitely buy, and the evidence offered is the author's first employer, which he says mistook such promises for commitment and lost about ten million dollars (ch. 2, p. 29). A list of fluff-inducing question openings follows, with the qualification that the mistake is valuing the answers rather than asking the questions, and that such a question can serve as a bridge into a concrete one (ch. 2, p. 29). The anchoring technique is shown twice: an inbox-zero claim anchored by asking what the inbox is at right now and when it last fell apart, which yields a ten-day recovery during a trip (ch. 2, p. 30); and a man complaining about loyalty cards, who in the pitched version promises to use the app and in the anchored version turns out never to have downloaded an existing one, which the author reads as a complainer rather than a customer (ch. 2, pp. 31–32). The general claim is that while using generics, people describe who they want to be rather than who they are (ch. 2, p. 31). Three alternative answers to the same anchored question are then shown to produce three different real signals: a critical-mass constraint, a discount-depth and privacy constraint, and an execution opening (ch. 2, pp. 32–33).

Ideas are treated as signals to be understood rather than instructions. The worked example is a request to sync to Excel, which is shown resolving into three different underlying situations, one of them a decisive differentiator and one of them not worth building (ch. 2, pp. 33–34). The longer evidence is the author's own company, Habit, selling to MTV: a request for analytics produced a dashboard, then CSV export, then PDF export, and the client still called every Friday to ask him to send the report; asking why branded reports were wanted revealed that nobody read them and the client only wanted something to send its own clients each week, so a scheduled email and a hand-built layout would have done, and he puts the loss at three months (ch. 2, pp. 34–36). The same digging is prescribed for emotional signals of any valence, with question lists for feature requests and for emotion (ch. 2, pp. 36–37). The rule of thumb is that ideas and feature requests should be understood, but not obeyed (ch. 2, p. 37).

Two sources of compliments are then named. Fishing for compliments is deliberate, a search for a blessing rather than for contradictory information (ch. 2, p. 37). The Pathos Problem is accidental, and happens when a founder exposes an ego and the other person starts protecting it; the repair is to keep the conversation on the other person and on concrete past cases, because people rarely lie about specific things that already happened (ch. 2, pp. 37–38). The author names Elon Musk, Reid Hoffman, and Gordon Ramsey as people said to seek negative feedback successfully, and tells the reader to ignore that example because it is not reproducible by someone whose feelings people are still worried about (ch. 2, p. 38). Being pitchy is called the other side of the same coin, a demand for a compliment rather than an invitation, with the repair being to apologize openly and hand the conversation back (ch. 2, pp. 38–39). The chapter ends on listening: when you interrupt to correct someone's understanding, you lose a glimpse of their mental model, and the rule of thumb is that the more you are talking, the worse you are doing (ch. 2, pp. 39–40).

### Chapter Three, Asking important questions (pp. 41–55)

The chapter's premise is over-correction: once a founder learns not to bias, they can start asking questions that are unbiased and trivial (ch. 3, p. 41). The proposed tests are two thought experiments, imagining the company has failed and asking why, and imagining it a huge success and asking what had to be true (ch. 3, p. 41). The stated standard is that every conversation should carry a question capable of destroying the currently imagined business, and that an unexpected answer which changes nothing was not an important question (ch. 3, p. 41). The evidence is the author's own omission: a company with legal ambiguity around content ownership where he never asked to speak to a customer's lawyers, which he says cost at least half a million dollars (ch. 3, pp. 41–42). The rule of thumb is to be terrified of at least one question in every conversation (ch. 3, p. 42).

The section on bad news argues that the cost of being wrong depends on how much has been spent finding out: spending five thousand of fifty thousand to learn a path is dead is called a good outcome (ch. 3, p. 42). The reason founders avoid it is framed as a craving for approval, answered by the claim that only the market knows (ch. 3, p. 43). The most informative responses are said to be lukewarm ones, which reliably signal that a person does not care, and the classic error is to raise your game and pitch until they say something nice, which can only produce false positives unless they are holding a check (ch. 3, p. 43). A short list of follow-ups is offered for understanding the nature of the apathy, including whether they are simply worn out by pitches (ch. 3, p. 43). The rule of thumb is that a lukewarm response carries more reliable information than an enthusiastic one (ch. 3, pp. 43–44).

The premature zoom is shown through a fitness app. In the bad version the interviewer mentions the idea first, hears that the person never goes to the gym, and asks anyway what their biggest problem with going to the gym is, then asks them to rank four attributes; the annotation is that any answer is dangerously misleading, and the author uses himself as the case, saying that his biggest stated obstacle would be commute time and that he would still ignore a workout-at-home app because he does not care about the category (ch. 3, pp. 44–46). The good version asks why not, anchors the generic with whether they ever joined a gym, and ends the conversation early (ch. 3, p. 46). A third version starts from goals and focuses rather than from fitness at all, on the argument that products solving problems already on that list are more likely to be bought (ch. 3, p. 47). The exception is stated immediately: when a problem is known to be a top priority, as marketing is for a small business, zooming in at once is safe (ch. 3, p. 47). The author then applies it to his own blog, where a question about blog marketing would produce a plausible and misleading answer because the blog is a hobby, and shows the conversation zoomed back out to the blog in general (ch. 3, pp. 48–49). A list of does-this-problem-matter questions follows, most of them about whether the person spends or makes money in the space and whether it is in their top three (ch. 3, pp. 49–50). The rule of thumb is to start broad and not zoom in until a strong signal appears (ch. 3, p. 50).

The elephant section is about failure points the founder finds less interesting than the one being explored. The example is a tool for overloaded teachers in the poorest schools, where the teachers' need is real and the schools' budgets are the thing that kills it (ch. 3, pp. 50–51). A second, longer example is a marketplace for professional speakers: the conversation yields a price, an alternative, and a promise to drop the agent and pay a cut, and the author's reading is that the key phrase, if you could get me more gigs, shifts the entire burden onto the product (ch. 3, pp. 51–52). The same shape is shown in online advertising and affiliate commissions, where the customer will pay if the traffic exists (ch. 3, p. 52). Product risk and market risk are then defined against each other, product risk being whether it can be built, grown, and retained, market risk being whether they want it, will pay, and are numerous enough (ch. 3, pp. 52–53). Three further examples follow: a farm-fertility tracker whose farmers promised to equip the herd and whose founder could not build the device; realtime deals for bars, where the bars will pay only once a consumer audience exists; and video games, which the author calls almost pure product risk (ch. 3, p. 53). The conclusion is that heavy product risk means less of the business can be proven through conversations, so building has to start earlier and with less certainty (ch. 3, p. 54).

The chapter closes with the list of three: pre-plan the three most important things to learn from each type of person, decided with the team in a calm environment rather than invented mid-conversation, different per type, changing over time, not repeated once reliable data exists, and usable to convert a chance encounter into a single well-aimed question (ch. 3, p. 55).

### Chapter Four, Keeping it casual (pp. 56–62)

The chapter argues with a named alternative. Steve Blank's three-meeting structure, one on the customer and their problem, one on the solution, and one to sell, is credited with solving bias, and rejected on cost: the author puts the true time cost of a one-hour meeting at about four hours once the calendar dance, commute, and review are counted, and calls it a large commitment to ask of a customer before there is anything to show (ch. 4, p. 56). He adds that in his early days nobody would take the first meeting at all, and that once his credibility made the structure viable he found it a poor use of the scarcest resource a startup has (ch. 4, p. 56). The replacement is to keep the first conversation casual, moving straight to the most important question on meeting a speaker at a conference rather than trying to schedule anything, which he says also makes a later meeting likelier (ch. 4, p. 57). The qualification is explicit: the separation of problem, solution, and sales conversations is still critical for avoiding bias, and only the meeting form is dropped (ch. 4, p. 57).

The meeting anti-pattern is the habit of turning every opportunity into a calendar block, which sets the expectation that a product will be shown and hides serendipitous chances (ch. 4, p. 57). The analogy is a stranger in a cafe who is asked out on a date instead of simply being talked to, when the conversation itself was already the date (ch. 4, pp. 57–58). The evidence is an idea about office-manager efficiency that the author took to an industry event, where without anyone realizing a meeting had happened he learned the real problem was debt collection rather than efficiency (ch. 4, p. 58). Formality is framed as a crutch for an ambiguous situation, with two symptoms given, the thanks-for-agreeing-to-this-interview opening and the one-to-five scale (ch. 4, p. 58). The rule of thumb is that if it feels as though they are doing you a favor by talking to you, it is probably too formal (ch. 4, p. 59).

Meeting length is given as a function of what is being learned rather than of the calendar: five minutes to learn whether a problem exists and matters, ten to fifteen for what you came for, an hour or more to learn the details of an industry from an expert who will monologue once pointed, and blocks of thirty minutes or more once the meetings turn sales-oriented, broken down as five minutes lost to lateness, five to greeting, five to goals and budget, ten to the product, and five to next steps (ch. 4, p. 60). The closing example is an idea for managing investor dealflow, which the author assumed was a spreadsheet nightmare; in the smalltalk before the meeting proper the investor pointed at a dozen sticky notes on the wall and explained that analysts kill most applications, which disproved the idea in five minutes before the interviewee knew what was being discussed, though the author notes it took a two-hour commute to reach those five minutes (ch. 4, p. 61). The rule of thumb is to give as little information about the idea as possible while still steering the discussion usefully (ch. 4, p. 62).

### Chapter Five, Commitment and advancement (pp. 63–74)

The chapter marks the point where the idea and the product are finally shown, which invites compliments again, and answers them with commitments (ch. 5, p. 63). Advancement is borrowed from sales as the move to the next stage of the real-world funnel, where the alternative outcome, a clear refusal, is also called a good result for learning (ch. 5, p. 63). The failure mode is the zombie lead, a contact who keeps taking meetings and saying nice things and never buys, which the author attributes to the founder's own clinginess and fear of rejection (ch. 5, p. 63). Commitment is defined as giving up something valued, time, reputation, or money; advancement as moving down the funnel; and the two are treated as one concept because they usually arrive together, as when a next step requires an introduction to a boss (ch. 5, p. 64).

The central claim is that there is no such thing as a meeting that merely went well: every meeting either succeeds, ending with a commitment to a next step, or fails, ending with a compliment or a stalling tactic (ch. 5, p. 64). Two traps are named, fishing for compliments and not asking for a commitment, and the author is explicit that rejection is not the failure, not asking is (ch. 5, p. 65). The rule of thumb is that a product or sales meeting whose next step is unknown was pointless (ch. 5, p. 65).

The currencies are then enumerated. Time commitments include a next meeting with known goals, sitting down to give feedback on wireframes, and using a trial for a non-trivial period. Reputation commitments include an introduction to peers or a team, an introduction to a decision maker such as a boss, spouse, or lawyer, and a public testimonial or case study. Financial commitments include a letter of intent, a pre-order, and a deposit (ch. 5, pp. 65–66). The strongest commitments combine currencies, as a paid team trial risks time, money, and reputation at once (ch. 5, p. 66). A compliment is said to cost nothing and therefore to carry nothing, though it still has one use as a warning that the person is trying to end the conversation (ch. 5, p. 66). The rule of thumb is that the more they give up, the more seriously their kind words can be taken (ch. 5, p. 66).

Eight meeting endings are then graded, listed together first (ch. 5, p. 67). A pure compliment is a bad meeting with precisely zero data, and harmless only because it is obvious (ch. 5, p. 68). A compliment plus a request to be told when it launches is a bad meeting, and the specific error named is mistaking that stall for a pre-order; the repairs offered are an introduction to a budget holder if very early, or a commitment to be an alpha user and a launch case study if further along (ch. 5, p. 68). A generic offer to introduce people when you are ready is called nearly good and currently bad, because the promise is too vague to be a signal; the repair is to ask who, and what ready means, which also tells you what your short-term goals should be (ch. 5, p. 69). A question about next steps is a good meeting, with the caveat that you must already know your next steps or you have ruined it (ch. 5, p. 69). A promise to definitely buy is a bad meeting with the false-positive danger called off the charts, and is identified as the answer that sank the author's first employer for ten million dollars; the repair is a letter of intent, a pre-purchase, a deposit, or introductions, and Kickstarter is praised for forcing the credit card out (ch. 5, p. 70). A question about when the trial can start is called maybe good, because setting up software and training staff is a real cost for a business but trying software can cost an individual nothing; the repair is to raise the price of the trial by asking for a case study, a team commitment, or a card that is charged nothing on cancellation (ch. 5, p. 70). A request to buy the prototype is called the best ending the author has seen, evidenced by a designer of a smartphone tripod who had gone through about a dozen 3D-printed prototypes because people kept buying them (ch. 5, p. 71). A request to come back and talk to the rest of the team is a good meeting, and the author notes that enterprise sales gives accurate signals early while consumer products are murkier because the conversation does not mimic the purchase (ch. 5, p. 71).

Fixing a bad meeting means forcing a next step or a rejection while being brushed off, with the explicit limit that this is not strong-arming and that a lack of excitement is itself the information you came for (ch. 5, pp. 71–72). The rule of thumb is that it is not a real lead until they have been given a concrete chance to reject you (ch. 5, p. 72). Even in a product-focused sales meeting, open-ended learning comes first, because a hard pitch returns only binary feedback, which is fine for tuning a feature and useless for asking whether anyone cares (ch. 5, p. 72). The chapter closes on first customers, who are described as crazy in a good way and identified with Steve Blank's earlyvangelists: in enterprise software they have the problem, know they have it, have the budget, and have already cobbled together their own makeshift solution, and in the consumer space they pre-order against a duct-tape prototype and recruit their friends (ch. 5, pp. 72–73). Two consequences are drawn: an unemotional contact is unlikely to write the first check, and an emotional one should be kept close (ch. 5, p. 73). The rule of thumb is that in early-stage sales the real goal is learning and revenue is a side effect (ch. 5, p. 74).

### Chapter Six, Finding conversations (pp. 75–89)

The chapter divides into going to them, bringing them to you, and creating warm intros. A founder scratching their own itch is told they already know their customers and should go talk to them, since the Mom Test means familiarity no longer biases the data (ch. 6, p. 75).

Cold outreach is treated as a means to its own end: the goal of cold conversations is to stop having them, because the first one or two turn into warm introductions if people's time is respected (ch. 6, p. 76). The rejection rate is dismissed as irrelevant unless the plan is to sell by cold call, since ninety-eight refusals out of a hundred still leave two conversations, and the evidence offered is a team that reached C-level executives at several major UK retailers through cold LinkedIn messages (ch. 6, p. 76). Serendipity is the second route: the author overheard a woman at an engagement party mention a talk in Tokyo and left with customer insight and a commitment to use a prototype, and the general claim is that taking an interest in the minutiae of someone's day makes you more interesting than almost anyone they have met (ch. 6, pp. 76–77). A good excuse is the third: an aspiring founder who had been turned away from cafe after cafe got to a manager and then to the owner's contact details because the author flagged down a waitress and asked about the story behind the beans, and a PhD student on the founding team is called the ultimate excuse (ch. 6, p. 77). Immersion is the fourth: the author gave free talks everywhere on the conference circuit to reach speakers who charge five to fifty thousand dollars a talk, and reports that he then decided big speakers and big conferences were a bad segment and walked away (ch. 6, pp. 77–78). Landing pages are treated last and with a caveat: Joel Gascoigne's landing-page test for Buffer is described, and the author's reading is that what moved the decision was not the conversion rate but the conversations that came from emailing every person who signed up; he says he is skeptical of the quantitative validation of landing-page metrics and values them as a source of qualified leads, and adds Paul Graham's generic launch as the same move (ch. 6, p. 78).

Bringing people to you is argued as a change in the power dynamic: the approacher is on the back foot and suspected of wasting time (ch. 6, p. 78). Four tactics follow. Organizing a meetup is called the most unfair trick the author knows for rapid customer learning, on the argument that sending the invitation confers assumed credibility, and he notes that nobody ever follows the recommendation (ch. 6, p. 79). Teaching is called undervalued as both a learning and a selling tool, since expertise plus a strong opinion is the combination that makes a teacher, and it reveals which parts of an offering resonate before anything is built (ch. 6, p. 79). Industry blogging makes lining up conversations trivial for anyone with an audience, and the author reports that even with no audience the blog was useful, because recipients of his cold email checked the domain and agreed to meet, which he reads as the traffic being irrelevant (ch. 6, pp. 79–80). Getting clever is illustrated by a founder selling to top universities who organized a semi-monthly knowledge-exchange call between the department heads of three of them, open for others to dial in, and thereby absorbed their credibility and reached the decision makers directly (ch. 6, p. 80).

Warm introductions are called the goal (ch. 6, p. 80). The author's method of last resort is to ask a room out loud: in a coworking space he stood on a chair and asked who knew anyone at McKinsey, and reports three beers, three conversations, and a diary full of introductions (ch. 6, pp. 80–81). He notes this is easier for consumer products, because everybody knows a recent mother or an amateur athlete (ch. 6, p. 81). Industry advisors are the next source: at his first company five advisors held about half a percent of equity each and mainly made credible introductions, and meeting each monthly produced a fresh batch weekly without burdening any of them; he adds that a good advisor's first conversation resembles a flagship customer's, so advisors can be recruited out of customer conversations (ch. 6, p. 81). Professors are called a goldmine because their grant funding comes from senior industry contacts who are self-selected to like new projects, and they are easy to reach (ch. 6, p. 81). Top-tier investors are named for business-to-business introductions and for landing higher-tier advisors (ch. 6, p. 82). Cashing in the keep-me-in-the-loop offers is included with a caveat that it is annoying and can burn bridges, and should not be a habit (ch. 6, p. 82).

Framing is the last section. Without a stated reason a meeting becomes a sales meeting by default, which is bad because the customer closes up about topics such as pricing, attention shifts to the founder, and the founder is not ready to sell (ch. 6, p. 83). Three bad framings are named: asking to interview someone, which signals boredom; asking for their opinion on what you are doing, which signals neediness; and asking for a coffee with no expectations set at all (ch. 6, p. 83). The format offered has five elements, vision, framing, weakness, pedestal, and ask, with a mnemonic built on those initials (ch. 6, pp. 83–84). Two worked emails follow, one about desk and office rental and one about advertising, the second reordered to put the admission of weakness as early as possible so it would not read as sales spam (ch. 6, p. 84). A third example shows the opening of the meeting itself, repeating the email, naming the introducer as a voice of authority, and moving immediately into a specific question about the interviewee's own portfolio (ch. 6, p. 85). The author is explicit that this is his method for warm introductions and that cold approaches are a different and more speculative matter (ch. 6, p. 85).

Three shorter sections close the chapter. On calls against in-person meetings, the author argues that he loses face and body language, that calls damage the power dynamic because people squeeze them between other activities and are annoyed by chat, and that phone calls sound like scripted interviews because they are; he adds that nobody becomes friends over the phone, so the warm introductions do not follow (ch. 6, p. 87). The advisory flip is a change of mindset rather than of method: go into the discussions looking for industry and customer advisors rather than for customers, because a needy vibe forfeits the position of power, because willpower is finite and changing the circumstances costs less than powering through, and because evaluating them puts the founder in control; the author adds that he would not tell them he is looking for advisors unless he already likes them (ch. 6, p. 88). On how many meetings, he cites the UX community for the rule of talking to people until you stop hearing new information, and gives his own experience as three to five conversations for a relatively simple industry and a focused segment, with more than ten still producing results that are all over the map reading as a segment that is too fuzzy (ch. 6, p. 89). The rule of thumb repeats the UX rule (ch. 6, p. 89).

### Chapter Seven, Choosing your customers (pp. 90–98)

The chapter's framing claim is that startups drown rather than starve, having too many options rather than too few, and that segmentation is the remedy (ch. 7, p. 90). The evidence offered is that large general businesses started specific, with Google helping PhD students find obscure bits of code, eBay helping collectors buy and sell Pez dispensers, and Evernote helping mothers save and share recipes (ch. 7, p. 90). A fuzzy sense of who is being served is said to produce three problems, being overwhelmed by options, being unable to prove yourself wrong, and receiving feedback that cannot be made sense of (ch. 7, pp. 90–91).

Three examples carry the argument. A woman with a nutritionally complete powdered condiment had uses for bodybuilders, restaurants, and mothers, and pleasing one disappointed the others; choosing mothers with young children who already shop at health food stores gave her both a segment and a place to find it, and she sliced further to small independent stores and asked them for shelf space, which the author reads as cutting through opinion by asking for a commitment (ch. 7, p. 91). The author's own segment, advertisers, was so broad that everything sort of worked and nothing could be proven right or wrong, since any feature could be defended by saying some group would love it; the eventual signal came from creative agencies (ch. 7, pp. 91–92). The third is a segment that is broad in a sneaky way: two founders running good conversations were getting twenty different must-have features from twenty conversations, and the diagnosis is that students, or in their case sales organizations of twenty-five to two hundred and fifty salespeople, contains a PhD student, a prep-school pupil, a homeschooling parent, a village sharing one computer, and a user on an intermittent connection, so they were having one conversation each with twenty kinds of customer (ch. 7, pp. 92–93). An industry expert is offered as the shortcut, because an expert can supply a taxonomy of the industry (ch. 7, p. 94). The rule of thumb is that inconsistent problems and goals mean the segment is not yet specific enough (ch. 7, p. 94).

Customer slicing is the method. From a broad segment, ask which type within it would want it most, whether everyone in the group would buy or only some, why they want it, whether the motivation is universal within the group, what additional motivations exist, and which other kinds of people share those motivations; that produces demographic groups and motivations, and the generic ones are sliced again (ch. 7, pp. 94–95). The behavioral half follows: what are these people already doing to achieve the goal or survive the problem, where can the demographic groups be found, and where can people doing those workarounds be found; a group that cannot be found is sliced further, because a segment with no route to it is not useful (ch. 7, p. 95). The resulting who-where pairs are ranked on being profitable, easy to reach, and rewarding to build a business around (ch. 7, p. 95). The worked example takes an app for students who want to speak more confidently and slices to graduating students facing a first job interview, then widens by motivation to first-time broadcast guests, wedding speakers, and authors going on tour, then to non-native speakers and young professionals who talk for a living; the places follow, admissions offices and department advisors, Amazon's upcoming release list, search advertising on wedding-speech terms, and a podcast about speaking; the author says he would shift the segment from students to people afraid of public speaking who are trying to get better, and start at a Toastmasters meetup where a dozen conversations fit into an evening (ch. 7, pp. 95–96). He adds two constraints on the choice: pick someone accessible and someone you enjoy being around, because the work is a grind otherwise, and the segment can be broadened again later (ch. 7, p. 97). The rule of thumb is that a good customer segment is a who-where pair (ch. 7, p. 97).

The closing section lists three ways to end up talking to the wrong people: too broad a segment, multiple segments of which some were missed, and a complicated business buying process with overlooked stakeholders (ch. 7, p. 97). Missed segments are sometimes obvious, as in a multi-sided marketplace, and sometimes not, as in an app for children that must be sold to parents as well, or a product for public schools that can be affected by teachers, students, administration, the parent-teacher association, and taxpayers (ch. 7, pp. 97–98). An important partner counts the same way, whether for manufacturing, distribution, or promotion, and their goals and constraints have to be understood as well as the customer's (ch. 7, p. 98). The last warning is against talking only to the most senior people available: the author says that while building interactive advertising products he spent his time with executives and none at all with the teenagers the products assumed, because reporting to his board that he had talked to ten children did not seem like the most important thing he could be doing, and that in retrospect it clearly was (ch. 7, p. 98).

### Chapter Eight, Running the process (pp. 99–111)

The chapter is about what surrounds the conversation. The named anti-pattern is one person attending every meeting and then telling the team what to do, which the author says is functionally the same as telling them what they will do, and which makes the customer an unanswerable trump card (ch. 8, p. 99). The failure is called a learning bottleneck, and his own evidence is a CTO who quit over the constant changes of direction; he notes that what he had learned was true and that it did not matter, because he had not communicated it (ch. 8, pp. 99–100). Symptoms are given, including the offer to do the learning so the rest of the team can worry about the product, and the complaint about having no time to talk to people (ch. 8, p. 100). The remedy has three parts, prepping, reviewing, and taking good notes (ch. 8, p. 100).

Prepping means knowing the current three big questions, knowing what commitment and next step will be pushed for, and spending up to an hour writing down best guesses about what the person cares about, so there is a skeleton of beliefs to update; a question answerable by desk research is researched first, and basic checks on a company take five minutes (ch. 8, p. 100). The whole founding team prepares together, so that the part of the company left out of the prep is not also left out of the conversation (ch. 8, p. 101). For a hostile cofounder, the author's reading is that the objection is not a refusal to face the product's foundations but a desire for speed measured in features built rather than in risk removed, and he offers two questions to surface hidden risk: if this company failed, what most likely killed it, and what would have to be true for this to be a huge success, the second credited to Lafley and Martin's strategy book (ch. 8, p. 101). He adds that these are not long strategy discussions and that a gut reaction is enough, and that if time is tight the only prep needed is deciding what you want to learn from these people (ch. 8, pp. 101–102). The rule of thumb is that a conversation whose learning goal is unknown should not be had (ch. 8, p. 102).

Reviewing is one paragraph of mechanics and one of insistence: review notes with the team, update beliefs and the three big questions, and also discuss the meta-level, which questions worked, what was missed, and how to do better, since the skill is more craft than science (ch. 8, p. 103). The author flags that reviewing sounds like a non-step and is tempting to skip (ch. 8, p. 103).

On attendance, everyone making big decisions, including technical ones, goes to at least some meetings, and two people is the best number, one talking and one taking notes, with the second able to catch a bad question or a missed signal; three is called overwhelming for the interviewee (ch. 8, p. 103). Going alone is fine once note-taking is concise, with the caveat that it is harder to catch yourself (ch. 8, p. 104). A shy founder with no cofounder is told to bring a friend as note-taker, and reminded that confidence is domain-specific (ch. 8, p. 104). The strongest claim in the section is that customer learning cannot be outsourced or hired out: bad news from a hired gun is hard to assimilate, and a lukewarm signal is tempting for a hire to gloss over, so until there is a working business model and a repeatable process the founders have to be in the meetings, though others may set up and lead them as long as the founder is listening (ch. 8, p. 104).

Note-taking is treated as the durable artifact. Exact quotes are wrapped in quotation marks so that later readers know they are verbatim, and the stated uses are marketing language, fundraising decks, and settling arguments with skeptical teammates; notes also make it harder to lie to yourself and let a later change of direction be reasoned from evidence rather than from a fresh round of interviews (ch. 8, p. 105). Twelve symbols are given, three for emotion (excited, angry, embarrassed), five for the person's life (pain or problem, goal or job-to-be-done, obstacle, workaround, background or context), and four for specifics (feature request or purchasing criterion, money or budget or purchasing process, a named person or company, and a follow-up task), with the note that the reader will invent their own (ch. 8, pp. 105–106). Obstacles get their own explanation, as the thing preventing a customer from solving a problem they want solved, with a corporate IT policy and the personal-phone workaround as the example (ch. 8, p. 106). The storage criteria are that notes be sortable and re-arrangeable, combinable with teammates' notes, permanent and retrievable, and kept apart from unrelated noise such as to-do lists (ch. 8, pp. 106–107). The media are then weighed: a primary notebook is called practically useless over months of sprawl; shared spreadsheets and Evernote are good for search and retrieval but a laptop in a meeting reads as rude, so post-meeting transfer is mandatory; a dedicated notebook works if carried; index cards are the author's favorite, one quote or learning per card with its symbol, dated and named afterward, because they can be laid out and sorted, and all the cards marked with the pain symbol can be pulled if the problem turns out not to matter; audio recordings suffered the same retrieval problem as notebooks, though he notes that people are surprisingly willing to be recorded and that asking blows the casual cover (ch. 8, pp. 107–108). He reports notes taken on paper plates, beer mats, and newspapers, and says the important property is that the notes are light enough to actually be reviewed (ch. 8, p. 108).

The process section opens with a limit on the whole book: talking to customers is a tool and not an obligation, and if it will not help, it should be skipped, the named bad case being a half-hearted survey sent to tick a box (ch. 8, p. 108). Eight signs of going through the motions follow, among them talking more than they do, being complimented, having no notes, not reviewing them with the team, getting an unexpected answer that changed nothing, and not being scared of any question asked (ch. 8, p. 109). Three checklists give the process before a batch of conversations, during one, and after a batch (ch. 8, pp. 109–110), with the twofold goal stated as attacking the questions that matter using the whole team's brains, and spreading new learning through the team (ch. 8, p. 110). The chapter closes on speed: an hour of prep rather than a week, a week or two of conversations rather than months before moving on a product, and continued conversation for the life of the company without real time cost (ch. 8, pp. 110–111). The rule of thumb is to go and build the company (ch. 8, p. 111).

### Conclusion, cheatsheet, and back matter (pp. 112–121)

The conclusion is a concession that the author still asks bad questions, including a meeting he says he ruined by slipping into pitch mode the day before he wrote the passage, and that most bad conversations can be fixed (Conclusion, p. 112). The section titled Hack it makes the counter-argument to the book's own process: Alexander cut the Gordian knot instead of untying it, and at a segmentation workshop a personal trainer listening to a group plan how to validate police officers as a customer held up his phone, asked why he would not just call them, and came back twenty minutes later with a trial session booked (Conclusion, p. 113). The cheatsheet restates the key skills with their chapters, the three rules, the three repairs, the six mistakes with their symptoms, the three-part process, the three results of a good meeting (facts, commitment, advancement), the signs of going through the motions, the twelve symbols, and the five framing elements (Cheatsheet, pp. 115–119). The closing pages point to momtestbook.com, the author's Twitter handle, his blog at thestartuptoolkit.com, and Founder Centric, the startup-education company through which he says he builds curriculum with other founders (Thanks, p. 120). The acknowledgements name ten line-by-line reviewers, credit Paul Graham, Saul Klein, and Steve Blank, and name the writers the author says directly shaped the book: Amy Hoy on worldviews, Brant Cooper on segmentation, Richard Rumelt and Lafley and Martin on strategy, Neil Rackham on sales, and Derek Sivers (Acknowledgements, p. 121).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** The book covers talking, which the author says is one aspect of Customer Development and should not be confused with the whole process (Introduction, p. 7). It assumes the reader already agrees that talking to customers is worth doing (Introduction, p. 7), and the author says he cannot teach the reader how to make a business huge (Introduction, p. 6).
- **Opinions.** The rule that opinions are worthless carries one stated exception, an industry expert who has built a very similar business (ch. 2, p. 25); the good-idea question is called self-indulgent noise except with a "deep industry expert" (ch. 1, p. 16).
- **The dream-product question.** Asking what their dream product would do is rated only sort-of-okay and turns bad without good follow-ups; it is defended as a setup rather than as a question worth an answer on its own (ch. 1, p. 17).
- **Fluffy questions.** Fluff-inducing questions do not have to be avoided entirely and are not called toxic; the mistake is valuing the answers, and such a question can serve as a bridge into a concrete one (ch. 2, p. 29). A leading question is allowed when the conversation is about to be abandoned anyway, with extra care taken over a positive answer (ch. 3, p. 47), and one dialogue annotates a fluff-inviting question as the best available route to the information wanted, with the note that not every question can be perfect (ch. 3, p. 49).
- **Mentioning the idea.** The idea does eventually have to be mentioned and money taken for it; the claim is about order, not about silence (ch. 1, p. 13).
- **Zooming in.** Starting broad is not universal: when a problem is known with certainty to be a must-solve one that people already pay for, as marketing is for a small business, the conversation can zoom in immediately (ch. 3, p. 47).
- **Conversations cannot settle a product-risk business.** Where the risk is in the product rather than the market, conversations prove less, and the author says building has to start earlier and with less certainty; he still recommends the conversations, because it would be tragic to build the technology and then find nobody pays (ch. 3, pp. 53–54).
- **The three-meeting structure.** Blank's separation of problem, solution, and sales conversations is credited with solving bias and kept; only its form as three scheduled meetings is dropped (ch. 4, pp. 56–57). Formal meetings are not abolished either: sometimes one cannot be avoided, for a full hour or for someone senior outside the founder's network (ch. 6, p. 82), and a casual approach is still advised inside a formal meeting (ch. 4, p. 61).
- **Friends as interviewees.** Blank's advice to start with friendly first contacts is endorsed, on the argument that under the Mom Test a friend's answers are not biased so long as the questions are good; the author notes he had no friendly first contacts in his own industry (ch. 4, p. 56).
- **Trials as commitment.** A trial counts as commitment only in proportion to what it costs the person: the author says he has tried countless contact-management apps at a cost of ten seconds each, and that a cheap trial has to be made more expensive before it means anything (ch. 5, p. 70).
- **Commitment is not pressure.** Pushing for a next step is limited explicitly: do not be annoying, this is not strong-arming, and a founder has no claim on anyone's contacts (ch. 5, pp. 69, 71).
- **Consumer against enterprise.** Enterprise sales is called tedious with the perk of accurate early signals, and consumer products much murkier because the conversation does not mimic the purchase process (ch. 5, p. 71).
- **Cold calls and favors.** Cold approaches are called a different beast and much more of a gamble than the warm-introduction method the framing format is written for (ch. 6, p. 85). Calling in old keep-me-in-the-loop offers is offered with the caveat that the author would not make a habit of it because it is annoying and can burn bridges (ch. 6, p. 82).
- **Phone against in person.** After arguing against calls, the author records that other people in the field like and recommend them, says to use whatever works, and narrows his own claim to starting in person (ch. 6, p. 87).
- **How many conversations.** The three-to-five figure is conditioned on a relatively simple industry and a focused customer segment, and the opportunity cost of meetings is called low for a sales-driven business where the conversations double as leads (ch. 6, p. 89).
- **Who does the talking.** Going solo is fine once note-taking is concise, with the caveat that it is harder to catch your own mistakes (ch. 8, p. 104). The rule that founders cannot outsource learning admits exceptional team dynamics where it works, and allows hiring people to set up and lead meetings as long as the founder is in the room listening (ch. 8, p. 104).
- **Note-taking.** The twelve symbols are offered with the note that the reader will probably not copy them and should build their own list (ch. 8, p. 105); several media are conceded to work, and the only property called essential is that the notes are light enough to be reviewed (ch. 8, pp. 107–108). Audio recording is allowed, with the caveat that asking blows a casual cover (ch. 8, p. 108).
- **Prepping.** Best-guess prep is said to be needed only rarely once the segment is focused enough (ch. 8, p. 100), and the whole prep collapses to one question if time is tight (ch. 8, p. 102).
- **The process itself.** Talking to customers is called a tool rather than an obligation, to be skipped if it will not help or if the founder does not want to do it (ch. 8, p. 108). The process steps are offered to be ignored or tweaked as the reader's situation requires (ch. 8, p. 109). And the closing section argues against the process directly, with the personal trainer who skipped the validation discussion, called the police, and booked a trial session in twenty minutes (Conclusion, p. 113).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each example, tool, or figure the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes it. One constraint applies to the whole section and is stated once: this session's web-search budget was exhausted before section 7 began, so every check below was made by fetching a named URL directly, and a claim whose evidence would have needed a search to locate is marked unchecked for that reason rather than silently kept.

- **eBay helped collectors buy and sell Pez dispensers (ch. 7, p. 90).** The story is a documented fabrication. Wikipedia's article on Pierre Omidyar records that the frequently repeated account of eBay being founded to help Omidyar's fiancée trade Pez dispensers "was fabricated by a public relations manager in 1997", was exposed in Adam Cohen's 2002 book *The Perfect Store*, and was subsequently confirmed as false by eBay itself (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Omidyar). The article on eBay records the first sale as a broken laser pointer for $14.83 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EBay). The example was already a known myth eleven years before the book printed it; see section 8.
- **The rule of thumb that applies "7 degrees of separation" to customer conversations, under Kevin Bacon's name (ch. 6, p. 81).** The number is wrong. The game is Six Degrees of Kevin Bacon, invented by three Albright College students in 1994, and it rests on six degrees of separation, the claim that any two people are six or fewer acquaintance links apart (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Degrees_of_Kevin_Bacon). The argument the author makes from it, that anyone can be reached by asking a few times, does not depend on the number.
- **Evernote helped mothers save and share recipes (ch. 7, p. 90).** No source was found for this. Wikipedia records Evernote as founded in 2007 by Stepan Pachikov, with the first public web application released on June 24, 2008, and describes the product as capturing and organizing text, images, and web content, with no mention of a recipe or maternal starting segment (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evernote). The claim is unchecked and unsupported by the one source reached.
- **Google helped PhD students find obscure bits of code (ch. 7, p. 90).** Unchecked; no source was reached, and the search that would have located one was unavailable.
- **Google Reader is disappearing (ch. 3, pp. 48–49).** Historical. Google announced the discontinuation on March 13, 2013, and shut the service down on July 1, 2013 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Reader). The example is written in the present tense about a live event, which dates the writing of that passage to the spring of 2013 and fixes the book's own vintage; the reader-loss worry it describes no longer applies to anyone.
- **Skype as a substitute for the in-person meeting (ch. 6, p. 87).** Gone. Microsoft, which acquired Skype in 2011, announced the retirement in February 2025 and shut the service down on May 5, 2025, redirecting the site to Microsoft Teams (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skype). The argument about the medium survives the product; the named product does not.
- **momtestbook.com for slides, videos, and other information (Thanks, p. 120).** Live, and changed in kind. The site now sells the book in paperback, Kindle, PDF through Gumroad, and audiobook, in several language editions, and points to a paid Udemy course, *Practical Customer Development*, of about ninety minutes of video plus four group workshops. The copyright holder shown is Robfitz Limited. The free slides and videos the book promises are not offered as such, and the site states no publication year or edition (momtestbook.com).
- **Founder Centric, the imprint on the cover and the company at aloha@foundercentric.com (cover, PDF p. 1; Thanks, p. 120).** Gone. foundercentric.com resolves to a ClouDNS parking notice stating the domain is registered but not configured as a DNS zone, with no site behind it (foundercentric.com). The book's own publisher line, "a Founder Centric book", now points at nothing, and the contact address in the closing pages cannot be assumed to work.
- **thestartuptoolkit.com, the author's blog (Thanks, p. 120).** Unchecked: the fetch returned no content, and neither liveness nor a last-post date could be established.
- **The edition this file reads.** A second edition is in progress. The author's personal site states that he is "Currently writing Mom Test: 2nd edition", and lists three books, *The Mom Test*, *The Workshop Survival Guide*, and *Write Useful Books* (robfitz.com). Everything in this file therefore describes the first edition.
- **Joel Gascoigne's landing-page test for Buffer (ch. 6, p. 78).** Broadly confirmed, with the causal reading unverified. Buffer's own account describes a two-page minimum viable product that collected email addresses, which Gascoigne tweeted while asking what people thought, and reports that a few people gave their email and that he "got some useful feedback via email and Twitter"; the product launched after seven weeks and had its first paying customer within four days (buffer.com/resources/idea-to-paying-customers-in-7-weeks-how-we-did-it). The post gives no conversion rate and no signup count, so the author's specific claim, that the conversations rather than the metrics moved the decision, rests on his reading of the same story rather than on a figure.
- **Lafley and Martin's strategy book, the source of the what-would-have-to-be-true question (ch. 8, p. 101).** The book is *Playing to Win*, by A. G. Lafley, the former CEO of Procter & Gamble, and Roger Martin, published in 2013 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Martin_(professor)) — the same year as *The Mom Test*, so the author was citing a book published alongside his own. That page does not mention the what-would-have-to-be-true question, so the attribution of the question itself is unchecked.
- **Gordon Ramsey, named alongside Elon Musk and Reid Hoffman as founders and figures who seek negative feedback (ch. 2, p. 38).** The chef's name is Gordon Ramsay; Wikipedia's article carries an explicit note distinguishing him from a different person spelled Ramsey (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Ramsay). The claim that the three actively seek negative feedback is unchecked.
- **Steve Blank's *4 Steps to the E.piphany* and *The Startup Owner's Manual*, and the term earlyvangelists (Introduction, p. 7; ch. 5, p. 73).** Unchecked; no source was reached for the publication details or for the attribution of the term.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **No sources, anywhere.** The book has no citations, no bibliography, no figures, and no studies. Every number in it is the author's own account or an acquaintance's: about ten million dollars lost to a future promise (ch. 2, p. 29), three months wasted on an analytics dashboard (ch. 2, pp. 34–36), at least half a million dollars for a question never asked (ch. 3, pp. 41–42), three to five conversations, offered explicitly as "In my experience" (ch. 6, p. 89), and a one-hour meeting costing four hours (ch. 4, p. 56). The author says openly that the book is a practical handbook built from his own projects and his work with founders (Introduction, p. 6), so this is disclosed rather than concealed; it still means no claim in the book can be checked against anything but the author.
- **A false example, and it was already known to be false.** The book uses eBay and Pez dispensers as evidence that big businesses start narrow (ch. 7, p. 90). Section 7 records that the story was fabricated by a public relations manager in 1997 and exposed in a 2002 book, eleven years before this one was published. The claim it illustrates may well be true; the illustration is not.
- **Two more examples that do not hold.** The seven degrees of Kevin Bacon (ch. 6, p. 81) is six, and the game is named for the number. Evernote as a product for mothers saving and sharing recipes (ch. 7, p. 90) could not be supported from the one source reached, which records a general note-capture product from the start. The chef named among people who seek negative feedback is spelled Ramsey (ch. 2, p. 38); the name is Ramsay.
- **Single cases generalized to rules.** One investor pointing at a dozen sticky notes settles the section's conclusion that the problem does not exist (ch. 4, p. 61) rather than that one firm has a workaround. One man who never downloaded a loyalty app is read as a complainer rather than a customer, and the reading is generalized in the same paragraph to people who imagine themselves the sort who solve petty annoyances (ch. 2, p. 32). One evening of chat at an industry event yields the finding that the real problem for office managers is debt collection rather than efficiency (ch. 4, p. 58), with no second event and no check. The MTV analytics motive is reconstructed from one sentence of one client contact, and the whole three-month loss is attributed to not having asked one question (ch. 2, pp. 35–36).
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** That a badly run conversation leaves a founder worse off than one who never had it (ch. 1, p. 10) is asserted and never tested; the book contains no case of a founder who did nothing. That practically everyone the author has watched talk to customers has been feeding themselves bad information (ch. 2, p. 24) is offered as observation with no count. That a friend's answers are unbiased once the questions pass the Mom Test (ch. 4, p. 56) is stated without a case behind it, and it is load-bearing, because the cheapest first conversations the book recommends are with people the founder already knows. And there is no case anywhere in the book where the method was followed and the business failed anyway, which is the shape of evidence that would test it.
- **Internal tension about formality.** Chapter four removes the scheduled meeting because formality is a crutch (ch. 4, p. 58). Chapter six then supplies a five-element framing format with a mnemonic, two model emails, and a script for the opening minutes of a meeting (ch. 6, pp. 83–85), and chapter eight supplies three checklists, an hour of written best guesses, a prep session with the whole founding team, twelve note symbols, and a review (ch. 8, pp. 100–110). Audio recording is offered and then said to blow the casual cover (ch. 8, p. 108). The book does not reconcile the apparatus with the objection it raised to apparatus.
- **Internal tension about the phone, and about the process itself.** The medium section argues at length that calls lose body language, damage the power dynamic, and never produce friends or warm introductions (ch. 6, p. 87). The closing section's admired story is a personal trainer who ignored a room full of validation planning, picked up the phone, and came back with a booking (Conclusion, p. 113). Similarly, after a hundred pages on why conversations decide whether a business lives, the reader is told that talking to customers is a tool rather than an obligation and may simply be skipped (ch. 8, p. 108); no test is offered for which case a reader is in.
- **Remedies that assume the credibility the book says the reader lacks.** The author records that in his early days nobody would take a meeting with him (ch. 4, p. 56). The routes he then recommends are giving free talks around a conference circuit while permanently on the road (ch. 6, pp. 77–78), five advisors holding about half a percent of equity each (ch. 6, p. 81), introductions from top-tier investors (ch. 6, p. 82), a PhD student on the founding team (ch. 6, p. 77), and a blog with a relevant audience (ch. 6, p. 79). Each presupposes access, equity to give away, or a credential, and the chapter does not say which of them is available to the reader it opened with.
- **A dated technology layer.** The worked example running through chapter one is a cookbook app for the iPad discovered through a Sunday newspaper's apps-of-the-week column (ch. 1, pp. 8, 11); the blog example turns on Google Reader shutting down (ch. 3, pp. 48–49); the alternative to an in-person meeting is Skype (ch. 6, p. 87); the recommended note stores are Google Docs spreadsheets and Evernote (ch. 8, p. 107). Section 7 has what became of each. The arguments are separable from the products, but a reader meeting them cold now has to do that separation themselves.
- **An unexplained notation.** Of the twelve note-taking symbols, the ones whose shape is not obvious are glossed in parentheses, a lightning bolt for pain, a goal for a job to be done, a distant mountain for context (ch. 8, pp. 105–106). The symbol for a specific person or company is the female sign, and it gets no gloss and no reason (ch. 8, p. 106).
