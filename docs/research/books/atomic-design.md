## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, page by page, with its own exceptions, what has changed since 2016, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that a web interface is not a collection of pages but a system of components, and that the page metaphor inherited from print and from the document origins of the web has "overstayed its welcome" as a way to scope and build web work (ch. 1, pp. 9–11). Atomic design is the author's answer: a methodology of five stages, atoms, molecules, organisms, templates, and pages, for building interface design systems in a deliberate and hierarchical way (ch. 2, p. 42). The stages are a mental model for seeing an interface as a whole and as a collection of parts "at the same time", and the book repeats that it is not a linear sequence from atoms up (ch. 2, pp. 42, 57). The rest of the book is about making the model real: the pattern library and its qualities, shown through the author's tool Pattern Lab (ch. 3), a cross-disciplinary workflow that gets design into the browser early (ch. 4), and the governance that keeps a design system alive after launch (ch. 5).

The author says what the claim is not. Atomic design is "not rigid dogma": the names atoms, molecules, and organisms can be replaced by whatever taxonomy helps an organization communicate, and GE's team did so (ch. 2, pp. 59–60). It "has nothing to do with" CSS or JavaScript architecture; it is about the interface, whatever technology renders it (ch. 2, p. 61). It is not web-only, and the book applies it to a native Instagram screen to prove the point (ch. 2, pp. 60–61). The chemistry analogy is dropped on purpose at the template stage, because clients and colleagues need ordinary language for the final output (ch. 2, p. 49). Pattern Lab is not a UI framework like Bootstrap, not tied to a language or a look, and not a content management system (ch. 3, p. 66), and the author says the point of chapter 3 is the qualities of a pattern library rather than any one tool (ch. 3, pp. 66, 92).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Foreword (Josh Clark and Dan Mall, pp. 6–7)

The foreword places the idea's origin at a 2013 kitchen table in Brooklyn during the TechCrunch redesign, where the author showed a screen of interface parts "floated free" of any page and described them as atoms, molecules, and organisms (Foreword, p. 6). The claimed payoff is speed: a fully responsive article page wired up from the kit of parts "within an hour", and the same approach later used on Entertainment Weekly and Time Inc. (Foreword, pp. 6–7). The authors are the author's frequent collaborators and say so (Foreword, p. 7).

### Chapter 1, Designing Systems (pp. 8–37)

The page has been the unit of reading for millennia and became the unit of the web because Berners-Lee built it to share documents; the chapter's premise is that how a thing is named shapes how it is built (ch. 1, p. 9). The metaphor helped early users, but "25 years into" the medium it distorts scoping: a five-page site, a homepage estimate, a 30,000-page university site (ch. 1, p. 10). Those 30,000 pages may be three content types on two layouts, and effort is set by the components on a page rather than the count of pages (ch. 1, p. 11). Modularity is older than the web, from Ford's assembly line through object-oriented programming to YUI and jQuery UI in the early 2000s, and matters now because of the "sea of devices"; the Future-Friendly manifesto is quoted on disruption accelerating (ch. 1, pp. 11–12). Modularity is then traced through four areas: strategy, where the three-to-eight-year redesign knocks users down Spool's escalator of acquired knowledge and iterative shipping replaces it (ch. 1, pp. 13–14); process, where capital-A Agile is distinguished from the informal wish to be agile, with Brett Harned quoted that a team will never truly be Agile (ch. 1, pp. 14–15); content, with Karen McGrane on structured chunks and NPR's COPE (ch. 1, pp. 15–16); and code, where OOCSS, SMACSS, and BEM bring object thinking to CSS (ch. 1, p. 16). Visual design follows: Stephen Hay on comps showing what a site "will never look like", Andy Clarke's design atmosphere, Samantha Warren's style tiles, and Dan Mall's element collages (ch. 1, pp. 17–18). Ethan Marcotte's responsive design, with its three tenets of fluid grids, flexible media, and media queries, made squishy pages but left each component's own behavior unsolved, which is what the author's This Is Responsive pattern site catalogs (ch. 1, pp. 18–19).

Front-end frameworks, Foundation and Bootstrap, are treated at length. Bootstrap was the most-starred repository on GitHub at the time of writing, with over 77,000 stars, and its draw is speed, tested patterns, and community (ch. 1, pp. 19–21). The costs: every site dressed in the same jumpsuit, so Nike and Adidas on Bootstrap would look alike; unused CSS and JavaScript shipped to users; a threshold past which fighting the framework costs more than it saved; and subscribing to someone else's naming, with "jumbotron" as the example (ch. 1, pp. 21–22). Dave Rupert's phrase "tiny Bootstraps for every client" is the resolution: the concept is right, and the answer is a system of your own (ch. 1, p. 23). The chapter then surveys style-guide types, brand identity, design language (Google's material design), voice and tone (MailChimp's shift to a serious tone when a card is declined), writing, code, and pattern libraries, which the rest of the book concentrates on (ch. 1, pp. 23–29). Benefits claimed for pattern libraries: consistency, illustrated by a health insurer's four interface designs in five clicks and by third-party portals matching a primary experience (ch. 1, p. 30); shared vocabulary, with Starbucks's "Blocks Three-Up" (ch. 1, pp. 30–31); education, per Anna Debenham, and relief from "special snowflake syndrome" (ch. 1, p. 31); a workflow in which going rogue is harder (ch. 1, p. 32); testing patterns in isolation (ch. 1, p. 32); speed, with MailChimp's Federico Holgado saying patterns from four primary screens carried to the rest of the app (ch. 1, pp. 32–33); and durability, since a redesign still needs forms and buttons (ch. 1, p. 33). Challenges: the hard sell against quarter-by-quarter thinking (ch. 1, p. 34); time, with Dennis Crowley's line about building the machine that builds the product (ch. 1, p. 34); pattern libraries treated as auxiliary and cut first, which the author compares to treating accessibility as a line item (ch. 1, p. 35); maintenance and governance, deferred to chapter 5 (ch. 1, p. 35); audience confusion and unwelcoming structure, with Yelp's guide as the good example (ch. 1, pp. 35–36); lack of context about where a pattern is used, illustrated by a "Highlight Block" of unknown provenance (ch. 1, pp. 36–37); and libraries that are loose sprays of modules with no methodology (ch. 1, p. 37).

### Chapter 2, Atomic Design Methodology (pp. 38–62)

The author looked to industrial design and architecture but settled on chemistry, remembered from a high-school class: atoms combine into molecules, molecules into organisms, and all matter reduces to a finite set of elements (ch. 2, pp. 39–40). Josh Duck's periodic table of HTML elements is the bridge: interfaces reduce to a finite set too (ch. 2, p. 41). The methodology's five stages are named and drawn, and the model is said to work concurrently rather than in sequence (ch. 2, p. 42). Atoms are HTML elements such as labels, inputs, and buttons that cannot be broken down further "without ceasing to be functional"; each has properties, like a hydrogen atom's one electron, and in a pattern library they show base styles at a glance but come to life only in application (ch. 2, pp. 43–44). Molecules are simple groups functioning as a unit, the search form of a label, an input, and a button being the example; a dedicated stage for them enforces the single responsibility principle and makes testing and reuse easier (ch. 2, pp. 44–45). Organisms are complex components of molecules, atoms, or other organisms that form distinct sections, such as a header of logo, navigation, and search, or a product grid that repeats one molecule (ch. 2, pp. 46–48). At templates the chemistry ends: a template is a page-level object that places components in a layout and articulates the underlying content structure, image sizes and character lengths included, with Mark Boulton quoted on knowing content structure rather than content (ch. 2, pp. 49–50). Pages are instances of templates with real representative content; they are what stakeholders sign off, and they test the system, so a page that breaks sends the team back to fix a molecule or organism (ch. 2, pp. 52–53). Pages also carry template variations: a cart with one item or ten, a dashboard section suppressed for a first-time user, a 40-character headline against a 340-character one, an administrator's extra buttons (ch. 2, p. 55).

Three advantages follow. First, moving between abstract and concrete, with Frank Chimero's painter stepping back from the easel; the author restates that the stages are not steps and that designing buttons in isolation and hoping is foolish (ch. 2, pp. 56–57). Second, a clean separation between the content skeleton (templates) and final content (pages) that still lets each shape the other; a name wrapping onto five lines in a person block is fixed at the atomic level (ch. 2, pp. 57–58). Third, naming: elements, modules, and components imply no hierarchy, while atoms, molecules, and organisms do, yet the names are not sacred, and GE's design team replaced them with Principles, Basics, Components, Templates, Features, and Applications after colleagues' confused looks (ch. 2, pp. 59–60). The chapter closes by applying the five stages to an Instagram screen and insisting the method is about interfaces, not about CSS or JavaScript architecture (ch. 2, pp. 60–62).

### Chapter 3, Tools of the Trade (pp. 64–92)

The pattern library is the cornerstone, and its benefits are listed again: consistency, speed, collaboration, vocabulary, documentation, easier cross-browser and accessibility testing, and a future-friendly foundation (ch. 3, p. 65). Pattern Lab, maintained by Dave Olsen, Brian Muenzenmeyer, and the author, is a static site generator, a documentation tool, and a starter kit, deliberately not a framework, language-bound, or a CMS; its plain default look exists so nobody mistakes it for suggested styles (ch. 3, pp. 65–67). The core mechanism is Mustache includes nested like Russian dolls, atoms inside molecules inside organisms inside templates, which keeps patterns DRY; the folder names, atoms through pages, can be changed to whatever suits the team (ch. 3, pp. 68–69). Time Inc.'s site is the worked example: a block-post molecule of thumbnail, headline, and excerpt; a header organism of logo, primary nav, and search; a homepage template of includes wrapped in named Mustache sections so each instance can take its own content (ch. 3, pp. 70–75). The final interface is described as one instantiation of its design system (ch. 3, p. 75). Dynamic data lives in JSON: defaults in `data.json`, per-page overrides in a file named for the page, so a placeholder headline becomes a real one (ch. 3, pp. 76–78). Pseudo-patterns extend this: designers on static tools design best cases where the name is Sara Smith and fits on one line, so the tool lets a `dashboard~admin.json` inherit the base data and flip an `isAdmin` flag that includes extra actions, with a collaborator whose title runs to a full sentence as the hostile datum (ch. 3, pp. 79–82). The claimed benefits of data-driven patterns are structure separated from content, an ad hoc CMS, a blueprint for back-end developers, and copy edits that non-developers can make (ch. 3, pp. 82–83).

The rest of the chapter lists the qualities the author wants in any tool. Fluid patterns that adapt to whatever container they land in are the goal, and container queries, then still under development, are named as the promise; meanwhile early responsive tools tested at 320, 480, and 768 pixel device widths, so the author built ish., which picks a random small-ish, medium-ish, or large-ish viewport, and found it more useful for educating clients than for finding bugs (ch. 3, pp. 83–85). Code view: Salesforce's Lightning shows HTML and SCSS, and Lonely Planet's Rizzo surfaces the include code instead, because its library and production are in sync (ch. 3, pp. 86–88). Living documentation: Markdown descriptions beside each pattern and numbered annotations on the rendered UI (ch. 3, pp. 89–90). Lineage: Pattern Lab shows what a pattern contains and where it is used, which tells a team what to retest after a change and which patterns are redundant (ch. 3, pp. 90–92). The closing list of qualities is descriptions and annotations, code, the full resolution spectrum, variations, real content, and context (ch. 3, p. 92).

### Chapter 4, The Atomic Workflow (pp. 94–141)

Design systems come down to people collaborating, with Mark Boulton quoted that the process is weird because people are (ch. 4, p. 95). The best time to start is "now", usually by piggybacking on a redesign or replatforming (ch. 4, p. 96). The pitch is reframed as saving time and money: consistency converts, reuse speeds delivery, shared vocabulary cuts meetings, baked-in accessibility reaches more users and reduces the risk of a lawsuit, and a living system absorbs A/B lessons (ch. 4, pp. 96–97). The interface inventory is the persuasion tool: a screenshot audit of every unique pattern, modeled on a content inventory, with United.com's button styles as the specimen (ch. 4, p. 98). Five steps: round up every discipline so all feel the pain; agree one capture tool, the author's Google Slides template being one; screenshot one instance of each unique pattern per assigned category, the categories running from global elements and navigation through forms, buttons, headings, blocks, lists, interactive components, media, third-party widgets, advertising, messaging, colors, and animation; present each category for five or ten minutes, which surfaces the inconsistent names for one pattern; and regroup around a combined document to decide what stays, goes, or merges (ch. 4, pp. 99–105). Timebox the capture to 30 to 90 minutes and capture everything, including 404 pages and legal terms, because users see one brand (ch. 4, pp. 103–104). Showing a CEO 37 unique button styles needs no design training to land (ch. 4, p. 105). If stakeholders still refuse, "do it anyways": build the system by default the way performance and accessibility are built in, and reveal it after launch; the argument is made with Wolfram Nagel's Lego analogy, a pile rummaged through against parts sorted first (ch. 4, pp. 106–110).

Expectations are then reset. Print's long shadow made the web something sold "like paintings", in Dan Mall's phrase, when the medium adds speed, screen size, ergonomics, accessibility, and preference to what counts as good design (ch. 4, pp. 110–114); the author lists accessible and resilient systems, flexible layouts, performance as a design principle, progressive enhancement, and future-friendliness as the expanded definition (ch. 4, p. 114). The waterfall is told as a story: a UX designer's PDF, a visual designer's `homepage_v9_final` file, a front-end developer finding seven typefaces and nine button styles too late, and a launch that lacks the comps' polish (ch. 4, pp. 114–117). Waterfall suits physical media where mistakes are costly, but "pixels are cheap", so disciplines work in parallel and keep consulting (ch. 4, pp. 117–118). Development is design: HTML and CSS are not programming languages, but they build the interface, so front-end work belongs inside the design process, and getting into the browser early exposes flexibility, network, motion, and rendering that a picture cannot (ch. 4, pp. 118–120). The process is compared to subtractive sculpture, fidelity built up over passes, with stakeholders reviewing work in progress (ch. 4, pp. 120–122).

Establishing direction has three parallel tracks. UX designers make lo-fi, mobile-first sketches and lists of content and display patterns, as for the Greater Pittsburgh Community Food Bank, or Jennifer Brook's gestural display patterns for TechCrunch, or a plain spreadsheet whose left column read top to bottom is the mobile view (ch. 4, pp. 122–125). Visual designers run the 20-second gut test, twenty to thirty sites scored one to ten, then style tiles, then element collages, before any full comp (ch. 4, pp. 125–130). Front-end developers act as "prep chefs": from day one they set up the environment and stub the header, footer, sign-up, login, and dashboard markup before knowing the design (ch. 4, pp. 131–132). Jason Santa Maria is quoted that ideas are meant to be ugly (ch. 4, p. 133). The TechCrunch header is the worked case: Dan Mall's collage, the author's grayscale Pattern Lab version edited live in browser dev tools on a client call, and the header included in every template so it was always seen in context (ch. 4, pp. 134–136). Comps still have a place: a client said element collages were like judging a face "by showing me the nose", so a full comp of the article template came only after the direction was agreed, and it was used to get approval rather than as a spec (ch. 4, pp. 137–139). Static comps are hypotheses; only the browser confirms them, and Dan Mall's "deciding in the browser" replaces designing in it; once in the browser, designs stay there, with spot comps at the organism level to iron out wrinkles (ch. 4, pp. 139–140). Each new template costs less as patterns mature, until a template is mostly stitching (ch. 4, p. 140). The chapter ends at launch with the system left behind in a style guide (ch. 4, p. 141).

### Chapter 5, Maintaining Design Systems (pp. 142–181)

A style guide can end in the trash beside the PSDs; Nathan Curtis is quoted distinguishing an artifact from a living, funded product (ch. 5, p. 143). The fix is a design-system-first mindset: a library that stops reflecting the products snaps off into the abyss, so a fix found on one product page, a custom dropdown underperforming the native one, is acted on at the system level and reaches every instance (ch. 5, pp. 144–146). The definition of done changes too, from a project delivered in a tidy package to a product with a roadmap (ch. 5, pp. 146–147). Ten imperatives follow (ch. 5, p. 147). Make it official: a system may begin as a side project, and the path is make a thing, show it is useful, then make it official with people, budget, governance, and a roadmap; if leadership still refuses, keep growing it until its value is undeniable (ch. 5, pp. 148–149). Makers and users are distinguished; Salesforce keeps about a dozen full-time makers, a smaller organization gives the hat to senior staff, an external consultant can teach an organization to fish but not own the system, and a scrappy startup's team may be both makers and users at once (ch. 5, pp. 150–154). The system scales best practices so users need not be senior to produce good work (ch. 5, p. 154). Make it adaptable: a system seen as unchangeable makes users go elsewhere, so governance answers what happens when a pattern does not fit, how requests, retirements, bugs, approvals, documentation, deployment, and announcements work, with a Slack channel, office hours, and state-of-the-union meetings; Canonical's Vanilla team drew a decision tree for accepting a pattern (ch. 5, pp. 155–157). Changes are modification, addition, and removal: patterns are modified for features, bugs, performance, and accessibility; a new pattern is assumed a one-off until a second application says "I want that"; removal is planned, with Salesforce's Sass Deprecate flagging what is leaving (ch. 5, pp. 157–159).

Make it maintainable: neglect is the existential threat, per Airbnb's Alex Schleifer, and the "holy grail" is a library and production kept in sync by one source, which Lonely Planet's Rizzo did through an API feeding both (ch. 5, pp. 159–160). Marcelo Somers's spectrum runs from copy-and-paste to baking the library into production; CSS and JavaScript are easy to share from a versioned CDN URL, markup is hard because it is tangled with back-end logic, and templating languages such as Mustache, Handlebars, and Twig are the bridge, which Phase2 crossed with Pattern Lab and Drupal on Twig (ch. 5, pp. 161–163). A decentralized culture or a federal government may not reach the grail, the Draft U.S. Web Design Standards being the example, and a handful of go-to patterns still points the way (ch. 5, pp. 164–165). Make it cross-disciplinary: a homepage carousel needs business owners, copywriters, art directors, UX, front-end, and back-end, and the style guide gathers them (ch. 5, pp. 165–166). Make it approachable: an attractive home increases use, but the first priority is a useful system, so make a thing first (ch. 5, pp. 166–167). Make it visible: evangelize before launch, publish change logs, roadmaps, success stories, and tips, as material design's changelog does, and automate notices, as Shyp's pull requests posted to a Slack channel (ch. 5, pp. 167–169). Train through pairing, workshops, webinars, tutorials, and onboarding; support through issue trackers, office hours, chat, forums, and outreach; invite pull requests, interviews, feedback requests, surveys, and open meetings (ch. 5, pp. 170–173). Make it public: a login reduces visibility, the patterns are "not nuclear codes", Styleguides.io lists over 150 public guides, and a public guide recruits, as Salesforce's did for Jina Bolton (ch. 5, pp. 173–175). Make it bigger, folding voice, brand, and code guidelines in, as Intuit's Harmony does with web, iOS, and Android side by side (ch. 5, pp. 175–176). Make it context-agnostic: name a pattern "card", not "product card", and blur the content when naming so the structure is what gets named (ch. 5, pp. 176–177). Make it contextual with usage documentation and lineage (ch. 5, pp. 177–178). Make it last: a system depreciates like a car without maintenance and appreciates like wine with it, and even a rebuild from scratch still needs buttons and form fields (ch. 5, pp. 179–180). The closing summary restates the ten imperatives (ch. 5, p. 181).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Not a sequence.** The five stages are a mental model held at once, not step one through five, and designing atoms in isolation and hoping the whole coheres is called foolish (ch. 2, pp. 42, 57).
- **Not the names.** The taxonomy is a communication aid and "not rigid dogma"; GE renamed every layer, and the folder labels in Pattern Lab are the team's to change (ch. 2, pp. 59–60; ch. 3, p. 69). The author later reaffirmed this in a 2019 post recorded in section 7.
- **Not the chemistry.** The analogy is dropped at templates because carrying it further confuses stakeholders (ch. 2, p. 49).
- **Not CSS or JavaScript.** Atomic design is about the interface irrespective of the technology; the modular CSS and JavaScript trends of chapter 1 are called separate (ch. 2, p. 61).
- **Not web-only.** The method applies to Word, Keynote, an ATM, and a native app (ch. 2, p. 60).
- **Not one tool.** No tool fits every setup, Pattern Lab is not a framework, and the chapter's point is the qualities of a library, not the tool (ch. 3, pp. 66–67, 92).
- **Static tools survive.** Photoshop and Sketch remain important for design atmosphere (ch. 1, p. 17); the design need not happen entirely in the browser (ch. 4, p. 120); full comps are not the devil and have a place once direction is set (ch. 4, pp. 137–139); spot comps continue at the organism level after the move to the browser (ch. 4, p. 140).
- **Agile, lowercase.** A team will never truly be Agile as the manifesto states, and adopting the whole process may be impossible (ch. 1, pp. 14–15).
- **Frameworks are conceptually right.** After the case against Bootstrap, the author says the concept is on point and the answer is a system of one's own (ch. 1, p. 23).
- **Not every one-off is a pattern.** A new pattern is assumed a one-off until another application wants it, because adding on every whim makes a bloated Wild West (ch. 5, p. 158).
- **Categories and inventories are incomplete.** The inventory categories are not set in stone and vary by interface (ch. 4, p. 102); the first pass will likely miss patterns and a second audit may be needed (ch. 4, p. 105); naming need not be settled in ten minutes (ch. 4, p. 104).
- **Forgiveness, but buy-in is preferable.** Building the system without permission is the fallback; getting stakeholders excited or at least their blessing is preferred (ch. 4, p. 110).
- **Official status may be refused.** Even with proven value the initiative may be shot down, and the answer is to keep growing it (ch. 5, p. 149).
- **Not everyone contributes.** In a large organization nimbler collaboration across thousands of developers is improbable; someone must own the system, and the rest are users (ch. 5, p. 150).
- **Outsiders' limits.** A consultant's influence is intrinsically limited because the system must become part of the organization's DNA (ch. 5, p. 153).
- **The holy grail may be out of reach.** Many platforms, a multinational spread, a decentralized culture, or a government may make a synchronized library unachievable, and a handful of go-to patterns is still worth having (ch. 5, pp. 164–165). Sharing CSS and JavaScript is easy; sharing markup is tough (ch. 5, p. 162).
- **Bugs at the application level.** Broken behavior is often noticed on one application and only "often" acted on at the system level, not always (ch. 5, p. 146).
- **Please do not.** Naming a carousel generically lets it go everywhere, which the author asks the reader not to do (ch. 5, p. 176).
- **Naming is hard.** Stated twice, with the blurring exercise offered as help rather than a solution (ch. 2, p. 59; ch. 5, p. 177).
- **The style guide is not enough.** Documentation alone does not make a system succeed; training and support are needed (ch. 5, p. 170), and a beautiful home for it is secondary to a useful system (ch. 5, p. 167).
- **ish. is for education more than bugs.** The author found his own viewport tool more useful for teaching clients than for finding defects (ch. 3, p. 84).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter. This book is the one the repo's component library is organized by: [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93) is titled "custom Atomic Design component library", and [ADR-0018](../../adr/0018-templates-are-a-design-system-layer-with-two-named-exits.md) calls Atomic Design one of two things the map "declared it would not change". The entries below record what those records took from the book and what they decided differently.

1. **The five stages are atoms, molecules, organisms, templates, and pages, working concurrently** (ch. 2, p. 42). Record: [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93)'s scope note, which scoped the library to "atoms, molecules, organisms only" and kept templates and pages as ordinary SvelteKit routes, superseded 2026-08-28 on [#410](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/410); [ADR-0018](../../adr/0018-templates-are-a-design-system-layer-with-two-named-exits.md), which adds Templates as a fourth tier in `app/src/lib/components/templates/` and leaves pages as routes; `style-guide.spec.ts`, whose `tiers` array reads `['atoms', 'molecules', 'organisms', 'templates']`.
2. **A template is a page-level object that places components into a layout and articulates the content structure, not the final content** (ch. 2, pp. 49–50). Record: ADR-0018, where a Template owns "page-level arrangement, and nothing else": chrome belongs to `+layout.svelte`, gutters and max-width to the Template, layout primitives stay internal, and region-internal arrangement is the page's.
3. **Atoms are basic HTML elements, labels, inputs, and buttons, that cannot be broken down without ceasing to function; in a library they show base styles at a glance** (ch. 2, pp. 43–44). Record: [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93), "Forms get priority", pulling form atoms ahead of the build order; `docs/design/workflow.md`, where raw `<a>` and `<button>` are "forbidden outside the atoms" by `svelte/no-restricted-html-elements`, the rule in `app/eslint.config.js` naming `Button.svelte` and `Link.svelte`; [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93)'s retrofit note as sharpened on [#187](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/187), where "full" means "no raw HTML for content-bearing elements" while structural wrappers stay native.
4. **Molecules are simple groups doing one thing; organisms are complex components forming distinct sections, and may contain other organisms** (ch. 2, pp. 44–46). Record: [#424](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/424)'s rule as ADR-0018 states it, a molecule is "a part of a section" and an organism "a whole one", applied there to put `StepRail` in the organism tier and `BackLink` in the molecule tier.
5. **The names atoms, molecules, and organisms carry a hierarchy that "components" and "modules" do not, yet any taxonomy that helps the organization communicate is fine, and folder labels are the team's to change** (ch. 2, pp. 59–60; ch. 3, p. 69). Record: ADR-0018, which keeps the book's names, records `PageShell` and `Scaffold` as considered and rejected for the cost of "no longer speaking Atomic Design" in the codebase organized by it, and rules that components carry "no `Template` suffix" because "the directory carries the tier"; `CONTEXT.md`, where a bare "Template" means the Atomic Design layer and the domain nouns are written qualified as Plan Template and Client Field Template, per [ADR-0001](../../adr/0001-practice-defined-plan-templates.md) and [ADR-0017](../../adr/0017-twelve-columns-a-practice-defined-layer-and-an-engagement-that-is-asked-for.md).
6. **Atomic design has nothing to do with CSS or JavaScript architecture** (ch. 2, p. 61). Record: [#95](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/95) and [ADR-0003](../../adr/0003-css-layout-primitives-as-native-custom-elements.md), which decide the CSS architecture on their own terms: scoped Svelte `<style>` blocks reading tokens, `@layer reset, tokens, base, utilities, components;`, and page-level layout primitives as light-DOM custom elements; [#97](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/97), typed Svelte props as the only external surface.
7. **Pages pour real representative content into templates to test the system, and carry template variations: a dashboard section suppressed for a first-time user, an administrator's extra buttons, a 40-character headline against a 340-character one** (ch. 2, pp. 52–55); **designers on static tools design the best case, so the tool flips data to show the worst** (ch. 3, pp. 79–82). Record: ADR-0018, whose `OverviewHub` takes "`isEmpty` + `empty`, both required" so a Template "cannot be instantiated without" an empty-state region, and whose 2026-08-30 amendment on [#480](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/480) adds `loading` and `loadError` as Template-owned states; `CONTEXT.md`'s **Fixture**, which holds "the longest realistic value" for every field and "never a representative one", with one row at every field's busiest and emptiest state; [ADR-0025](../../adr/0025-layout-is-verified-across-the-continuum.md), whose check runs on "content a real Practice produces".
8. **Content data is separated from pattern structure in JSON: defaults in `data.json`, per-page overrides in a file named for the page, pseudo-patterns inheriting and overriding** (ch. 3, pp. 77–82). Record: `CONTEXT.md`'s **Fixture**, where a route's fixture is "the `page.fixture.ts` beside it", which names the route's parameters and answers its requests; `CONTEXT.md`'s **The continuum check**, where the check and the drag surface "both read the same **fixture**", so a screen is described once.
9. **A name that wraps onto five lines in a person block is fixed at a more atomic level; templates state character lengths and image sizes** (ch. 2, pp. 50, 58). Record: `CONTEXT.md`'s **Content floor**, "the available space below which a named thing stops fitting", the only layout number an author writes, and its rule that for a long value of a Practice's own data "wrapping it is the correct outcome"; [ADR-0024](../../adr/0024-layout-is-intrinsic-and-320px-is-a-conformance-commitment.md).
10. **Front-end frameworks make every site look alike, ship unused code, and impose someone else's naming; the answer is a tiny Bootstrap of your own** (ch. 1, pp. 21–23). Record: [ADR-0021](../../adr/0021-govuk-is-the-reference-for-service-patterns.md), where "Only the decision is taken": not `govuk-frontend`, not its classes, not Nunjucks, not its typeface or focus bar, with adopting `govuk-frontend` outright listed as considered and rejected; [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93), "no Storybook or other external tooling" and `hugo/` out of scope; [#95](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/95), scoped styles over a utility-first framework.
11. **Style guides come in flavors, brand identity, design language, voice and tone, writing, code, and a mature system folds them under one roof** (ch. 1, pp. 23–29; ch. 5, p. 175). Record: `docs/design/brief.md`, the design brief settled on [#409](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/409); `CONTEXT.md`'s Client register per [ADR-0005](../../adr/0005-one-context-client-register-at-the-ui-edge.md), "binding on the client portal and advisory everywhere else"; ADR-0021's content rules, no "please" and no "valid" or "required" in an error message, beside [#463](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/463)'s no-pronoun rule.

13. **Container queries, still being developed when the book was written, will let a component adapt to its container rather than the viewport** (ch. 3, p. 84). 14. **Responsive design's three tenets include CSS media queries, and lo-fi wireframes are mobile-first** (ch. 1, p. 18; ch. 4, p. 123). Record: ADR-0003, which reserves media queries for "page-level/viewport-relative layout" and `prefers-*` settings; ADR-0024, where 320px is "a **low-vision number**" and not a small-phone number; `CONTEXT.md`'s **Intrinsic layout** entry, whose `_Avoid_` line lists "mobile-first, breakpoint-driven", and **Responsive**, narrowed to "a **stated user preference**" and "never to available space".
15. **Early responsive tools tested at 320, 480, and 768 pixels; ish. picks random small-ish, medium-ish, and large-ish viewports so the whole resolution spectrum is considered** (ch. 3, pp. 84–85). Record: ADR-0025, which calls sampled widths "breakpoint thinking wearing a test's clothes" and drops the rendered width matrix of [ADR-0023](../../adr/0023-layout-is-intrinsic-and-320px-is-the-floor.md) for the continuum check, "naming no width"; [#527](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/527), the drag surface, where a handle a person drags "names no width at all"; `CONTEXT.md`'s **The continuum**, "the continuum is not 320, 480 and 768".
16. **A style guide lets patterns be viewed and tested in isolation; the pattern library is the centralized hub of every UI component** (ch. 1, p. 32; ch. 3, p. 65). Record: [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93), an "in-app living style guide route" with "no Storybook" or other external tooling; [#104](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/104), the living style guide's design; `app/src/routes/style-guide/style-guide.spec.ts`, which reads the tier directories off disk and fails for any component with no `/style-guide/<slug>/+page.svelte`; ADR-0018, which opts Templates into that gate because "Templates are the layer most likely to drift".
17. **The holy grail is a library and production in sync, so a pattern change shows in both; markup is the hard part to share** (ch. 5, pp. 159–162). Record: [#93](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/93), the "in-app living style guide route", which lives at `app/src/routes/style-guide/` and imports the tier components; [ADR-0019](../../adr/0019-pen-dev-is-the-working-surface-and-code-is-the-truth.md), "`app/src/lib/components/**` and `app/src/lib/styles/tokens.css` are canonical".

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Evidence is the author's own projects.** TechCrunch, Time Inc., Entertainment Weekly, and the Greater Pittsburgh Community Food Bank are the worked examples (ch. 4, pp. 123–139; ch. 3, pp. 70–78), the foreword is by the two collaborators on the first of them (Foreword, p. 7), and the tool of chapter 3 is the author's own. No project that tried the method and abandoned it appears.
- **Benefits asserted without measurement.** Faster delivery (ch. 1, pp. 32–33), more conversions from consistency, fewer meetings, and a reduced risk of being sued (ch. 4, p. 97) are stated as outcomes; the only supporting figure is one MailChimp developer's recollection that patterns from four screens carried over (ch. 1, p. 33). The "within an hour" article page (Foreword, p. 6) is the foreword's claim about the author.
- **Illustrative numbers with no source.** The health insurer's four designs in five clicks (ch. 1, p. 30), 37 button styles (ch. 4, p. 105), seven typefaces and nine button styles (ch. 4, p. 116), and the 30,000-page site that is really three content types (ch. 1, p. 11) are rhetorical, and section 7 could check none of them.
- **The tool has not outlived the book.** Chapter 3 teaches the qualities of a pattern library through Pattern Lab's Mustache includes, JSON data files, and tilde-named pseudo-patterns (ch. 3, pp. 68–82); Pattern Lab was archived in May 2026 (section 7). The "holy grail" chapter's mechanism for sharing markup is a templating language shared between library and CMS (ch. 5, pp. 162–163), and both named examples, Rizzo and Phase2's Drupal bridge, are gone or unchecked. The book does not anticipate a component framework in which the library and the product are one codebase, which is the arrangement that makes the grail ordinary.
- **Container adaptation is preached and viewport testing is practiced.** The chapter argues for components that adapt to whatever container they land in (ch. 3, pp. 83–84), then offers ish., which resizes the viewport (ch. 3, pp. 84–85). The author says the tool taught clients more than it found bugs (ch. 3, p. 84).
- **The hierarchy is looser than the names promise.** The argument for the chemistry names is that they imply a hierarchy modules and components do not (ch. 2, p. 59), yet organisms may contain other organisms (ch. 2, p. 46), the Instagram walkthrough sorts a navigation bar into molecules and a photo into organisms by feel (ch. 2, p. 61). No test is given for which tier a component belongs to.
- **Templates and pages are defined by Pattern Lab's data mechanism.** A template is the structure and a page is that structure with JSON poured in (ch. 2, pp. 49–53; ch. 3, pp. 76–78). For an application whose content arrives from a server and whose screens carry state, the book offers one dashboard example with an `isAdmin` flag (ch. 3, pp. 79–82); publishing and e-commerce sites are the rest of the material.
- **Internal tension on permission.** Build the system without asking, and reveal it after launch (ch. 4, pp. 107–109), sits beside "Make it official" with allocated people and budget (ch. 5, p. 148) and a preference for buy-in (ch. 4, p. 110); the book acknowledges the tension and resolves it by sequence rather than by argument.
- **Governance is written for large organizations.** The makers-and-users model, Salesforce's dozen full-time makers, office hours, webinars, state-of-the-union meetings, and Yammer channels (ch. 5, pp. 150–173) address a company with many product teams; the smaller organization gets one paragraph, in which governance becomes a hat for senior staff (ch. 5, p. 152).
- **Dated device and industry framing.** Phablets, netbooks, and dumb phones (ch. 1, p. 15; ch. 4, p. 112), Photoshop and PSDs as the designer's tool (ch. 4, pp. 115–116, 137), and a "25 years old" industry (ch. 4, p. 110) place the book in 2016; section 7 records what has moved.
- **Accessibility and tokens are named but not treated.** Accessibility appears in lists of things to bake in (ch. 1, p. 35; ch. 4, pp. 97, 114) with no method; design tokens do not appear at all, and the author added them to the model himself three years later (section 7).
- **Naming advice cuts against the book's own examples.** The rule to name by structure rather than content, "card" not "product card" (ch. 5, pp. 176–177), follows a chapter built on `block-post`, `story-feature`, and `factoid-advertising` (ch. 3, pp. 71–75), which are content names.
