## 3. The claim

The thesis, in the authors' frame, is that a SaaS company is a math problem rather than a snowflake: every one of them, whatever it sells and to whom, runs on the same four numbers — current customers, new customers per month, monthly churn rate, and monthly ARPA — which together predict the month the company stops growing, a point the authors name the Growth Ceiling and place between $10,000 and $30,000 MRR for most companies' first encounter with it (ch. 1, PDF pp. 30–31). Only three of those numbers can be changed, so there are only three levers: acquisition, retention, and expansion (ch. 2, PDF pp. 45–46). The book's nine remaining chapters are one playbook per point on a shape the authors call the SaaS Hourglass, which narrows through marketing channels, funnel, and the sales call to a chokepoint at conversion and widens again through activation, customer health, advocacy, pricing, price increases, and services (ch. 2, pp. 47–49). The order matters as much as the content: a founder can do the right thing in the wrong order and still fail, so the calculator comes before the playbook (ch. 1, p. 30; ch. 2, p. 56). The claimed evidence is seven years of SaaS Academy, thousands of coached founders, about 300 playbooks, and five of the authors' own exits (Introduction, PDF pp. 18–19; ch. 2, p. 54).

The authors say what the claim is not. It is not a book about any one tactic — chapter 3 says explicitly that it "isn't about ads" despite opening with them (ch. 3, PDF p. 65). It is not a book of shiny objects or cutting-edge technology, and the authors say they do not care whether the playbooks are sexy (Introduction, PDF p. 19). It is not a "shelf help" book, and the reader is asked to install the playbooks rather than finish the chapters (Introduction, PDF p. 21). It is not addressed to the choice between bootstrapping and raising: the authors say they are not bashing venture capital, that Martell raised it for two companies and invests as an angel, and that the frameworks hold either way (Introduction, PDF pp. 13, 20; One Last Story, PDF p. 248). And the book is not a treatise on how a founder spends their own time, which is sent to Martell's earlier *Buy Back Your Time* (ch. 2, PDF p. 46; One Last Story, PDF p. 248).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Foreword (Patrick Campbell, PDF pp. 8–10)

Campbell, the founder of ProfitWell before its acquisition by Paddle, frames the book as data rather than opinion and names three things a reader should expect: that acquisition, retention, and expansion underpin every SaaS company, that the authors prove their frameworks with client examples, and that every chapter ends with immediate next steps (Foreword, PDF pp. 8–9). He singles out chapter 6 as a plan used with hundreds of companies to halve churn (Foreword, PDF p. 10). The book is addressed to anyone in SaaS or "any recurring revenue business" (Foreword, PDF p. 10).

### Introduction (PDF pp. 12–22)

The authors are the four principals of SaaS Academy, a coaching program for B2B SaaS founders created after Dan Martell's exit from Clarity.fm in 2015, built for founders past their first $10,000 in monthly recurring revenue (Introduction, PDF pp. 13–15). Their credentials as offered: Martell's exits at Spheric, Flowtown (acquired by Demandforce, 2011), and Clarity.fm; Petitpas's company Parakeeto; Page's rise from commission sales rep to CEO at Silvertrac and its 2019 acquisition at an 8x revenue multiple; Verlaque's UpLaunch, sold in January 2020 at over a 10x revenue multiple (Introduction, PDF pp. 14–17). The evidence base claimed is seven years of SaaS Academy, thousands of founders coached, about 300 playbooks, and a sales demo process the authors say clients have used to sell over a billion dollars of software (Introduction, PDF pp. 18–19). The stated reason for publishing now is that the capital needed to start a SaaS company has collapsed, more people start them every day, and the marketing channels are noisier, so starting is easy and growing is hard (Introduction, PDF p. 20). The book is for a founder who already has a B2B SaaS company, "whether you're at $1 million MRR" or has not launched (Introduction, PDF p. 19).

### Chapter 1 — Your SaaS Company Tastes Like Chicken (PDF pp. 25–40)

The opening story is Verlaque's UpLaunch, a marketing platform for gyms that reached $18,000 MRR within twelve months of launch and then sat at the same number for eighteen months, gaining customers and losing as many (ch. 1, PDF pp. 26–27). The authors name the condition a Growth Ceiling: the point where customers arriving and customers leaving equalize and revenue stops moving, which their data puts between $10,000 and $30,000 MRR for most SaaS companies, and which they say is the main reason founders come to them (ch. 1, PDF p. 31). The argument is that a SaaS company is a math problem, not a snowflake; the snowflake analogy is that enormous variety rests on three elements (ch. 1, pp. 29–30). Four numbers predict the ceiling: current customers, new customers per month, monthly churn rate, and monthly ARPA (ch. 1, p. 31). The worked example is a fictional company, Centsical, at 500 customers, 60 new per month, 10% monthly churn, and $150 ARPA, which reaches its ceiling in eleven months because churn is a percentage of a growing base while new customers are a fixed count (ch. 1, pp. 32–34). Robert Smith of Vista Equity Partners is introduced as a supporting voice and his words sit in a figure that does not render (ch. 1, p. 30). The chapter's resolution is that Verlaque narrowed back to gym owners, cut marketing initiatives and features, and grew again (ch. 1, pp. 36–37). The chapter's five closing principles include that the businesses which do not plateau adjust before they arrive, and that more businesses "die from indigestion than from starvation" (ch. 1, pp. 38–39).

### Chapter 2 — The Only Three Levers You Can Pull (PDF pp. 43–58)

Page's story at Silvertrac: two hired salespeople took the close rate to over 40 percent while ninety-day cancellations went from 10 percent to nearly 50 percent, because they were selling to customers who did not own computers; he fired both and took over the calls (ch. 2, PDF pp. 43–44). The lesson is that only three inputs to the Growth Ceiling can be changed, so there are only three levers: acquisition, retention, and expansion, or get more customers, keep customers longer, and make customers more valuable (ch. 2, pp. 45–46). Expansion unlocks what the authors call net-negative revenue churn, where expansion from existing customers outpaces losses so the business grows without a single new customer; Twilio is the example, expanding each account "between 30 percent and 40 percent" per year while passing $1 billion, $2 billion, and $3 billion of ARR (ch. 2, p. 45). The funnel shape is wrong for SaaS because it stops at the sale; the authors replace it with the SaaS Hourglass, narrowing through acquisition to a chokepoint at conversion and widening again through retention and expansion (ch. 2, pp. 47–48). The multiples claim: both Page's and Verlaque's companies sold between eight and ten times revenue, and Kevin McArdle of Big Band Software, who has acquired nearly fifty companies, is quoted saying no metrics matter more than retention and acquisition (ch. 2, p. 48). Three experiments on the Centsical numbers: lifting sales from 60 to 70 per month buys twelve months of runway, cutting churn from 10 to 7 percent buys twenty-four, and lifting ARPA from $150 to $175 makes the company more valuable but moves the ceiling not at all, because the customer count is unchanged (ch. 2, pp. 52–55). Shopify's expansion from shopping-cart software into payments, point of sale, and logistics is the ARPA example, from a 2019 Escape Velocity podcast interview (ch. 2, p. 55). Stacking churn and ARPA together takes the ceiling to thirty-five months and about $135,000, and adding the sales ramp to about $157,000 (ch. 2, p. 56). The authors' own coaching call on the example is to fix retention first, then re-evaluate (ch. 2, p. 56). Each section of the book is written to stand alone so a reader can skip to the lever the calculator points at (ch. 2, p. 56).

### Chapter 3 — The $2 Million Facebook Ad (PDF pp. 64–84)

Trainual, at $4,000 MRR, spent $3,000 on one Facebook ad for thirty days on a consultant's rule of $100 a day or nothing, needed five customers to break even at roughly $100 a month for six months, and got six; they scaled ad spend to $5,000, $10,000, $13,000, and $25,000 a month on the same single ad play and did not add a second channel until $165,000 MRR (ch. 3, PDF pp. 64–65). The chapter's claim is not about ads but about focus (ch. 3, p. 65).

Channels and funnels are distinguished: channels generate net-new attention and are chosen, funnels convert that attention and are essentially the same for every company (ch. 3, pp. 66–67). There are four macro channels. **Earned media and partnerships** is borrowing attention from someone who already built an audience, and the organizing question is who the customers already pay attention to; modern forms are podcast guesting, industry events, social collaborations, guest posts, and joint webinars rather than traditional press (ch. 3, pp. 68–69). Its one risk is that it is the only channel not pointing at a property the company owns, so the attention must be captured with a lead magnet; Martell guested on over a hundred podcasts in three months before the *Buy Back Your Time* launch, and Petitpas attributes over $1 million of Parakeeto revenue to podcast guesting (ch. 3, pp. 69–70). David Lecko of DealMachine is cited as going from $120,000 to $723,000 MRR in under a year on partners alone, with partners reselling a co-branded version for a revenue share (ch. 3, p. 70). **SEO** is positioning content to be found; the authors count YouTube, Reddit, Quora, Product Hunt, and review sites such as G2 and Capterra as search engines, and note that large language models and Perplexity are changing the field as the book is written (ch. 3, p. 71). Bottom-of-funnel keywords are the most competitive and the first results are ads, so the larger opportunity is awareness and consideration keywords; Zapier's programmatic page-per-integration-pair is the example (ch. 3, pp. 71–73). **Paid ads** work fast when they work and burn cash when they do not, cost efficiency decays with scale, and the advertiser is at the mercy of the platform: Apple's iOS 14 rollout in the third quarter of 2021 raised average Meta CPM over 17 percent, from $3.28 to $3.85 (ch. 3, p. 74). The authors say they rarely recommend paid ads as a first channel for a self-funded company (ch. 3, p. 74). **Cold outreach** suits a founder who is cash-poor, time-rich, and without a network; Wes Bush of ProductLed is quoted saying most companies under $10,000 MRR should be doing it, and Nathan Barry broke a $1,500 MRR plateau at ConvertKit, now Kit, with cold email, reaching $15,000 MRR and then $100,000 through affiliates, with the company now past $30 million ARR (ch. 3, p. 75). Andre Cvijovic at Referrizer passed $200,000 MRR on commission-only outbound dialers at 30,000 dials a week producing a hundred demos a week (ch. 3, p. 75). The authors flag that AI is saturating outbound and quote Mike Manzi as suspecting it becomes ineffective within a few years (ch. 3, p. 76).

Petitpas's own path is the worked case: 300 cold emails a day for sixty days to pre-sell ten customers, then one email to Carl Smith of Bureau of Digital produced dozens of inbound conversations and reframed the strategy as reaching one person with access to hundreds; he moved to earned media, started a podcast to get invited onto bigger ones, and later stacked SEO on the backlinks it produced (ch. 3, pp. 76–77). Matt Prados of Review Wave went from zero to $200,000 MRR in two and a half years on trade shows alone (ch. 3, p. 78). The selection tool is a worksheet scoring each macro channel red, yellow, or green on five questions: a documented process, dedicated people, a scorecard of inputs and outputs, a testing cadence, and a CAC payback period under ninety days (ch. 3, pp. 79–80). The instruction is to pick the column closest to green regardless of whether it feels like the right channel, take it all the way green, and only then stack the next one (ch. 3, pp. 80–81). Verlaque's UpLaunch is the stacking example: three channels at once produced a boring blog, $10,000 of wasted ads, and slow partner growth, while partners alone later carried the company to nearly $1 million ARR, after which Facebook ads worked because the market had already heard of them (ch. 3, pp. 82–83).

### Chapter 4 — How To Convert Attention into Customers (PDF pp. 87–99)

Kyle Racki of Proposify is the opening example, plateauing in 2017 and later reporting that mid-funnel blog posts produced a 51 percent lower cost of conversion (ch. 4, PDF p. 87). The funnel has four stages: awareness, consideration, decision, and conversion (ch. 4, p. 88). The premise is that in most markets fewer than 10 percent of people are shopping at any moment, so a company writing only about its own software is addressing a tenth of its opportunity; Dan Uyemura's PushPress, selling to independent gym owners who are also the coach and the janitor, is the counter-example, with a blog about growing a gym rather than about billing software (ch. 4, pp. 88–89).

**Awareness** is free ungated content built on up to five customer hot buttons, the emotional issues that keep the ideal customer awake, found by talking to as many people in the market as possible (ch. 4, p. 91). Its accelerant is retargeting ads, which the authors call table stakes for any company taking its funnel seriously, placing a platform pixel on the content to tag visitors into an advertising audience; Dev Basu of Powered by Search is quoted that roughly 97 percent of initial website traffic leaves and never returns on its own (ch. 4, p. 92). **Consideration** is the lead magnet, a gated resource traded for an email address and sometimes a phone number, which Taki Moore's rule says should be short, actionable, goal-oriented, and easy to implement; an eighty-six-page whitepaper is named as the common overbuild, and UpLaunch's email-template lead magnet is credited with over $500,000 of sales (ch. 4, p. 93). The 4-D chess move claimed is that a lead magnet teaches the customer to think about the problem in a way that positions the platform as the only viable solution (ch. 4, p. 93). Its accelerant is an email and phone nurture sequence; the named rookie mistake is sending the visitor straight to the download instead of a thank-you page, which loses a sales-call offer and lets a fake address through (ch. 4, p. 94). **Decision** is the marketing website, where the most common mistake is weak positioning about who is served and how; case studies mapped to the ideal customer and a one-click path to trial or demo booking are the other elements (ch. 4, p. 95). Its accelerant is everything at once (ch. 4, p. 95). **Conversion** is treated as a destination rather than a stage and is handed to chapter 5 (ch. 4, p. 96). The funnel is audited on the same red-yellow-green worksheet, and fixed from the bottom up, because fixing the top first risks moving people down a funnel whose problems do not convert (ch. 4, pp. 97–98).

### Chapter 5 — Shatter the Sales Chokepoint (PDF pp. 102–118)

Alex Duta of Albiware, selling to restoration contractors, closed about 15 percent while showing every feature every time; narrowing to at most three features chosen by the customer's own stated pain tripled his close rate, a figure the book gives in the previous chapter's running text and repeats in a box that does not render (ch. 4, PDF p. 98; ch. 5, pp. 102–103). The Suez Canal and the Ever Given are the chokepoint analogy: about sixty-eight ships a day, over 12 percent of global trade, and $10 billion of trade delayed per day while the ship was stuck (ch. 5, p. 103). The sales process sits at the narrowest point of the hourglass, so a miscalculation there stops everything upstream (ch. 5, pp. 103–104).

The method is to give demos, not tours. April Dunford is quoted that a pitch is about the customer's problem and how the seller uniquely solves it (ch. 5, p. 105). The Builder's Curse is the authors' name for a technical founder's urge to show one more feature; the paramedic analogy is that a patient does not ask for an inventory of the ambulance (ch. 5, pp. 105–106). Stretching the gap is asking questions that push on a pain until it is unignorable before showing anything, with Neil Rackham's *SPIN Selling* finding that successful salespeople ask more questions (ch. 5, p. 106). Energy management: talking about the prospect creates energy, talking about the software spends it, so the call is deposits with the fewest withdrawals needed to close (ch. 5, p. 107). The Rocket Demo Builder has three parts. **The Setup** is pre-call research from LinkedIn, social profiles, the company site and its reviews, reduced to a short bullet list, then an opening that appreciates, checks the pain points without assuming, and states the end goal, then a decisive agenda that does not say "or not", then a two-or-three-sentence company overview (ch. 5, pp. 109–112). The authors say they have audited over a thousand sales calls and that amateurs spend fifteen or twenty minutes on the weather because they lack belief that they can help (ch. 5, p. 110). **The Feature-Ask Flow** is one feature per pain point followed by three questions — whether it solves the problem, what the business impact would be, and whether the prospect can see the team using it — which surface objections while there is still time to handle them; the method is credited to Jacco van der Kooij (ch. 5, pp. 112–113, 117). **The Close** is asking for the deal, in most cases for a credit card, on the call: Martell is described as waiting on the phone while a prospect pulls over to find a card, because every drop-off point is a chance to lose the deal (ch. 5, p. 114). Where the decision-maker is absent, the Virtual Close is a dry run of the buying process; where nothing closes, BAMFAM, book a meeting from a meeting, is the only other acceptable outcome for a qualified conversation (ch. 5, pp. 114–115).

### Chapter 6 — Activation Is a Hurdle Race to First Value (PDF pp. 124–144)

The opening statistic is Jon Taffer's, given on #AskGaryVee in 2017: after three visits a restaurant guest is 70 percent more likely to keep returning, and the Red Napkin strategy engineers those three visits with a free first meal and a coupon handed over at the end of each (ch. 6, PDF p. 124). The SaaS translation is First Value, the one moment that makes a user understand the product, tracked as a core KPI (ch. 6, p. 126). Martell's Flowtown is the example: users linked their Twitter account, reached the step of scheduling a tweet, and left because they had nothing to say, so the team preloaded quotes and activation rose (ch. 6, pp. 125–126). Twitter's own case is that posting was assumed to be the value moment until the data showed consumption was, after which new users were given accounts to follow (ch. 6, p. 126). Hiten Shah is quoted that without a single killer core use case, product-market fit is a dream diluted by features (ch. 6, p. 126). Weston Zimmerman's SynkedUP could not get churn under 5 percent monthly until activation was sped up by provisioning accounts on the sales call and automating steps, and is reported under 1 percent at the time of writing (ch. 6, p. 127). The penicillin analogy: selling more of a product that customers churn out of is over-prescribing (ch. 6, p. 127). The math is rerun on two founders at 1,000 customers, 100 new a month, and 10 percent churn: raising sales 50 percent against halving churn, with the second leaving revenue about 40 percent higher before the next ceiling (ch. 6, pp. 128–130).

Three named causes of failed activation. **No clear instructions**, the Dashboard Dump, where a new customer is emailed a login and dropped on an empty dashboard with no sample data; Steve Krug's *Don't Make Me Think* is quoted on how a site that was not made obvious erodes confidence in the organization behind it (ch. 6, pp. 131–132). The instruction is to assume the platform is ten times more complicated than it seems and that nobody reads the documents (ch. 6, p. 132). **Too much work**, asking a new user to install a snippet, connect Salesforce, map fields, grant permissions, and invite a team before any value; James Clear's four laws from *Atomic Habits* are offered as a litmus test for whether the flow creates or breaks a habit (ch. 6, pp. 132–133). **No guardrails**: an onboarding flow pointed at First Value, in-app prompts, trigger-based emails, and available support (ch. 6, pp. 133–134). Canva is the worked example of removing inputs, scraping a company name and logo from the signup domain to present a pre-filled brand kit and preloading a placeholder quote rather than showing a blank page, with Melanie Perkins quoted on refining the first minutes so people felt they could do it (ch. 6, pp. 134, 140–141). Brad Redding's Elevar found one in two customers failing to activate, asked the churned ones why, heard that there were too many options, and fixed it by moving support earlier, collecting the customer's most important integration on the way in, and narrowing onboarding to that one platform; activation reached nearly 80 percent (ch. 6, pp. 135–136).

The framework is four steps. Find the First Value moment, which must be the same for every customer, by asking best customers and churned customers what they were trying to achieve (ch. 6, pp. 137–138). Nail the stepping stones under the Rule of Three, at most three steps between signup and First Value, and fewer is fine (ch. 6, pp. 137, 139). Guide each step by watching session recordings and click streams, installing signposts inside the product, and replacing inputs with automations, integrations, or facilitated sessions (ch. 6, pp. 139–141). Install guardrails, of which the authors name event-driven emails — changed by whether the user is on or off track rather than by days since signup — and collecting phone numbers, on the claim that configured text outreach has roughly triple the deliverability of email (ch. 6, pp. 141–142). Nir Eyal's *Hooked* is quoted on simplifying the action a user takes in anticipation of a reward (ch. 6, p. 138). The closing note is Redding's: he offered one-to-one implementation as a stopgap, found it effective, and kept it as a paid service (ch. 6, p. 142). The chapter's principles include measuring activation weekly (ch. 6, p. 143).

### Chapter 7 — Finding Your C.H.I. (PDF pp. 147–169)

Cvijovic of Referrizer was turning over his entire customer base annually; the chapter's method retained twenty more customers a month within a month, which at a $2,000 average lifetime value the authors give as an extra $40,000 in MRR (ch. 7, PDF pp. 147–148). The model is HubSpot's Customer Health Index, credited to Dharmesh Shah: a single score from 0 to 100 per customer, computed from frequency of use and from feature usage, that predicts whether a customer will still be there next month, and which the company rallies around; the authors note the customer is unaware the score is falling (ch. 7, pp. 149–150). The Customer Engagement Elevator sorts customers onto four floors: Advocates, called purple; All Stars, green; Average, yellow; and At-Risk or unengaged, red (ch. 7, pp. 152–153). Three actions move people up a floor — research which behaviors mark a healthy customer, lift customers into more of those behaviors, and monitor the base (ch. 7, p. 153).

The starting metric may be a SWAG, the authors' term for a guess: an early-stage client whose value event happened only every sixty days used login frequency instead, with daily logins as healthy, weekly as at-risk, and none in seven days as unengaged (ch. 7, pp. 155–156). The Pizza Meter is the analogy — Frank Meeks's Domino's stores in Washington tracked national crises by pizza orders, with about fifty on an average night at the White House against over 900 the night before the Clinton-Lewinsky story and 800 at the Pentagon on 23 March 1999 (ch. 7, pp. 157–158). Another company used time taken to complete a task, having found that canceled customers took longer, and set trigger emails at a threshold (ch. 7, p. 158). A mature index combines activity data, feature usage, and sometimes NPS or an account manager's subjective rating (ch. 7, p. 159). Lift is illustrated by Adii Pienaar's practice of treating a broken thing as the moment to build a memory, and by Disney cast members replacing a dropped ice cream (ch. 7, pp. 159–161); the more advanced form is proactive, reaching customers whose usage is slipping before anything breaks (ch. 7, p. 161). Monitor is a daily dashboard, as Page ran at Silvertrac, with the note that the yellows hold more opportunity than the reds (ch. 7, p. 162). Josh Pigford's Baremetrics is the cancellation example: churn had gone from 6 to 13 percent, so the team removed self-service cancellation and required a call, did not argue anybody out of leaving, found that many wanted a feature already on next month's roadmap, kept 15 percent of them, and brought churn back to 5 percent (ch. 7, pp. 163–164). HubSpot revises its criteria only every four to six months, with over 3,000 customers at the time, and the authors' rule of thumb is to look quarterly (ch. 7, pp. 154, 166). Groove's 2013 test is the bridge to the next chapter: an email offering a free month for a referral, sent to power users and to a random control, produced nearly 400 percent more referrals from the power users (ch. 7, p. 166).

### Chapter 8 — The Circle of Trust (PDF pp. 172–184)

Notion is the opening case: three years without product-market fit, the first version scrapped eighteen months in, most of the team laid off, $150,000 borrowed from a co-founder's mother, a move to Japan to cut costs, and a 2.0 launch in 2016; the authors put the company past 20 million active users and $100 million of revenue at a valuation over $10 billion (ch. 8, PDF pp. 172–173). The turn was noticing users teaching and supporting each other on Twitter and Reddit, quoted from Camille Ricketts, and then building on it with communities, template galleries, an ambassador program, and incentives for tutorials (ch. 8, pp. 172–173).

The claim is that social proof is any content in which the customer is the hero, and that user-generated content is its most potent form; a Nielsen study is cited for 92 percent of consumers trusting recommendations from friends and family over advertising (ch. 8, pp. 173–174). Dropbox's referral program, offering free storage, is credited with zero to 4 million users in the first year and 400 million by year seven (ch. 8, p. 174). The arithmetic offered is that if one customer in two refers another, a $100-a-month customer is really worth $150, which also raises what a lead is worth and therefore what the company can afford to spend to acquire one (ch. 8, p. 174).

The Win/Ask method rests on Isen and Levin's 1972 payphone study in San Francisco: 4 percent of callers helped a woman who dropped papers, and nearly 84 percent did after finding a dime in the coin return (ch. 8, pp. 174–176). The translation is that a software company can see from its own data when a customer is winning and time the ask to that moment — a review as soon as a Shopify merchant makes a first dollar, a referral ask when a Mailchimp list passes a thousand (ch. 8, p. 176). The Customer Value Chain sequences three asks against three increasing wins. Stage 1 is reviews and user-generated content, made as easy as possible, including pre-writing a testimonial from the customer's own data for approval and directing the review to whichever site needs authority (ch. 8, p. 177). Stage 2 is case studies and customer content; Jonathan Pototschnik of Service Autopilot ran a Biggest Badass Competition offering a $10,000 trip to Italy for an eight-to-twelve-minute video of a customer's transformation, coached entrants on how to tell the story, and used the results across marketing, sales, and onboarding — content he says would have cost six figures to produce, from a bootstrapped company competing with funded rivals, sold for over nine figures in 2019 (ch. 8, pp. 178–179). The 3S Testimonial Template coaches the customer through situation, struggle, and solution, with a fictional worked example (ch. 8, pp. 179–180). Stage 3 is referrals and references, asked right after the customer says something nice, on Cialdini's commitment and consistency principle (ch. 8, pp. 180–181). The authors note that an anointed advocate is the least likely customer to churn, because the status was announced publicly (ch. 8, p. 177). Scale comes from automating the capture of wins: UpLaunch emailed every client at their first fifty leads and routed a 10-out-of-10 NPS score to marketing for a case study, and Page's Silvertrac team carried a quota of twelve reviews and one case study per quarter (ch. 8, p. 182).

### Chapter 9 — How to Build the Pricing Plan You Actually Deserve (PDF pp. 190–212)

Joe Gaboury of SimpleConsign went years without adjusting price, on principle, and reached about $2 million of annual revenue before hitting his own ceiling; restructuring pricing added almost $750,000 of revenue immediately and cost him one customer, who returned (ch. 9, PDF pp. 190–191). He had computed that he could lose about 27 percent of his customers and break even, which is what made the decision possible (ch. 9, p. 191). The authors' blunt position is that a reader of this book should probably raise prices, and Martell's line is that nobody saying no means the price is too low (ch. 9, p. 192). Much of the chapter is credited to Marcos Rivera, author of *Street Pricing* (ch. 9, p. 192). The diagnosis of how most founders priced: look at competitors who do not do the same thing, compare features that do not match, ballpark a number, cut it 20 percent, and later add a tier 30 percent below and one 30 percent above (ch. 9, pp. 192–193). SaaS is hard to price because the costs are front-loaded and each transaction does not stand on its own (ch. 9, p. 194).

The Pricing Triangle, credited to David Skok's writing and Rivera's training, has three sides. The **Value Metric** is the unit that grows as the customer gets more value; Patrick Campbell is quoted that getting it right lets a founder get much else wrong, and his research is cited for companies pricing on a value metric growing at double the rate of those differentiating on features alone (ch. 9, pp. 195, 199). A great value metric is tied to the growth of the customer's own business (ch. 9, p. 198). The target is a tenfold return: UpLaunch knew two new gym members covered the subscription and twelve approached 10x, and ran a marketing play designed to produce the first two inside thirty days so the cost was covered before the second bill (ch. 9, pp. 199–200). Rivera's six questions test a value metric: does it grow with usage, is it easy to track, is it easy for the customer to understand, can the customer predict it, is it perceived as fair, and does it grow when the customer's business grows (ch. 9, p. 203). **Depth-of-usage** drivers are secondary meters such as API calls, database size, contacts, or seats (ch. 9, p. 196); Searchie prices on Hubs with a video-storage add-on, and WebinarNinja puts an audience-size slider above its plans while keeping webinar length and user count as quieter drivers (ch. 9, pp. 196, 203–204). **Feature fencing** is either gating features by plan or pulling one out as an add-on (ch. 9, p. 205). Trevor Mauch's Carrot found features on the top plan used by under thirty percent of customers and a jump from $99 to $200 that the value did not justify; breaking them into suites that attach to any plan moved ARPA from $116 to $130, about 12 percent (ch. 9, pp. 205–206).

The chapter's central warning is not to disincentivize the behavior the product wants. Netflix's value metric is probably hours watched, and charging on it directly would make people watch less, so the pricing runs on account size and feature fencing instead (ch. 9, pp. 197–198, 201). Slack's value metric is probably messages sent, and charging per message would have managers telling teams not to post; instead the free tier gives unlimited messaging and caps integrations and message history at ninety days, keeping the messages and unlocking them on upgrade (ch. 9, pp. 201–202). Old mobile plans with free nights and weekends are the counter-example of pricing that changed behavior (ch. 9, pp. 201–202). Rivera's plan-threshold benchmarks are given as 60 percent of the lowest plan hitting its limits in six to nine months, 40 percent of the middle plan in eight to twelve, and 20 percent of the top plan in twelve to eighteen (ch. 9, pp. 207–208). Bill Wilson's rule is that each plan should do a job the customer is hiring the product for (ch. 9, p. 208). Where usage data is missing, the instruction is a SWAG tested on new customers only, with existing customers moved over once a structure works (ch. 9, p. 209).

### Chapter 10 — The Ultimate Price-Increase Method (PDF pp. 215–228)

Dan McGuire's cube19, acquired by Bullhorn in 2021, raised prices about 20 percent by excluding long-term accounts, holding one-to-one conversations that offered the old price in exchange for a three-year contract, and adding modules, services, and support; ARPA rose over 10 percent, expansion outpaced churn, and no customer left (ch. 10, PDF pp. 215–216). The arithmetic offered against the fear of an exodus: a 20 percent increase can absorb losing 17 percent of clients, a 30 percent increase 23 percent, and doubling the price 50 percent, and the authors call these worst cases that founders overestimate (ch. 10, p. 218). Campbell's data, from a platform the authors say was connected to nearly one SaaS company in five, is cited for most bootstrapped companies updating pricing once every three years, against which companies that adjust quarterly are said to grow four times faster (ch. 10, pp. 217–218, 227). Bill Wilson's line is that not changing price is effectively a price decrease (ch. 10, p. 217). Frank Slootman's *Amp It Up* is quoted on intellectual honesty in decisions, against founders' emotional resistance (ch. 10, p. 218). The funnel effect: raising ACV from $2,500 to $3,000 removes thirty scheduled calls from a $100,000 target and lowers CAC, so even flat revenue is a more efficient operation (ch. 10, p. 219). Lincoln Murphy is quoted that pricing should be an input, not a spreadsheet result (ch. 10, p. 220).

The method is a spreadsheet of every customer with the old price, the new price, and the percentage change, sorted descending, which stack-ranks impact and sets the outreach tier: founder outreach for VIP and high-ACV accounts facing a major increase, customer-success outreach for average accounts facing one, email for the rest (ch. 10, pp. 220–221). The email template has three parts. Step 1 quantifies the value delivered, ideally information the customer has already seen on a dashboard, then says plainly that this is a price increase; the authors forbid hiding it at the bottom and forbid naming inflation as the reason, on the argument that inflation is hitting the customer too and apologizing for a raise is a bad look (ch. 10, pp. 222–223). Step 2 rewards loyalty by naming how long the customer has been there and giving six months before the new price starts, because churn from an increase clusters immediately after it; the accompanying instruction is never to grandfather forever, since permanent grandfathering is what forces later increases above 50 percent (ch. 10, pp. 223–224). Step 3 leaves a safety net, an invitation to talk one to one; Campbell's result across thousands of companies is that replies are overwhelmingly positive when the increase is reasonable, with NPS taking a large hit above 50 percent (ch. 10, p. 224). A salvage offer bridging three to six months, without a permanent discount, is the prepared answer to a reply (ch. 10, p. 224). A price increase is also named as the moment to sell a year of the current price paid in advance, bypassing the customary annual discount; Russ Perry of Design Pickle is the example (ch. 10, pp. 225–226). The chapter's last section is about tooling: companies typically wired Stripe plans directly into the codebase, so a price change becomes an engineering project, and the authors' recommendation is to spend that roadmap time installing a subscription management platform instead, so pricing tests do not queue behind the backlog (ch. 10, pp. 226–227).

### Chapter 11 — You're Already a Services Company (PDF pp. 231–243)

The premise is that venture capitalists treat services as a drag on a SaaS valuation, that this is true if services become two-thirds of revenue, and that services built the right way make the company more valuable (ch. 11, PDF p. 231). The list of services a SaaS company is already performing, mostly free: onboarding and configuration, support across channels, quarterly business reviews, custom implementation, customer-requested feature development, and custom integrations (ch. 11, pp. 231–232). Redding's Elevar is the case: onboarding expense was delaying CAC payback but could not be cut without churn, so the team moved from free concierge onboarding to charging $500 for it; the win rate did not move, the cash came forward, and customers took the process more seriously once it was paid (ch. 11, pp. 231–232).

Two services the authors say almost every SaaS company should charge for. **Paid onboarding**, at a rule of thumb of 10 to 20 percent of annual contract value, with the side benefit that it gives the seller something to discount so that recurring revenue stays at list (ch. 11, pp. 233, 242). **Priority support**, which Campbell named as the industry's most underused add-on and for which he reports that about 20 percent of customers opt in; Disney's Genie+ at $15 to $30 against a $160 admission, estimated at over $300 million of revenue, is offered as the same 10-to-20-percent ratio (ch. 11, pp. 233–234).

The SaaS Services Planner has three phases. **Map the problems** by asking what the company would build if it were not confined to software, then charting the customer's starting point, ideal end state, every action in between, and every problem at each step, ranked not by what could be charged but by the risk each poses to recurring revenue (ch. 11, pp. 236–238). Petitpas's Parakeeto is the story: stuck at $10,000 MRR for a year, four quarters of the build-ship-hope cycle with no growth, and the question that changed it (ch. 11, pp. 234–236). **Build the process** by taking the customer's existing way of solving the problem and making it better, faster, or doing it for them, tested on one customer and repeated, with a warning not to build a second business or an in-house agency (ch. 11, p. 238). **Nail the pricing** in three steps: set a margin target, with up to 40 percent recovering some cost, 50 percent breaking even, 60 percent a modest profit, and 70 percent and up funding growth, because unutilized time takes 10 to 20 percent and overhead another 20 to 30 (ch. 11, p. 239); estimate delivery cost by dividing fully loaded salary by 2,080 hours (ch. 11, pp. 239–240); and divide the delivery cost by one minus the margin target (ch. 11, p. 240). Flat-fee pricing is preferred over hourly because it sells more easily and holds more margin, subject to Petitpas's rule about estimate accuracy; the worked example prices a six-hour data migration at about $417 on a 40 percent target (ch. 11, pp. 240–241). The closing claim is that a services strategy pulls all three levers at once, and that Parakeeto added more MRR in the week after its new offer than in the prior eighteen months (ch. 11, pp. 241–242).

### One Last Story, and the summary section (PDF pp. 245–254)

Maddie Bell of Scheduler AI launched in 2022 and plateaued near $8,000 MRR. She entered the four numbers into a spreadsheet every Monday, cut every channel except cold outbound on LinkedIn because it taught her the most about customers fastest, learned that mid-market SaaS sales teams were the buyer, raised the price from $49 to $499 a month and kept closing, and installed the Rocket Demo Builder to take close rates over 30 percent; the company raised a multimillion-dollar round in early 2024 (One Last Story, PDF pp. 245–247). The summary section is a diagnostic index: compute the ceiling, test which lever moves it most, then route to a chapter by whether the problem is attention, demand generation, sales, activation, customer health, social proof, pricing structure, a price increase, or services (Run the System, PDF pp. 252–254). Sources are thirty-nine endnotes carrying the URLs and citations behind the book's figures (Sources, PDF pp. 260–267).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the authors limit their own claim, admit a counter-case, or say a rule does not apply.

- **Scope.** The book is written for a founder who already has a B2B SaaS company, though the authors say anyone could find it useful and that it applies from pre-launch to $1 million MRR (Introduction, PDF p. 19). Campbell widens it in the foreword to any recurring-revenue business (Foreword, PDF p. 10).
- **Sequencing over completeness.** The sections are built to stand alone and the reader is told to skip to the lever the calculator points at, on the argument that the right thing in the wrong order still loses (ch. 2, PDF p. 56; ch. 1, pp. 30, 39).
- **Expansion does not move the ceiling.** The authors' own third experiment shows that raising ARPA makes the company more valuable and leaves the Growth Ceiling timeline where it was, because the customer count is unchanged (ch. 2, PDF p. 55).
- **Ramping sales is sometimes right.** Where churn is already as low as it realistically goes, the authors say increasing sales and marketing is probably the right call, if it is affordable (ch. 6, PDF p. 130).
- **Paid ads first.** The authors say they rarely recommend paid ads as a first channel for a self-funded company, and name Trainual as the exception in the same paragraph (ch. 3, PDF p. 74).
- **Cold outreach.** Whether it works depends primarily on the market, and it needs enough money in a deal to pay the people dialing; at other price points it becomes prohibitively expensive or the payback period very long (ch. 3, PDF pp. 75–76). The authors also warn that AI saturation may make it ineffective within a few years (ch. 3, p. 76).
- **Execution over selection.** The reader is told that a guess is acceptable, that executing well on the wrong channel still grows a company, and that the column closest to green wins whether or not it feels right, because the commitment is not permanent (ch. 3, PDF pp. 79–81).
- **CAC payback.** The ninety-day target may be missed and the founder may still move to the next channel, as long as the other four measures are green and payback is trending down and the company is not cash-constrained; the reason given is that payback can be inflated by a new salesperson or by wrong pricing rather than by the channel (ch. 3, PDF pp. 81, 84).
- **The Rule of Three is a ceiling, not a target.** Three stepping stones is the maximum; two or one is better, and the authors warn against waiting for a perfect flow (ch. 6, PDF p. 139).
- **The Customer Health Index will be wrong.** The authors say so flatly, twice, and point to HubSpot revising its own criteria every four to six months; the instruction is to start with logins or a guess and iterate, and then to leave it alone long enough to collect data (ch. 7, PDF pp. 154, 157, 165–166).
- **Reaching out to unengaged customers backfires sometimes.** Calling a few dozen red accounts will surface people who had been meaning to cancel (ch. 7, PDF p. 163).
- **Not every customer reaches stage 2.** The Customer Value Chain is described as a natural progression that only the happiest and most engaged customers complete, and customers are named as poor storytellers who have to be coached (ch. 8, PDF pp. 179–180).
- **A value metric can be the wrong price driver.** Anchoring price to the value metric alone can make users want to use the product less; Netflix and Slack are the two worked cases of deliberately not charging on the value metric (ch. 9, PDF pp. 195, 197–198, 201–202).
- **Features in the top plan can hurt.** Bundling features most customers do not need into the highest plan is named as harming retention and flattening expansion, against the intuition that more inclusion is generous (ch. 9, PDF pp. 205–206).
- **The starting point may be a guess.** The authors say their own SWAG is how most founders set their first price and that it is not entirely bad to start there, and they prescribe a SWAG again where usage data is missing, tested on new customers only (ch. 9, PDF pp. 193, 209).
- **What counts as a major increase is left to the reader.** The threshold for founder outreach depends on contract value, dollar amount, and concentration risk, and the authors say to follow instinct — with the caveat not to use that as a reason to skip the increase (ch. 10, PDF p. 221).
- **Price increases have a ceiling of tolerance.** NPS is reported to take a large hit above 50 percent, and a salvage offer is the prepared exception for a customer the increase genuinely strains (ch. 10, PDF p. 224).
- **Grandfathering has a short form and a forbidden form.** Six months of the old price is the recommended concession; permanent grandfathering is named as what creates the 50-percent corrections later (ch. 10, PDF pp. 223–224).
- **Services can decrease a valuation.** The authors concede the venture-capital position holds where services become two-thirds of revenue or the company effectively becomes an agency (ch. 11, PDF p. 231).
- **Services are not the goal.** The reader is told not to build a second business, not to hire a half dozen people, and to sacrifice services revenue rather than recurring revenue when something must be discounted (ch. 11, PDF pp. 238, 242).
- **Flat-fee pricing has a limit.** It is preferred only where the delivery estimate is reliable; where it is not, the margin can be eaten with no way to recover (ch. 11, PDF p. 240).
- **The demo has a variant the book does not print.** A box titled *What If My Sales Motion Is Different?* addresses a low-priced product-led offering, and its content does not render in either the extraction or the page image (ch. 5, PDF p. 113).

- **Kit, formerly ConvertKit, past $30 million ARR (ch. 3, PDF p. 75).** Unchecked. kit.com refuses an unauthenticated fetch with a 403 and the encyclopedia article under the old name no longer exists.
- **Dan McGuire's cube19, acquired by Bullhorn in 2021 (ch. 10, PDF p. 215).** Unchecked. The obvious Bullhorn press-release path returns 404 and no other source could be reached.
- **The Suez Canal figures: about sixty-eight ships a day, over 12 percent of global trade, $10 billion of trade delayed per day (ch. 5, PDF p. 103).** Unchecked. Endnote 15 points at a 2021 Bloomberg feature behind a paywall. The canal's traffic has in any case been disrupted since 2023 by Red Sea attacks, which the 2024 text does not mention.
- **Page's Silvertrac exit.** The book names two different acquirers: chapter 2 says Track Force Valiant (ch. 2, PDF p. 44) and About the Authors says K1 Investments in 2019 (About the Authors, PDF p. 258). Unchecked as to which is right; the discrepancy is internal to the book and is noted again in section 8.
- **Unchecked by construction, because the source is a private figure with no outside record.** Campbell's finding across 13,000 mid-market companies and the claim that quarterly price adjustment produces four times faster growth (ch. 10, PDF pp. 218, 227); his finding that value-metric pricing doubles the growth rate (ch. 9, p. 199); his figure that about 20 percent of customers opt into priority support, which endnote 35 attributes to a remark made live at a SaaS Academy event (ch. 11, p. 234); the $10,000-to-$30,000 first-plateau band drawn from SaaS Academy's own client data (ch. 1, p. 31); and every client result in the book — SynkedUP's churn under 1 percent, Referrizer's twenty retained customers a month and $2,000 lifetime value, Elevar's activation near 80 percent and $500 onboarding fee, Carrot's ARPA from $116 to $130, SimpleConsign's $750,000 and 27 percent break-even, cube19's ARPA over 10 percent and no customers lost, Trainual's $165,000 MRR on one channel, DealMachine's $120,000 to $723,000, Review Wave's $200,000, Parakeeto's $1 million from podcast guesting, Service Autopilot's competition videos, Scheduler AI's $49 to $499, and Dropbox's zero to 4 million users. These are the authors' own accounts or their clients', with no outside source to check against.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **The sample is the authors' own program.** Nearly every company in the book is a SaaS Academy client, coach, alumnus, speaker, or one of the four authors' own — Trainual, DealMachine, Parakeeto, Review Wave, Proposify, PushPress, Albiware, Elevar, SynkedUP, Referrizer, Service Autopilot, Carrot, Searchie, WebinarNinja, SimpleConsign, cube19, Design Pickle, Scheduler AI, UpLaunch, Silvertrac. The counter-cases, companies in the same program that ran the same playbooks and did not grow, do not appear anywhere, and the book claims its conclusions are drawn from "mountains of data" on thousands of coached founders without printing any of it (ch. 1, PDF p. 30).
- **The book's three headline data claims all come from one man, who also wrote the foreword.** Patrick Campbell supplies the 13,000-company pricing finding, the four-times-faster growth figure, the doubling from value-metric pricing, and the 20 percent priority-support opt-in (ch. 9, PDF p. 199; ch. 10, pp. 218, 227; ch. 11, p. 234), and he is the author of the foreword that tells the reader the book is facts rather than opinion (Foreword, PDF pp. 8–9). One of the four is sourced to a remark made live at the authors' own event (endnote 35, Sources, PDF p. 266).
- **The Growth Ceiling model is arithmetic presented as a law.** The authors call it "not a theory" and "just numbers" (ch. 1, PDF p. 35), but the result follows entirely from holding new customers per month constant while churn scales with the base. Every input is assumed fixed, including the churn rate itself, which the rest of the book argues is the most changeable number in the business. The model has no term for word of mouth, for referral growth the book devotes chapter 8 to, or for a company whose acquisition also grows with its customer count.
- **A figure that does not survive arithmetic.** The snowflake passage says scientists estimate there are more possible snowflakes, by a factor the book writes as 10⁷⁰, than there are "atoms in the entire universe", and then prints a number with seventy zeros (ch. 1, PDF p. 29). The commonly cited estimate for atoms in the observable universe is around 10⁸⁰, so the number printed is smaller than the quantity it is said to exceed.
- **An internal contradiction about the acquirer.** Silvertrac's buyer is Track Force Valiant in chapter 2 (PDF p. 44) and K1 Investments in About the Authors (PDF p. 258).
- **Correlation presented as cause.** SimpleConsign's $750,000 is attributed to the pricing change alone with no other variable named (ch. 9, PDF p. 191). Carrot's ARPA move from $116 to $130 is credited to unbundling, though the same passage says the plans and price points were reworked at the same time (ch. 9, pp. 205–206). Trainual's run from $4,000 to $165,000 MRR is told as the result of one ad and never mentions the product, the market, or anything the company did in those years besides buy media (ch. 3, pp. 64–65). Dropbox's first four million users are credited to the referral program (ch. 8, p. 174), and Notion's recovery to community-led growth, in a passage that also lists personal-network intros, guest posts, and Reddit, Hacker News, and Product Hunt (ch. 8, pp. 172–173).
- **A study carrying more weight than a study can.** The payphone experiment is asked to establish that a person who has just experienced a small good thing will help a stranger, and from that, that a software company should time its referral asks to a customer's win (ch. 8, pp. 174–176). The jump is from a dime and a stack of dropped papers in 1972 to a business relationship, and the reported effect — from 4 percent to nearly 84 percent, which the authors themselves describe as a 2,000 percent increase — is the kind of magnitude that later social-psychology replication has often failed to reproduce. Section 7 records that the cited source could not be opened.
- **The advice contradicts its own anchor story.** Chapter 3 opens with a $50,000-a-year company betting 75 percent of a month's revenue on Facebook ads, and then states that the authors rarely recommend paid ads as a first channel for a self-funded company (ch. 3, PDF pp. 64, 74). The chapter that argues a founder should pick the channel closest to green regardless of whether it feels right is titled after the one that was neither.
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** The four numbers, the three levers, and the 3-High/3-Low style metrics are all defined in subscription terms — MRR, monthly churn rate, ARPA, net-negative revenue churn — and the book offers no way to compute any of them for a product sold per transaction rather than per month; the nearest it comes is a sentence conceding in the foreword that the model is for anyone in SaaS or in "any recurring revenue business" (Foreword, PDF p. 10). The recession and durability arguments rest on no data at all. The claim that configured text outreach has roughly triple the deliverability of email carries no source (ch. 6, p. 142). The assertion that retargeting is table stakes is made in a book published two years after the same chapter's iOS 14 story about platform-level tracking restrictions (ch. 4, p. 92; ch. 3, p. 74).
- **The content the reader most needs is behind a form.** Nine chapters end by sending the reader to softwarebook.com for the worksheet, calculator, template, or swipe file that operationalizes the chapter, and several of the book's own numbers — the energy-flow chart, the Growth Ceiling graphs, the Customer Engagement Elevator, Campbell's findings, the price-increase email itself — sit in figures and boxes rather than prose. Section 7 records that the site now requires an account. A reader of the book alone has the shape of every framework and few of the artifacts.
- **The lost boxes are not incidental.** In both the text extraction and the page images, every shaded callout and every chart renders blank, including the definition of net-negative revenue churn (ch. 2, PDF p. 45), the CAC payback primer (ch. 3, p. 74), the three Feature-Ask questions at their first appearance (ch. 5, p. 113), the activation interview questions (ch. 6, p. 138), Campbell's 13,000-company result (ch. 10, p. 218), Martell's average-increase figure (ch. 10, p. 220), the onboarding price guidance (ch. 11, p. 233), and the price-increase email template itself (ch. 10, p. 222). Several survive only because the closing principles of the same chapter restate them in running text.
