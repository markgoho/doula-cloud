## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that the waste-elimination methods of lean manufacturing transfer to marketing, and that a business gets more leads and more profit by doing *less* marketing through three force multipliers — tools, assets, and processes — governed by nine named principles (Chapter 1, pp. 21–23, 28; Conclusion, p. 261). Lean is quoted through Womack and Jones as "a way to do more and more with less and less" while coming closer to giving customers exactly what they want, and waste in lean is defined as "anything that consumes resources the customer doesn't value" (Chapter 1, p. 20). Applied to marketing, the test the author sets is that the marketing should be valuable enough that "your target market would pay you to receive it" (Chapter 1, p. 21). The book's stated synthesis is between two traditions it names: Seth Godin's remarkable-work-for-a-tribe end of the spectrum and Dan Kennedy's measured direct-response end, taking direct response's measurability and brand marketing's goodwill at once (Introduction, pp. 12–14; Chapter 1, pp. 23–25). Marketing without infrastructure is the failure mode the book opens on, called "random acts of marketing" (Introduction, p. 8).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a strategy book: *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* was "highly focused on strategy" and this one "is much more tactical, while still being self-contained" (Introduction, p. 26). It is not technical instruction, because books "are surprisingly difficult to update" and screenshots and how-tos "would be obsolete by the time you read them", so those live in an online companion, the Lean Marketing Hub (Chapter 1, p. 30). It is not original, and the author concedes the criticism directly — the work has been called "nothing new" and "common sense", which he largely agrees with, answering that "common sense is not common practice" (Chapter 1, p. 29). It is not a single school: the author declines the labels "brand guy" and "direct response guy" and says the more labels a person applies to themselves "the stupider you get" (Introduction, p. 13). It is not for a reader who wants the contradictions resolved — the book's opening gambit is the "midwit" meme and an instruction to hold conflicting ideas rather than reconcile them (Introduction, pp. 9–11, 14). And it is not easy in the sense of effortless: the author writes that implementing the book "will often feel like crawling on your hands and knees" over broken glass, and estimates "only 3 percent of people are real action-takers" who will tolerate it (Introduction, p. 12).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

Each chapter opens with a printed summary page and closes with a list of Action Items; both are named in Chapter 5 as a deliberate copywriting device (Chapter 5, p. 107).

### Introduction (pp. 7–14)

The opening frame is a scriptural one — a city on a hill, a lamp not put under a basket — and the argument that merit alone does not produce business success: "you don't get what you deserve; you get what you negotiate", and "hope is not an effective marketing strategy" (Introduction, p. 7). Five failure scenarios are offered as diagnosis: money burned on a branding agency for a logo, a web developer whose site generates no leads, marketing that feels "pushy, cheesy, or sleazy", an underwhelming marketing hire, and ads that attracted low-quality leads. The author's reading is that none of those suppliers were bad, but that the buyer "set them up for failure" by having no infrastructure to plug a tool or tactic into (Introduction, p. 8). "Entrepreneur" is defined for the book as "someone who solves problems for a profit", and "customers" is used to cover clients, patients, users, donors, and members (Introduction, pp. 8–9). Marketing is then asserted to be both easy — simple, learnable, a proven framework and process — and really hard, with the 3 percent action-taker estimate attached (Introduction, pp. 11–12). Godin and Kennedy are each summarized and each credited as an influence, with the direct-response tradition traced back to Claude Hopkins, John Caples, and Robert Collier (Introduction, pp. 12–13).

### Chapter 1, Leaning Into Marketing (pp. 17–31)

The origin story is a speaking engagement in León, Guanajuato, at a lean-manufacturing conference, where Luis Socconini of the Lean Six Sigma Institute told the author "I consider your book to be lean marketing" (Chapter 1, pp. 18–19). Lean's history is given through Toyota's post-war Production System against Ford mass production, Japan's rise to a manufacturing power in the 1970s and 1980s, and Jeff Bezos naming *Lean Thinking* a favorite (Chapter 1, pp. 19–20). Mass marketing is cast as the marketing analog of that waste: it "focuses on interrupting as many people as possible with a standard message about an average product" (Chapter 1, pp. 20–21). Principle 1 follows, framed through economic externalities — most marketing produces negative ones, like factory smoke, while lean marketing produces positive ones "benefiting even those who'll never become customers" (Chapter 1, p. 21). Principle 2 comes from value stream mapping and flow: marketing is happening in sales conversations, delivery, and customer service whether or not it is called that, so siloed departments are the "ultimate business inefficiency" (Chapter 1, pp. 21–23). Brand marketing is defined as marketing "that's not measurable" and is given the John Wanamaker half-the-money line; direct response is defined as advertising "designed to pay for itself" whose practitioners "care about the 1 percent of people who saw their ad and clicked" and "burn a lot of goodwill in the process" (Chapter 1, pp. 23–24). Six Sigma's fewer than 3.4 defects per million opportunities is offered as the standard marketing could learn from (Chapter 1, p. 25). The author declines to pick a side, noting that companies often start with direct response and move toward brand marketing at scale (Chapter 1, p. 25). The book's relation to *The 1-Page Marketing Plan* is stated: strategy there, tactics here, "1 + 1 = 3" together (Chapter 1, p. 26). Product-market fit is defined as meeting real customer needs better than the alternatives, with marketing as "an amplifier" — "a bad singer with a good microphone is just a loud, bad singer" — and the caution not to turn that into paralysis by analysis (Chapter 1, pp. 26–27). Leverage is defined as anything that multiplies the force of an input, and the three force multipliers are introduced: tools, assets, processes (Chapter 1, pp. 27–28). The Action Items send the reader to review every marketing activity for value, review the customer journey, and "use the 1-Page Marketing Plan canvas" (Chapter 1, pp. 30–31).

### Chapter 2, Who Are Your People? (pp. 33–51)

The claimed biggest marketing mistake is starting with the product and then looking for a market, called in Silicon Valley "a solution in search of a problem"; Principle 3 follows (Chapter 2, p. 34). The niche argument uses a clay-shooting instructor's advice to aim where the target is going, and names Facebook's first two years in colleges, Apple's six years of iPod, and Amazon starting as a bookstore (Chapter 2, pp. 35–36). The target is "an inch wide and a mile deep", and "women over 40 years old" is given as an example of a segment that is not narrow at all, being 1.5 billion people (Chapter 2, p. 36). Alex Hormozi's "woman in the red dress" from *The Matrix* is borrowed for the distraction of new markets, with a "parking lot" for future ideas as the author's remedy (Chapter 2, pp. 36–38). "Specificity sells" and generality repels, argued from commercial-intent search terms, from the gendered pricing of identical razors and deodorants ("the pink tax"), and from pain-relief packaging targeted at a body part when the active ingredient is identical (Chapter 2, pp. 38–39). Whether two audiences are different markets or segments is tested with five questions about message, media, nurturing, sales process, and onboarding, worked through a baby-products maker selling both direct-to-consumer and wholesale (Chapter 2, pp. 39–40). Demand is to be tapped, not generated: generating it is possible but slow and costly, with Colgate's 1873 toothpaste and the decades it took to make brushing universal as the example, and "too many people are trying to sell binoculars to the blind" as the failure (Chapter 2, p. 41). Eugene Schwartz's five stages of customer awareness — unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, most aware — are reproduced with a sample search query and a recommended tactic for each; the unaware "are usually not worth marketing to" (Chapter 2, pp. 41–43). Differentiation comes from "talent stacking" at an intersection rather than being best in the world, argued from Usain Bolt's 9.58 in Berlin in 2009 where fourth place was 0.35 of a second behind, from Mike Michalowicz's "Different is better", and from Dharmesh Shah's Venn diagram of success, which the author runs on himself to estimate about 100 competitors worldwide (Chapter 2, pp. 43–46). Seven dimensions to niche on are listed: location, demography, shared values, industry, desire, problem, and trend, with a note that a growing and affluent segment is "like a cheat code" (Chapter 2, pp. 46–47). Rory Vaden is quoted: a person is "most powerfully positioned to serve the person you once were" (Chapter 2, pp. 47–48). Where that advantage is absent, the "go undercover" method is offered: find where the market congregates online (Facebook Groups, X, Reddit, LinkedIn, Discord, older forums, Hacker News), read its podcasts, books, and YouTube channels while allowing for the "airbrushed version" people give in interviews, subscribe to its trade journals and study who advertises in them, and above all attend its in-person conferences, which the author calls his best hack — a day there beating six months of online research (Chapter 2, pp. 48–50). The chapter closes on market selection as the lever on price: the same skill sold to a different market earns "many more zeros", illustrated by a story-content specialist who moved to pre-IPO companies and took equity (Chapter 2, pp. 50–51).

### Chapter 3, What Are You (Really) Selling? (pp. 53–74)

Product-market fit is introduced through the genie and the starving crowd, and through Marc Andreessen's line that "the market pulls product out of the startup", which the author ties to customer pull in lean (Chapter 3, p. 54). Most failures "die of starvation, not murder", and a claim of having no competitors is "a huge red flag indicating low demand" (Chapter 3, p. 54). Starbucks, McDonald's, and Apple are named as businesses built on demand-side pull rather than on being best (Chapter 3, p. 54). Scott Adams's "moist robots" is borrowed for the predictability of human behavior (Chapter 3, p. 55). The Five Whys, taken from the Toyota Production System, is demonstrated on a factory line and then on a car purchase that resolves to status (Chapter 3, pp. 56–57). The seven core commodities follow, presented as "the only things people really buy or sell": money and wealth; time and convenience; sex and mating; status, fame, and approval; safety, peace of mind, and basic needs; leisure, entertainment, and play; and freedom (Chapter 3, pp. 57–61). Each gets a diagnostic question and a short argument; convenience is supported with Evan Williams's "Convenience decides everything" (Chapter 3, p. 59); the author notes the categories are not clean, that one purchase can be multidimensional, and that the commodities trade against each other (Chapter 3, p. 61). Focus groups and surveys are distrusted because "people lie to you and to themselves" (Chapter 3, p. 57). Vitamins versus painkillers: the brain is wired for survival, so an optional future benefit sells hard and immediate pain relief sells easily; crucially, not determined "by your product but by your market" (Chapter 3, p. 62). The product is "just a delivery mechanism" for the commodities, "the gelatin capsule that holds the active ingredients" (Chapter 3, p. 62). The author's own pivot is given as the worked case: coaching clients failed to execute, so a marketing coordinator was prescribed, then hiring stalled, so the firm added a recruitment service and clients "started getting massive results" (Chapter 3, p. 63). "When the main thing isn't the main thing" argues that the deciding factor is often peripheral — supercar owners buying the engine note, health-conscious travelers choosing a hotel on its gym (Chapter 3, pp. 64–65). Wealth is argued to come mostly from doing "common things uncommonly well" in boring industries over five to twenty years, and from reducing friction: online ordering, integration with a customer's logistics, delivery, rental instead of ownership, customizing a commodity or commoditizing a custom item (Chapter 3, pp. 65–66). Four value levers are then named — time, effort, risk, and side effects — where risk splits into supplier risk (answered with reviews, case studies, testimonials, and guarantees) and customer risk (the prospect's self-doubt about their own part), and "perception is reality, and perceived value is value" (Chapter 3, pp. 66–68). Positioning is argued from a specialist doctor who ran forty minutes late and was rude and was still paid an eye-watering fee; the middle of the market is "the worst place to be", and positioning as cheapest is advised against because it attracts customers who "complain the most, pay late, and need the most support" (Chapter 3, pp. 68–70). Price is presented as a signal of quality and of status, with a Rolls-Royce at Hyundai prices losing its appeal and its price being "a feature", and with costly signaling in gazelles and peacocks as the analogy (Chapter 3, pp. 70–71). The utility-signaling spectrum runs from a $5 T-shirt to a $200 designer one, taking in a prestigious degree and a never-used private tennis court, with Tiffany's diamonds and a paper clip priced at $165 (and $1,500 in gold) as the limit case, and the warning that a competitor cannot simply copy the markup (Chapter 3, p. 71). Cost-plus pricing is named as a common mistake against value-based pricing, on the grounds that "customers don't care about your cost structure" is the reason given (Chapter 3, p. 72). The velvet rope closes the chapter: an exclusive experience built from visual elements, jargon, or insider rituals, with iPhone blue bubbles, a college sweater, golf's "breaking 80", the Jeep Wrangler wave, and named superfan groups as examples (Chapter 3, pp. 72–73).

### Force Multiplier 1: Tools (pp. 76–78) and Chapter 4, Your Marketing Nerve Center (pp. 80–90)

The part opens on a flat-packed standing desk and a handyman's power drill, then on combine harvesters as leverage for farmers, and states Principle 4 (Force Multiplier 1 intro, p. 76). Tools are "the best employee you'll ever have" and "automation does for your time what compound interest does for your money", but the author warns against being "faked out" by them: Michelangelo's hammer and chisel do not make the statue, and most tools augment rather than automate (Force Multiplier 1 intro, p. 77). *Jidoka*, or autonomation, "automation with a human touch", is offered as the model, illustrated by Tony Stark's suit (Force Multiplier 1 intro, p. 77). Technology's second job is removing friction, and the counter-example is the business that adds it — the telephone voice assistant, the unnavigable website (Force Multiplier 1 intro, p. 78).

Chapter 4 makes the CRM system "the beating heart of your marketing infrastructure" and complains that most businesses use it "purely as a passive database" (Chapter 4, p. 81). Three classes are distinguished — marketing automation, sales management, and operational — with overlap expected, and the best CRM system is named as "the one that you'll actually use" (Chapter 4, p. 81). Five functions are required of the marketing one: storing customer data, tagging and segmentation, triggering automations, sending broadcast messages, and reporting; a system may be stronger in one than another and should be chosen on which strengths match the requirement (Chapter 4, pp. 81–82). "Message" is defined broadly to include email, text, push notification, voice message, and physical mail (Chapter 4, p. 82). Stored data runs from name and email to address, phone, location, notes, dates, interests, and actions, with custom fields (a florist's "Partner's Name") and automatic web-page tracking, hedged with "we don't want to be overly creepy in our use of data" (Chapter 4, pp. 82–83). Tagging is worked through three contacts of a realtor in "the idyllic town of Fakeville" and three listings, showing which message each should and should not receive; irrelevant messages "create marketing 'waste'", and the rule of thumb is "to over-tag rather than under-tag" (Chapter 4, pp. 83–84). Automations are triggered by behavior, elapsed time, tags, and "an almost unlimited number of variables", with a welcome sequence and an abandoned-cart email as the worked examples (Chapter 4, pp. 84–85). Broadcasts carry perishable content — a Black Friday campaign across postcard, email, and text — while evergreen content rides on automations (Chapter 4, pp. 85–86). Reporting covers opens, clicks, and purchases, and can extend to lead scoring that itself triggers automations (Chapter 4, p. 86). The sales-management CRM assigns and tracks each salesperson's activity and may record calls (Chapter 4, pp. 86–87). The operational CRM varies by industry: project management for consulting, a helpdesk or job-management system for break-and-fix trades, the e-commerce platform for retail, and an industry-specific system where workflows are regulated, with medical practice-management systems storing X-rays, appointments, and clinical notes named as the example (Chapter 4, pp. 87–88). Whatever the industry, it should hold contact details, notes on interactions, files, regulatory compliance, and workflow status (Chapter 4, p. 88). The three are mapped onto the customer journey — marketing automation at awareness, both at nurturing, sales at conversion, operational at delivery — and tied together natively or with third-party workflow-automation tools (Chapter 4, pp. 88–89). All-in-one systems are "unusual for them to do all these functions well", and building a custom platform is advised against without "a huge budget, an in-house it department, and a very high tolerance for pain", on lock-in and white-elephant grounds (Chapter 4, p. 89).

### Chapter 5, Programming Moist Robots (pp. 92–110)

copywriting is called "the master skill of marketing", under marketing as "the master skill of business" (Chapter 5, p. 93). Ten copywriting commandments follow. **1, entertain.** People "don't have short attention spans, they have short boredom spans", so the cardinal rule is "don't be boring"; content is "infotainment", and Joseph Sugarman's greased-slide passage from *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook* is quoted at length, where each element's only job is to make the reader read the next one. Entertaining does not mean funny; two business-insurance headlines are contrasted (Chapter 5, pp. 94–95). **2, a confused mind says no.** Write the way you talk (a police statement rewritten in plain speech); be intentional, because "the message sent is not always the same as the message received"; write simple sentences; cut ("Old MacDonald" rewritten to one line) toward a high signal-to-noise ratio; use one- and two-syllable words a twelve-year-old would follow; break text up visually; write drunk and edit sober; and have the computer read the text back, because reading one's own writing means reading what one meant to write (Chapter 5, pp. 96–98). **3, headlines.** David Ogilvy is quoted that five times as many people read the headline as the body copy and that writing it spends "80 cents out of your dollar"; the advice is to spend a quarter of the writing time on headlines and aim for fifty variations, and Betteridge's law of headlines is cited as the reason the question form is both effective and "a little sneaky" (Chapter 5, pp. 98–99). **4, name it and claim it.** Stage names, "vegan leather", Blue Ribbon Sports becoming Nike, KFC; the author names his own frameworks as his best-known brands (Chapter 5, pp. 99–100). **5, ask.** Avianca Flight 52, which ran out of fuel 20 miles short of JFK on January 25, 1990 because the crew never used the words controllers are trained to respond to, is the extended example for timidity in a call to action; "awareness is a good start, but action is what you really want" (Chapter 5, pp. 100–101). **6, emotion commits the crime, logic does the cover-up.** Buyers decide emotionally and justify logically, including in business: the reader is "selling h2h: human-to-human", with "nobody ever got fired for buying ibm" as the case, and the observation that in enterprise sales presenting as the safe option beats presenting as the best one. Numbers are a "copywriting danger zone" and should be made to mean something — "1,000 songs in your pocket" rather than five gigabytes (Chapter 5, pp. 102–104). **7, write before you write.** A story bank, a content bank, a swipe file, a snippets file, and a "made me buy" file, with the downside that passive reading becomes impossible (Chapter 5, pp. 104–105). **8, tell stories.** A two-step framework learned from Vinh Giang — the incident, relived with visual, auditory, kinesthetic, and smell detail rather than reported, then the point, kept to the end so any story can be attached to any argument — plus analogies, metaphors, and similes as the smaller weapon (Chapter 5, pp. 105–106). **9, a dual readership path.** Regular subheadings serve readers and skimmers at once, and chapter markers do the same for audio and video (Chapter 5, pp. 106–107). **10, summarize before and after**, which is what the book's own chapter summaries and action items are doing (Chapter 5, p. 107). *The Elements of Style* and Stephen King's *On Writing* are recommended for more (Chapter 5, p. 107). The chapter closes with the Magnetic Messaging Framework, seven filters for short-form copy: is it about them; is it easy to understand; is it believable (answered with proof — "94 percent of first-time buyers become repeat customers" rather than "the best customer service in the industry"); is it interesting or unique; is it "the good thing without the bad thing"; is it clear who it's for, for which a "flag" before the headline is offered ("Dentists in California: …"); and is the next action clear (Chapter 5, pp. 108–110).

### Chapter 6, Artificial Intelligence (pp. 112–119)

Every technology wave runs hype, then panic, then mass adoption, and the printing press drew the same complaints as the internet and AI (Chapter 6, p. 113). Hans Rosling's *Factfulness* instincts — negativity, fear, single perspective — are offered as the reason people misjudge the wave; marketers exploit them and entrepreneurs should beware them (Chapter 6, pp. 113–114). The author's own forecast is optimistic and explicitly so, including that superintelligence is "inevitable" (Chapter 6, p. 114). Andreessen's "software is eating the world" is cited, and the claim is that process-driven work goes first, white-collar as well as blue-collar, leaving creative work; AI "won't take your job", but "someone using ai will" (Chapter 6, pp. 114–115). Disruption is argued to create as well as destroy, with David Autor's finding that 60 percent of 2018 employment was in jobs that did not exist in 1940 (Chapter 6, p. 115). The extended historical case is recorded sound in cinemas: a union campaign in 1930 costing the equivalent of $10 million against recorded music, roughly 24,000 theater musicians employed in 1927, 30 percent of them out of work by 1930 and 50–75 percent in some markets — and yet the live music industry ending up "bigger than ever" (Chapter 6, p. 116). Applied to marketing, volume-based manual work is disappearing and the marketer's work moves into the value stream (Chapter 6, p. 117). Large language models are explained as next-word predictors with a "temperature" setting, which is offered as the reason their default output is generic: "I doubt" they will ever produce "great writing", but as "mediocre writers" they make "excellent writing assistants", used for research, summarization, copy editing, refining, and brainstorming (Chapter 6, pp. 117–118). The closing claim is that because these tools take natural language, "writers are essentially the new software engineers" is the closing line (Chapter 6, p. 118).

### Force Multiplier 2: Assets (pp. 121–122) and Chapter 7, Your Brand (Start with Buy) (pp. 124–132)

The part opens on the author's childhood, *Rich Dad Poor Dad*, and the definition of an asset as "something that puts money in your pocket whether you work or not"; the wealthy earn from assets rather than labor, and labor income "is not scalable, it's not salable, and your earnings stop when you stop" (Force Multiplier 2 intro, p. 121). Principle 5 follows, with the hunting-versus-farming distinction: weak assets force cold calling and constant prospecting, and "when you stop, the lead flow stops"; a high customer acquisition cost is named as a symptom of the same thing (Force Multiplier 2 intro, p. 122). Marketing is reframed from expense to capital investment through an oil well that is upgraded rather than worked harder (Force Multiplier 2 intro, p. 122).

Chapter 7 opens against "follow your passion", "start with why", and core-values workshops. Financial success "fuels passion far more often than the reverse"; the screw manufacturer did not start out passionate about screws; "passion, like profit, is a result", and the better mantra is "love what you do" (Chapter 7, pp. 125–126). Somerset Maugham's inspiration striking "every morning at nine o'clock sharp" is the professional's model (Chapter 7, p. 126). About 99 percent of the work of building a business around a passion has nothing to do with the passion (Chapter 7, p. 127). Simon Sinek's "Start with Why" is attacked directly: every example of it looks to the author "much more like revisionist history than deliberate strategy", the "why" can be changed at a one-day offsite while what you sell and to whom cannot, and "only your mother and best friend buy your stuff because of 'why you do it'". A list of what an entrepreneur already does — creates jobs, wealth, a good life, tax revenue, value it takes only a fraction of — precedes the line "the most noble purpose of your business is to make a profit", with the concession that a charity or social enterprise is a different case; a for-profit business is told to "start with buy, not why" (Chapter 7, pp. 127–128). A brand is then defined with the Feynman Rule — jargon signals not understanding — as "the personality of a business", of which colors, fonts, and logos are only how it dresses (Chapter 7, p. 129). Maya Angelou's line on how you made them feel is quoted, with the note that evoking feeling through design and ads is "difficult, expensive, and often has limited cut-through" (Chapter 7, p. 129). Nike's, Coca-Cola's, and Apple's advertising is argued to be "a result of their sales success, not the cause of it", each having begun with the founder selling by hand, which is Principle 6 (Chapter 7, pp. 129–130). Brand purpose is dismissed via accounting: under GAAP and IFRS a self-created brand cannot go on the balance sheet, but a purchased one appears as goodwill, so a brand's "only one purpose" is "to charge a premium above the intrinsic value of what you do", and without that premium "you don't have a brand" (Chapter 7, pp. 130–131). Brands come from repeated "deposits of goodwill"; hype, scams, and pressure overdraw the account, and the only way to compensate is discounting (Chapter 7, p. 131).

### Chapter 8, Your Flagship Asset (pp. 134–143)

A flagship asset helps the target market get "a result in advance of them buying from you" rather than promising help after purchase (Chapter 8, p. 135). It is distinguished from a lead magnet, which is narrow and campaign-specific; the flagship asset is "your primary broad-spectrum lead magnet", usually free, and its four goals are to demonstrate help by helping, to crystallize uniqueness, to turn invisible prospects into visible ones, and to build goodwill whether or not the person buys (Chapter 8, p. 135). It is centered on a "big idea" that makes the market think "I want that", and takes one of three forms. **Content**: the Michelin Guide, launched by the Michelin brothers in 1900, is the archetype and a win-win, since a tire company profits from people driving more; for service businesses it is usually the do-it-yourself version of the work. The objection that giving away the secrets cannibalizes revenue is rejected — "I've never seen a single instance" of that — with the analogy that the more airplay a song gets, the more concert tickets sell, and the advice to "save the best for first". A nonfiction book is called "the gold standard" and "the nuclear weapon of business cards". Other forms named are how-to guides tied to a tool, awards and leaderboards (the Michelin Guide, Forbes 30 Under 30, the Billboard Hot 100, the Inc. 5000), white papers and research reports (Akamai's State of the Internet reports), and structured data reports where pricing is opaque (a realtor's 90-day sale report, the Rapaport Price List in diamonds) (Chapter 8, pp. 136–138). **Experiences**: Red Bull's extreme sports, a signature keynote delivered for decades, the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, TechCrunch Disrupt, the Victoria's Secret Fashion Show — with the caution that this is an advanced strategy at risk of becoming an unmeasurable brand exercise, and that a taster, trial, or sample is usually the better early spend (Chapter 8, pp. 138–139). **Tools**: Buckminster Fuller is quoted that a tool leads to new ways of thinking; the Love Language Quiz, scorecards ("collecting data about your prospect is a feature rather than a bug"), Google Analytics as free tooling that both feeds Google data and qualifies ad buyers, HubSpot's Website Grader, free color-palette and logo generators, and the author's own 1-Page Marketing Plan canvas, without which he says the book "wouldn't have been anywhere near as successful" (Chapter 8, pp. 139–140). The tripwire argument follows: some prospects are listable (personal injury lawyers in Texas) and some are invisible, and a download titled "The Diabetic's Guide to Continuous Glucose Monitoring" identifies the invisible ones at the right moment in the buying cycle (Chapter 8, pp. 140–141). Two named mistakes: using the asset to brag or as "a useless teaser", and demanding a lot of information or a sales call before handing it over — "capture the minimal viable data possible", and treat it "like a public service" (Chapter 8, p. 141). Thinking upstream is the design method: the prospect moves from A to B to C, not A to Z, so the asset raises problem awareness and genuinely helps on the way (Chapter 8, p. 142).

### Chapter 9, Your Website (pp. 145–156)

Most websites are "just online brochures" with stock photography, unsubstantiated leadership claims, and a weak call to action, and the fix starts with naming the site's one job as if it were an employee — revenue or average order value for e-commerce, calls or form fills for a franchise, bookings in medicine, and for most businesses capturing email addresses into the CRM (Chapter 9, p. 146). The arithmetic given is that "only a tiny percentage, typically about 3 percent, are ready to buy today", so a "contact us" page loses the other 97 percent (Chapter 9, p. 146). An email address is valuable to the business and feels risky to the visitor, so an exchange of value is required (Chapter 9, p. 147). Content upgrades — checklists, worksheets, extra tips, interviews or case studies, alternate media, discount codes — are recommended over generic lead magnets because they are tied to the page already being read, and are delivered automatically by the CRM (Chapter 9, pp. 147–148). Landing pages are defined as binary: fill it out or leave, with navigation bars and pop-ups removed, a strong headline, fast load, and only essential fields; "each additional piece of information" requested "will reduce your overall completion rates", with a multi-step form that takes the email first as the workaround, and dedicated tooling for split testing (Chapter 9, pp. 148–149). The hero section gets a three-part structure: "Here's what I've got", "Here's how it makes your life better" (one sentence, two at most), and "Here's what I want you to do next" — worked on a caffeine-infused protein bar with a primary call to action to buy and a secondary one for a free sample. A jargon-filled real home page ("Solutions for Your Long-Term Goals", "core values of passion, integrity, and creativity") is the counter-example, and "clarity is king" the rule (Chapter 9, pp. 149–151). Further hero advice: a brighter color for the primary button, imagery of people achieving the outcome with their gaze directed at the button, no auto-playing video, animation, or carousels, and social proof (Chapter 9, p. 151). The About page is the second most visited; the visitor's four reasons for being there are listed, and the common mistake is making it about the business — "No one cares that your grandfather started the business in 1974" unless that fact is relevant to the market — so the page leads with the most compelling thing for the prospect, focuses on transformation over qualifications, uses real photos, and ends with a call to action (Chapter 9, pp. 151–152). Other pages are inventoried: products and services with pricing (or a price range if custom), a contact page whose form routes to the right system, sales pages, genuine FAQs, a "wall of love", a content hub, compliance pages, and live chat where it can be staffed (Chapter 9, pp. 152–153). On domains, .com is "the mack daddy" and the default for US or global audiences, with a prepended or appended word as a stopgap, dashes and unusual characters avoided for phonetic ambiguity, defensive registration of .net, .org, and .co, and local extensions for local markets; the author owns over 300 domains (Chapter 9, p. 154). Seven design and user-experience elements are required even when hiring a developer: navigable UX watched across ages and abilities with heat-mapping, mobile responsiveness, load speed monitored over time, on-page SEO, SSL and an updated CMS, analytics, and professional photography instead of stock (Chapter 9, pp. 155–156).

### Chapter 10, Your Intellectual Property (pp. 158–168)

Intellectual property changes how a business is valued in an acquisition: without it the conversation is earnings multiples, with it the conversation is what the acquirer could do with the assets, which the author says let him sell companies "for far more than they were worth on an earnings multiple basis" (Chapter 10, p. 159). Naming comes first, because the name "is difficult and expensive to change": be clear rather than clever, "if you're explaining, you're losing", and check the domain, the social usernames, trademarkability, infringement, and phonetic obviousness (Chapter 10, pp. 159–160). Word selection runs descriptive (clear but hard to trademark), suggestive (the recommended middle ground), arbitrary (distinctive but advised against in an age of search engines), invented (easiest to trademark, costly to explain), and foreign-language, each with examples (Chapter 10, pp. 160–161). Construction runs singles, multiples (with a bonus for alliteration), compounds, misspellings, and acronyms (Chapter 10, pp. 161–162). A trademark attorney may be needed, and phonetic ambiguity is costed concretely: a podcast ad spent spelling a name is airtime not spent selling, and it adds friction to the call to action — Lyft's trade-off is named as deliberate, 7-Eleven's as something a company founded in the internet era would likely have avoided (Chapter 10, pp. 162–163). Standard operating procedures are the next asset: step-by-step instructions, "detailed recipes", often faster to make as screen recordings with voiceover than to write, kept centrally, updated periodically, and written by the people who do the work, since "the theory and practice diverge so much" when written for someone else (Chapter 10, pp. 163–164). David Jenyns's *SYSTEMology* is recommended for depth. SOPs and systems buy "the three E's of entrepreneurial freedom" — Expansion, Escape, and Exit — where exit value matters even to an owner who never sells, "because it gives you options" (Chapter 10, p. 164). Trademarks and patents are treated as barriers to entry that grow in importance with size; the author reports takedowns being "a part-time job for a person on my team" and says trademarking made them easier. The smiley face is the licensing example: Harvey Ball created it in 1963 in about ten minutes and did not trademark it, Franklin Loufrani did, and the mark "has now generated over $500 million in licensing fees" (Chapter 10, pp. 164–165). Early-stage businesses are told to keep filings minimal until traction, because spending on them while neglecting product-market fit is "success theater" (Chapter 10, p. 165). Style guides come next, both design (color palette, fonts, imagery, icons, logo usage) and the less common copywriting style guide (formal or relaxed, slang, humor, opinions, email formatting, memes), on the ground that inconsistency reads as untrustworthiness — "someone who behaves unpredictably feels untrustworthy, perhaps even dangerous" — and that mismatch between an ultra-luxury product and a homemade-looking site creates cognitive dissonance (Chapter 10, pp. 165–166). The chapter closes on monetizing by-products, with Amazon Web Services and Fulfillment by Amazon as the cases and the author's own certification and train-the-trainer programs as his, framed as turning cost centers into profit centers and inputs into capital investments (Chapter 10, pp. 166–167).

### Force Multiplier 3: Processes (pp. 170–173) and Chapter 11, Business Is a Team Sport (pp. 175–190)

The part opens on the author staying up past midnight in a YouTube rabbit hole and concludes "because I relied on waning willpower". Goal-setting methods are rejected for the same reason; the replacement is "loose goals, tight systems", quoting James Clear that "the more disciplined your environment is, the less disciplined you need to be", and tied to *kaizen*. People do what they do "because they're already doing it", and "inertia explains almost all behavior-driven results" Goals win once; systems win repeatedly (Force Multiplier 3 intro, p. 170). Compound interest follows, with the worked figure that $50 a week for 50 years deposits $130,000 and, at 8 percent compounded monthly, ends at $1,718,541, and with the S&P 500's "inflation-adjusted annual return of about 8 percent" as the basis. Principle 7 follows: early marketing feels disappointing for the same reason compounding does, and "you can't produce a baby in one month by getting nine women pregnant" (Force Multiplier 3 intro, pp. 171–172). The part closes on the claim that prospects research a considered purchase and hold a conversation about the business whether or not it is present — "conversations lead to conversions" (Force Multiplier 3 intro, pp. 172–173).

Chapter 11 names "Superman Syndrome" and lists its Kryptonite: nobody to keep the founder accountable, constant interruptions, no sense of priority, expensive trial and error, opportunity cost, and inability to disconnect — a condition of playing "in single-player mode" that is unrelated to team size (Chapter 11, pp. 176–177). Strengths are to be strengthened: the author's swimming and sailing failures against how easily programming came to him, and the conclusion that it takes "the same effort" to make a weakness "slightly less weak" as to make a strength "exceptional" (Chapter 11, pp. 177–178). Weaknesses are staffed, using Gino Wickman's visionary and integrator roles from *Traction*; most founders are visionaries and need an integrator to run the marketing infrastructure (Chapter 11, pp. 178–179). The generalist marketing agency is then attacked at length: outsourcing non-core functions is smart but "usually disastrous when applied to core activities like marketing", because Peter Drucker put marketing and innovation as the only two business functions; outsourcing marketing is "like hiring someone to treat your spouse well" to have a good marriage. Three specific problems are named — divided attention across hundreds of clients, commission on ad spend as a misaligned incentive, and the fact that "you're building their intellectual property and capability—not yours". Specialist agencies and freelancers are exempted and praised, but routine execution — most copy, content, and running the infrastructure — is to be in-house, and the strategy is the entrepreneur's own responsibility (Chapter 11, pp. 179–181). On the first marketing hire, a "top-gun" marketer or CMO is "probably not" it: their experience is managing teams and vendors, and the biggest risk is that someone from a large company produces "safe", institutional marketing "sanitized and frankensteined by various committees". A marketing coordinator focused on execution is recommended instead, hired on attitude rather than experience — "don't hire—invest" — because "technical skills can be learned" and attitude "generally can't be", and in keeping with lean manufacturing's preference for smaller general-purpose machines over large specialized ones (Chapter 11, pp. 181–182). Three skills are wanted in the coordinator: the ability to write clearly (and to be a native speaker of the target market's region, because because it is "obvious when a foreigner tries to pass off as a local"), comfort with technology, and leadership potential, since growing existing team members beats bringing strangers in over them (Chapter 11, p. 183). Remote work is endorsed, with the author's team spread across seven named countries and the claim that "the best person for the job" need not be "in your zip code" (Chapter 11, pp. 183–184). A-players only: Netflix's memo is quoted — "We model ourselves on being a professional sports team, not a family" — and the argument is that B- and C-players cost more in supervision than they save in salary and "slow down, demotivate, and drag down your A-players", that an organization "will always bottleneck with your weakest team members", and that "you get the standards you tolerate" is the summary. A-players "come with 'batteries included'" and seek roles they are not yet qualified for (Chapter 11, pp. 184–185). Retention is framed as being "a magnet, not a jail", answering "what if I train them and they leave?" with "what if you don't train them and they stay?", and extending to helping a departing employee leave well (Chapter 11, pp. 185–186). OKRs are rejected — they "work flawlessly in theory but falter in practice" and invite consultants — in favor of Peter Thiel's rule at PayPal that each employee has one thing and is evaluated only on that, because with three priorities "most will gravitate towards the less valuable tasks with clearer paths to success". Two objections are answered: people do need to do multiple things, but one thing usually subsumes the others; and unintended consequences are real, illustrated with the Delhi cobra bounty that led people to breed cobras, so the metric should be "easy to measure and hard to game" (Chapter 11, pp. 186–187). The marketing system is then laid out as a cron-like "What, When, Who" table, with a worked example of five recurring and event-triggered tasks and a named owner for each — including for tasks the CRM fully automates, because "even automated systems can have malfunctions", and someone must be responsible; SOPs carry the how (Chapter 11, pp. 187–189). The chapter closes with the "dead man's switch": content sits in the author's review queue and, with "no response from me after 48 hours" they "automatically proceed to be published". The stated precondition is that this only works because of detailed SOPs, style guides, and training — work "way off the mark" would make the system unviable (Chapter 11, pp. 189–190).

### Chapter 12, Email Marketing (pp. 192–207)

Email is defended against the recurring "email is dead" article: it has survived spam and platform shifts, internal chatter has moved to Slack and Teams so inboxes now carry outside mail, spam filters are better, and the Lindy Effect says a fifty-year-old technology has another fifty in it (Chapter 12, p. 193). Four challenges structure the chapter: delivered, opened, read, actioned. **Delivered** depends on technical configuration, sender reputation, and content. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are each defined, with the note that most CRM systems support them out of the box but setup needs technical help (Chapter 12, p. 194). Reputation is influenced by spam reports, frequency and volume, and engagement patterns; content is scanned for spammy language, too many links, URL shorteners, attachments, image use, keyword stuffing, and lack of personalization (Chapter 12, pp. 194–195). **Opened** depends on five things the recipient sees: sender name, subject line, avatar, preheader, and sender email address — and on that last, the author "highly recommend[s] avoiding role-based usernames", with `noreply@` the "worst of all" and "a massive missed opportunity". Send timing is a sixth factor (Chapter 12, pp. 196–197). **Read**: one topic per email, because "if you pack too much in one email, your message will get lost" and a chess player does not reveal five moves at once; personalization from CRM data; formatting and mobile rendering. The recommendation is plainly formatted, personal-looking email sent from an individual's name and address rather than a company's, because "both spam filters and humans" recognize "the difference between personal and promotional emails", with e-commerce the exception (Chapter 12, pp. 197–198). **Actioned**: one call to action per email, possibly repeated. Asking for a reply is called "a highly underrated response mechanism", for four reasons — no application switch, safer than a link given malware fears, turning a broadcast medium into a conversational one, and whitelisting that improves future deliverability — with the caveat that replies must be answered, routed to a shared inbox or helpdesk, and possibly treated as warm leads (Chapter 12, pp. 198–199). On frequency the answer is "somewhere between once a week and twice a day", with once a week the recommended minimum, because below that "your email list decaying" produces unsubscribes and spam complaints when people forget they opted in: "All relationships deteriorate without regular contact". The unsubscribe worry is answered with "someone unwilling to give you their attention likely won't give you their money either" and and a list that is never emailed produces nothing A massage-clinic client is the example of emails that are mostly not about the business, functioning "essentially a public service" with the service mentioned in passing (Chapter 12, pp. 199–201). Three email types make the system: short-term welcome sequences (which fulfill the opt-in promise, start a relevant conversation, and segment based on the answer, worked through a presentation-skills trainer), broadcasts for perishable news, and long-term evergreen sequences (Chapter 12, pp. 201–202). Five anti-spam practices follow — consent through opt-in with a note on soft opt-in, transparency including business name and postal address in the footer as "usually a legal requirement", relevance, an honored unsubscribe mechanism, and legality, naming CAN-SPAM and GDPR with "if in doubt, get legal advice" (Chapter 12, pp. 202–203). The soap opera sequence is a narrative launch sequence in three movements — the introduction that establishes character and problem, the deep dive with a new twist per email, and the solution — each ending on a cliffhanger, worked through a fertility expert, with the instruction that it it "should be built on truth" and is not to be used "to deceive", and the test of whether it "provides value even if they don't buy" (Chapter 12, pp. 203–205). The chapter closes on the "super signature", credited to Dean Jackson: a postscript at the foot of value-building emails offering a few specific, non-pushy next steps, on the premise that "people hate being sold to, but they love to buy" and that the pool of longer-to-mature buyers is "ten times larger or more" than the 3 percent ready today. The author reproduces his own super signature in full, which signals that he reads replies personally and lists three ways to go further (Chapter 12, pp. 205–207).

### Chapter 13, Content Marketing (pp. 209–227)

The chapter opens on marketing agencies enrolling in the author's programs, and reads it as the fall of technical trickery: keywords, hashtags, backlinks, and clever targeting once produced visibility, and as platforms grew more sophisticated, "the effectiveness of this technical trickery has fallen off a cliff" (Chapter 13, p. 210). The mechanism is that platforms now infer intent from behavior — dwell time, watch percentage, swipes — rather than from likes, follows, and keywords, so "your stories, the way you tell them", and the genuine value created for an audience are "now what matters", and the line between paid and organic has blurred because the best-performing ads do not look like ads; Howard Gossage is quoted that people "read what interests them, and sometimes that's an ad" (Chapter 13, pp. 210–211). Owning versus renting follows: the flagship asset, the email list, and the website are owned, while "on social media, you're a tenant" who can be deplatformed, shadow-banned, or evicted under rules that are "opaque and arbitrary", and even large ad spend does not shield an account. Three further reasons to move a social audience to an owned asset: the loss risk, the distraction — distraction being "the core product of social media" — and ephemerality, against the author's own years-old website, podcast, and email content still producing leads (Chapter 13, pp. 212–213). The social media formula is given deliberately unappealingly: choose a platform, post every day, get a bit better each day, repeat for two to five years, with Warren Buffett's "because nobody wants to get rich slow" as the reason few do. Not only is the marketer a tenant, "the rent is due every day", since platforms boost consistent posting and deprioritize breaks. The apparently effortless account is said to be backed by a team — Dolly Parton's it "costs a lot of money to look this cheap" is applied to it (Chapter 13, pp. 213–214). Being platform native follows, with the historical parallel that early radio was newspapers read aloud and early television was adapted radio; broadcasting identical content everywhere "won't get far" because "the same person consuming the same content will behave differently on LinkedIn than on TikTok". Each platform's dominant subculture is characterized in turn — Instagram, X, LinkedIn, TikTok, Reddit — with the author conceding the caricature and saying he keeps the generalizations in mind anyway; the recommendation is one platform to start, and self-contained pieces, since smarter algorithms will show content to people with no context on the creator (Chapter 13, pp. 214–216). Five content creator archetypes are then named: The Expert (most common; the risk is being boring, since a creator will "never outdo search engines and Wikipedia as sources of information"), The Curator, The Interviewer (a way to borrow authority, with Oprah Winfrey as the model and the challenge of getting past rehearsed answers), The Amateur on a Journey (Tim Ferriss, and the "If I can do it, you can too" narrative), and The Enigma (Gordon Ramsay is offered as an Expert–Enigma hybrid). Imposter syndrome is addressed first: "Real imposters never have imposter syndrome" is the author's answer, expertise is relative to the audience rather than to peers, and Tiger Woods is named as someone who would likely not be the best instructor for a novice golfer (Chapter 13, pp. 216–219). The media-company mindset follows as Principle 8: the author would rather have "one relevant visitor" than "10,000 irrelevant ones", likes are vanity metrics, and content is treated as a product line that lets the market pull (Chapter 13, pp. 219–220). Two challenges are named. Discouragement — early low view counts are "actually good because it's likely your early content creation efforts will suck", and the advice is not to look at the numbers during the rookie phase. And insufficient polarization — "appealing to everybody is appealing to nobody", negative comments indicate it is working, and arguing with them is a loss: "never wrestle a pig, because you'll both get dirty but the pig will enjoy it" (Chapter 13, pp. 220–221). Selling without selling: overt selling is "the fastest way to have your audience swipe away", so the author's instruction is to make the product "a prop that's incidental to the content", like an Omega watch in a Bond film rather than a call to action to buy one. The power-tools example builds a community of hobbyists around demonstrations and tutorials with the product in frame but never the subject, and the same discipline applies to the founder's own role in the community — people go to church for the shared philosophy, not the priest (Chapter 13, pp. 221–222). Endurance gets two strategies: Gary Vaynerchuk's "Document, don't create", where behind-the-scenes footage of work already happening is both easiest to make and, in the author's experience, the biggest hit; and working in the modality one creates most naturally in, which may differ from the one used for consuming (Chapter 13, pp. 222–223). A history of paid digital advertising closes the chapter: cheap clicks arbitraged for profit, then an escalating bidding war in which "the craziest guy was the last one standing, but the real winner was the arms dealer", then a second arms race in data and tracking, then a consumer and government backlash that removed targeting, tracking, and attribution capabilities. The conclusion is that digital advertising is now digital advertising is now "complementary" to organic content and "not the instantaneous solution to lead flow that it once was" (Chapter 13, pp. 223–225). Four best practices are named as durable: work with platform experts, always be testing (with ad fatigue as the further reason to rotate creative), use retargeting, and break even on the front end, which "allows you to have an effectively unlimited marketing budget" because growth is not bottlenecked by cash flow, with lifetime value coming from the back end (Chapter 13, pp. 225–227). Technical how-tos are again sent to the Hub, because ad platform policies "change almost daily" (Chapter 13, p. 225).

### Chapter 14, Keeping, Delighting, and Multiplying Your Customers (pp. 229–249)

"Customers for life" is the framing mantra: goodwill deposits build the brand, raving fans are worth more than their own lifetime value because they refer, and customers leave less for something done wrong than for having no "reason to stay". The claim is that many businesses could "double or even triple your revenue without adding a single new customer" (Chapter 14, pp. 230–231). Onboarding comes first, citing Joey Coleman's *Never Lose a Customer Again* on the first 100 days, with the argument that retention processes start at signup rather than later. The worked example is a SaaS 30-day trial taken with a credit card, never set up because the data import looked daunting, paid for twice without use, then canceled — and the author's own experience that onboarding a signup into actual use is where the wins are. Buyer's remorse is expected regardless of industry, from a rough handover, inflated expectations, or the realization that effort is required (Chapter 14, pp. 231–232). Paul Graham's "do things that don't scale" supplies six practices: manually recruit users, handcraft the experience, work closely with early customers (the Airbnb founders photographing listings in New York), do things bigger competitors will not, firefight manually, and keep a personal connection as the business grows. The counter to the scalability objection is that without mastering onboarding a business "won't ever have to worry about scale" (Chapter 14, pp. 232–233). Returning to the front line — a day a month or a quarter inside customer service or the helpdesk — is prescribed because secondhand reporting is "aggregated, massaged, and sanitized"; what it surfaces is recurring issues, gaps in messaging, and stopgap fixes, and each customer found is happy (ask for a testimonial), unhappy (fix it before it becomes a review), or neutral, and lots of neutral and unhappy ones may indicate a product-market fit problem (Chapter 14, pp. 233–234). Recovery is argued to beat flawless delivery: being heard and having a complaint fixed thoroughly "not only repaired the relationship but strengthened it", with the disclaimer "I'm not suggesting that you purposely create problems to fix them" (Chapter 14, pp. 234–235). "Fix it twice" follows from the Five Whys: resolve the immediate problem and then fix the systemic reason it could happen, with the clarification that fixing twice does not mean "the first fix should be quick and sloppy" (Chapter 14, pp. 235–236). Proof is the next section. Claims of being faster, better, and lower risk are made by everyone and cannot be verified before purchase, so differentiation runs on "overwhelming proof… in terms of both quantity and quality". Seven sources of social proof are listed: reviews and ratings, testimonials from people in the prospect's situation, quotes from authorities, well-known customers, awards, celebrity endorsements, and measurement — backing a claim with a number such as average resolution time or a net promoter score. A "wall of love" with dozens or hundreds of testimonials is contrasted with a few vague anonymous quotes, and a physical version is suggested for a business with premises. For a business with no proof yet, two honest routes are given — give it "away for free to a select group" in the target market "and ask for an honest review in exchange", or ask a past colleague for a character testimonial — prefaced with the author's "first piece of advice" is "not to be dishonest and to make it up" (Chapter 14, pp. 236–238). Collection is to be process-driven and partly automated, and made frictionless: video testimonials carry real technical obstacles, and customers get stuck on what to say, so samples should be supplied. The reframing device is to ask for "feedback" rather than a "testimonial", then ask four questions — why they were skeptical, what decided them, what benefits they got, and who they would recommend it to — and afterward ask permission to use the answers; recording a call and editing it down is the frictionless video route (Chapter 14, pp. 238–239). Referrals get three methods. **Ask**, on the observation that "high-status, powerful people tend to be the most prolific askers", with Steve Jobs quoted at length on asking for help, and the psychological claim that people do not refer "to do you a favor" but "for themselves", because a good referral raises their standing. Two real emails from the author's inbox are reproduced as a bad request (anonymous, self-focused, logistics offloaded, unpersonalized) and a good one (personalized, clear value proposition, social proof, respectful of time, clear ask, easy next step) (Chapter 14, pp. 239–242). **Make it part of your product**, by setting the expectation during sales or onboarding so the request is not a surprise, and possibly making it reciprocal. Incentives are argued against for informal referrals, because the real payoff is social and "getting a direct payoff with an incentive kills this", and may even lower the referrer's standing when discovered — while formal arrangements such as joint ventures, affiliate programs, and reseller agreements are a different case, with disclosure often a legal requirement in real estate, finance, and healthcare, and incentivized referrals sometimes illegal. Network effects are named as the strongest form (Chapter 14, pp. 243–244). **Arm your referrers** with an asset they can pass on, because a vague "if you know someone…" puts all the work on them, and because most people only refer when they are sure a person is ready — whereas passing on an asset "doesn't create obligation or sales pressure". The author's own practice is giving partners copies of his books; a voucher or gift card mimicking a commercial gift card, a sampler pack, or the flagship asset are the other forms (Chapter 14, pp. 244–245). Gifting follows Cialdini's reciprocity: meaningful, unexpected, and customized, skipping major holidays and skipping logo trinkets, which are "not a gift for your customer" but "an advertisement". John Ruhlin's *Giftology* supplies the rule that the gift be best-in-class for whatever budget exists and carry the recipient's name rather than the company's (Chapter 14, pp. 245–246). "Shock and awe" physical mail is argued from packages having near-100-percent open rates while physical inboxes have emptied as digital ones filled, sometimes making a mailed package cheaper than digital attention; contents may include a handwritten note, materials, social proof, books or reports, samples, and gifts (Chapter 14, pp. 246–247). The chapter closes on the delay problem — customers quitting before the transformation arrives — with three remedies: set clear expectations about how long and how hard it will be (and refuse "desperate money", which is bad ethically, bad for lifetime value, and bad for the team); engineer quick wins, with the caution that customers can get hooked on them; and provide a visual roadmap, used both on sales calls for invisible services and throughout the journey to show "you are here". A telecommunications company's "no news update" every few days during a multi-week provisioning delay is the worked example, where "satisfaction levels dramatically increased as customers felt looped in" (Chapter 14, pp. 247–249).

### Chapter 15, Metrics (pp. 251–260)

"Most people won't" is the opening: most will not click, opt in, open, click through, or complete, and this is normal rather than evidence of a broken product. "I tried it, and it didn't work" is the midwit's phrase, and the lean alternative is the Andon cord — stop the line, find the root cause, fix, restart — which is Principle 9 (Chapter 15, pp. 252–253). Troubleshooting a paid campaign is then reduced to five possibilities: not clicking the ad, not opting in, not getting or opening the email, not reaching the sales page, not buying. These are said to be "the only possibilities", so the useful response is to identify which one (Chapter 15, p. 253). Metrics split into lagging (profit, churn, revenue — there is "nothing much you can do about last month's numbers") and leading, which are early warnings; a daily opt-in count that suddenly falls is the worked example, and the point is made in both directions, since a rising leading metric signals a moment to double down (Chapter 15, pp. 253–254). Lifetime value is named "the metric that matters", because there are "literally only two ways to grow your business" — more customers, or more money from existing ones — and because lifetime value determines what can be spent to acquire, to delight and retain, and "how big of a moat you can create". The common mistake is computing it from revenue rather than profit, which overstates it; the exception is venture-backed businesses focused on growth. A worked calculation follows: $1,000 spent a year, $300 of variable expenses, $700 profit, three years' average tenure, $2,100 lifetime value, with fixed expenses excluded and the figures recalculated regularly and per major segment. Five ways to increase it are listed: raising prices, upselling, ascending to premium tiers, increasing purchase frequency, and reactivating churned customers (Chapter 15, pp. 254–256). Customer acquisition cost follows, defined and worked at $100,000 over 1,000 customers, with the claim that "whoever can afford to spend the most to acquire and retain a customer will win" and that a low lifetime value forces scrimping that "rarely leads to amazing customer experiences" (Chapter 15, p. 256). Return on ad spend and return on investment are distinguished as granular-per-campaign and broad-per-strategy (Chapter 15, pp. 256–257). Subscription metrics get their own short section: monthly recurring revenue and churn rate, with the bucket analogy — MRR is the water, churn is how fast it leaks — and a worked case of 1,000 subscribers at $10 with 2 percent monthly churn needing 20 new customers a month to stand still (Chapter 15, p. 257). Thirteen "micrometrics" are then listed with one-line definitions — conversion rate, click-through rate, cost per click, traffic, bounce rate, engagement rate, lead-to-customer rate, marketing qualified lead, sales qualified lead, net promoter score (defined in full), email open and click rates, average order value, and products per customer — prefaced with the warning that "more fiction gets written in spreadsheets" than in books and that isolated metrics can make any campaign look good (Chapter 15, pp. 257–259). The chapter closes on a dashboard of the handful that matter, expanded only when troubleshooting, and on time horizon: zooming out shows trends and loses detail — it "will tell you that the average zebra is gray" — so both levels are needed (Chapter 15, p. 259).

### Conclusion (pp. 261–263) and About the Author (pp. 264–265)

Marketing "has become bloated", and Dr. Ben Carvosso's "compressing for quality" — doing less, more intentionally — is offered as the summary, with the caveat that it is not the "quality over quantity" cliché, because "reps matter". The author's observed pattern is that the winner is not the best-resourced, the luckiest, or the smartest, since smart people plan to perfection and either never execute or execute at low intensity; hence "be dumb" and execute with intensity, because "every master was once a disaster" is the encouragement (Conclusion, p. 261). The closing instruction is to start immediately, since inspiration is "highly perishable" (Conclusion, p. 262). What was cut from the book is said to be in the Hub, described as an "essentials" album with the B-sides online, and framed as a community against entrepreneurial isolation (Conclusion, p. 262). "Pay it forward" rejects "giving back" as implying a debt, on the grounds that entrepreneurs "create value in the marketplace" and capture "a small portion of it" (Conclusion, p. 263). The author bio, written in the first person because the third "is kind of weird", lists the credentials: top 0.01 percent of nonfiction authors, translation into over 30 languages, multiple exits across IT, telecommunications, and marketing, and reasonable working hours (About the Author, pp. 264–265).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply. The book hedges unusually often, and says so: its opening device is an instruction to hold conflicting ideas without resolving them (Introduction, pp. 9–11, 14).

- **Scope of the book itself.** The foundations chapters may be skipped: a reader with a solid grasp of target market and product-market fit, or who is "impatient to get to the tactical portion", is told to "feel free to skip ahead to Chapter 4" (Chapter 1, p. 27). Everything technical is deliberately absent and lives on a website, because printed screenshots and how-tos "would be obsolete by the time you read them" (Chapter 1, p. 30; Chapter 13, p. 225). Strategy is another book's job (Chapter 1, p. 26).
- **Product-market fit is not to be perfected first.** It is not to become "a point of procrastination and paralysis by analysis"; fit "may be imperfect at first" and will need course correction or a pivot, and "you'll rarely achieve product-market fit on your first try" (Chapter 1, p. 27; Chapter 3, p. 63).
- **Niching.** The author "wouldn't say you can't be too niche", but "when you think you've niched enough, you probably haven't" — the caution is stated and then set aside (Chapter 2, p. 36). The market "doesn't have to be a huge market. It just needs to be the right market for you" (Chapter 2, p. 36).
- **Separate plans per audience.** Whether two audiences need separate marketing plans "depends on whether you truly have different target markets or if they're just segments of an existing market", answered by five questions rather than a rule (Chapter 2, p. 39).
- **The unaware.** They "are usually not worth marketing to", with one path offered anyway for someone with a genuinely unique product who "insist[s] on a life of frustration and pain": echo an emotion or attitude they identify with (Chapter 2, p. 42).
- **Generating demand is possible.** It is "very difficult, very expensive, and takes a very long time", not impossible; Colgate is the counter-case where it worked (Chapter 2, p. 41).
- **The seven core commodities do not partition cleanly.** Desires cannot be "packaged neatly" and many have "multidimensional drivers", and the commodities trade against each other (Chapter 3, p. 61).
- **Vitamin or painkiller is a property of the market, not the product.** A Mercedes-Benz S-Class may read as "a vitamin by one market segment but as a painkiller by another" (Chapter 3, p. 62).
- **Positioning as cheapest.** Advised against, but with a named exception: it "should be done with extreme caution and only if you have some unique input cost advantage" (Chapter 3, p. 69). Premium positioning is not a license for poor service: the author is "by no means suggesting" positioning as "an excuse for poor customer service" (Chapter 3, p. 69).
- **Tools.** Tools may "completely automate many processes", but most "will be used for augmentation rather than complete automation", and technology cannot take over the sales or marketing function (Force Multiplier 1 intro, p. 77). The best CRM "is the one that you'll actually use", and a system may be strong in one function and weak in another, which "is totally normal" (Chapter 4, pp. 81–82). All-in-one systems are a trade-off "you're willing to accept in exchange for the simplicity of having a single system", and the custom-platform route is allowed for larger enterprises with budget, an IT department, and "a very high tolerance for pain" (Chapter 4, p. 89).
- **Entertaining does not mean funny.** Being "inappropriately funny" can detract, and "your job isn't to be a comedian" (Chapter 5, p. 95). Length is not to be capped: emails "should be as long as they need to be while still being engaging" (Chapter 12, p. 197).
- **The magnetic messaging filters are a target, not a checklist.** "It's unlikely that you'll hit every single one in a short-form message" (Chapter 5, p. 108). The ten commandments are "not an exhaustive list" but "80 percent of the results for 20 percent of the effort" (Chapter 5, p. 107).
- **Large language models.** Mediocre writers by design, and "you can't rely on [them] to do the writing for you", yet "they make excellent writing assistants" and are part of the author's daily toolkit (Chapter 6, p. 118). The prediction that they will never produce great writing is hedged as "I doubt" (Chapter 6, p. 117).
- **Passion.** "I'm not against passion. If you have it, more power to you. Use it as fuel", and some people genuinely are born with one, though "they're a minority"; fear, desperation, and revenge are named as fuels that "can be just as effective or even more so" (Chapter 7, pp. 125–127).
- **"Start with why".** Rejected for for-profit businesses, allowed elsewhere: for "a social enterprise or charity" starting with why "could make sense", and a genuine driving why should be used "by all means" (Chapter 7, pp. 127–128).
- **Brand visuals.** Colors, fonts, and logos are "not inconsequential but certainly not their whole personality" (Chapter 7, p. 129), and Chapter 10 returns to say that having cautioned against fixating on the superficial, "congruence and consistency in the way you show up are important" (Chapter 10, p. 165).
- **The flagship asset.** "Typically, your flagship asset is free, but it doesn't have to be" (Chapter 8, p. 135). Flagship experiences are singled out as risky: "For many businesses, this should be considered an advanced strategy", at risk of becoming an expensive brand exercise, so "a better use of resources early on for most smaller businesses" is a taster, trial, or sample (Chapter 8, p. 139). "Most users of the free tools may never become paying customers, many will" (Chapter 8, p. 140), and the lines between flagship content and ongoing content marketing "are blurred" (Chapter 8, p. 138).
- **Landing page fields.** The advice is to collect just the email to start and to test the impact before adding fields; a multi-step form is the compromise when more data is wanted (Chapter 9, p. 149).
- **The .com rule.** ".com" is "not a hard-and-fast rule" — businesses are built on other extensions — but it "should be considered the default", and a local market outside the United States should use its own extension (Chapter 9, p. 154).
- **Naming.** Descriptive names are liked but "difficult to trademark"; invented names are easiest to trademark but "force you to devote a lot of marketing resources"; Lyft's misspelling is named as a trade-off the company "obviously… chose to make" (Chapter 10, pp. 160–162).
- **Trademarks and patents.** At an early stage these filings are "a balancing act", to be kept "to a minimum until you have traction", because spending on them while product-market fit is unsettled is "success theater" (Chapter 10, p. 165).
- **Agencies.** The attack is on generalist agencies and on abdication, not on outside help: specialist agencies are "worth their weight in gold", the author "frequently hire[s] agencies and freelancers", and experts are recommended for setting up digital advertising (Chapter 11, pp. 180–181; Chapter 13, pp. 225–226). Some "top-gun" marketers "worked their way up from practical execution", it is "the pure managers you need to beware of", and an experienced CMO "may be a good hire once you've already got a large marketing team and a lot of traction" (Chapter 11, p. 182).
- **The one-thing metric.** Two objections are raised by the author himself and answered rather than dismissed: people do need to work on multiple things, and single metrics have unintended consequences, so the metric "won't be perfect at first, so you must periodically monitor, calibrate, and correct course" (Chapter 11, pp. 186–187).
- **The dead man's switch has a precondition.** Work "way off the mark" would make it unviable; it works "because of our detailed standard operating procedures, style guides, and team training" (Chapter 11, p. 190).
- **Email format and frequency.** Plain formatting is for "most businesses", with e-commerce the stated exception where product visuals matter (Chapter 12, p. 198). Frequency "depends on your relationship" and lands at "somewhere between once a week and twice a day", varying by industry and news pace — twice daily for a stock-market business, over the top for a doctor (Chapter 12, pp. 199–200). Anti-spam law "var[ies] from country to country", and "if in doubt, get legal advice" (Chapter 12, p. 203).
- **Soap opera sequences must be true.** It "should be built on truth", and no lean marketing tactic is to be used "to deceive" (Chapter 12, p. 204).
- **Platform caricatures.** The author concedes he is "being a bit handwavy" and that there are "genuine people on Instagram, nice people on X, and normal people on LinkedIn" — the author keeps the generalizations anyway (Chapter 13, p. 216).
- **Content creator archetypes combine and change.** A creator can take a hybrid or invent one, and archetypes "can also naturally morph from one to another" (Chapter 13, pp. 216, 219).
- **Cute personal content.** "Cute photos of your dog or random thoughts are ok occasionally", but not as a media channel (Chapter 13, p. 214).
- **Social proof when there is none.** The two honest routes are prefaced with the author's "first piece of advice": "not to be dishonest and to make it up" (Chapter 14, p. 237).
- **Recovery is not an excuse for defects.** "I'm not suggesting that you purposely create problems to fix them", and fixing twice does not mean "the first fix should be quick and sloppy" (Chapter 14, pp. 235–236).
- **Referral incentives.** Argued against for informal referrals on psychological grounds — the author is "not saying referrals don't happen this way", only that it is "much less common than most entrepreneurs think" — while "the situation is completely different when there's a formal referral relationship" such as a joint venture, affiliate program, or reseller arrangement, where disclosure is often legally required and incentivized referrals are sometimes illegal (Chapter 14, p. 243). Network effects are acknowledged as "not always easy or possible to build" (Chapter 14, p. 244).
- **Quick wins can backfire.** "There's a danger that customers get hooked on quick wins and become short-term-focused", so the quick win is to be labeled as one (Chapter 14, p. 248).
- **Lifetime value from revenue.** Normally a mistake, with a named exception: "(typically venture-backed) businesses that are solely focused on growth and market penetration" (Chapter 15, p. 255).
- **Micrometrics.** "In isolation, they don't mean much", and are offered only for drilling down when troubleshooting (Chapter 15, p. 258).
- **Compressing for quality is not quality over quantity.** "Reps matter" (Conclusion, p. 261).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, company, or claim the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. **WebSearch was unavailable to this session — its per-session budget was already spent when the checks began — so every check below ran on WebFetch against a named URL.** That narrowed what could be reached: a claim with no obvious canonical URL, or a site that refused the fetch, is marked unchecked with the reason rather than silently kept. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure.

- **The Lean Marketing Hub, `LeanMarketing.com/hub`, which the book routes every technical how-to to (Chapter 1, p. 30; Chapter 4, p. 89; Chapter 9, p. 155; Chapter 10, p. 166; Chapter 12, p. 206; Chapter 13, p. 225; Conclusion, p. 262).** Live. The page offers a free toolkit, community guides and cheatsheets, a complimentary 40-minute marketing audit with an advisor, live training, and community access, behind a registration form asking for location, business stage, and revenue. The copyright line reads "Lean Marketing Group LLC" with a 2026 year. The book's deferral mechanism therefore still works as designed.
- **Avianca Flight 52 (Chapter 5, pp. 100–101).** Holds in every particular checked: January 25, 1990, a Boeing 707, about 20 miles short of JFK, and — word for word as the book prints it — "Eight of the nine crew members and 65 of the 149 passengers on board were killed." The NTSB probable cause names both the fuel mismanagement and "their failure to communicate an emergency fuel situation to air traffic control", which is the point the author draws from it (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avianca_Flight_52).
- **Betteridge's law of headlines (Chapter 5, p. 99).** Holds, with a longer pedigree than the book gives it: Ian Betteridge stated it in February 2009, but the same observation appears in Andrew Marr's *My Trade* (2004) and as "Davis's law" in a 1991 Murphy's Law compilation (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betteridge%27s_law_of_headlines).
- **Netflix's memo, "We model ourselves on being a professional sports team, not a family" (Chapter 11, p. 184).** Still current. Netflix's live culture page carries "We model ourselves on a professional sports team, not a family. Families are about unconditional love", so the quotation and the argument both stand (jobs.netflix.com/culture).
- **HubSpot's free Website Grader (Chapter 8, p. 140).** Still live and still free, now presented as checking pages "for Speed and SEO" (website.grader.com).
- **Colgate's 1873 toothpaste (Chapter 2, p. 41).** The date and the form hold: "In 1873 the Colgate company began the mass production of aromatic toothpaste in jars", with the collapsible tube following in 1896 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toothpaste). **Unchecked:** the attached claim that "Colgate spent decades and millions of dollars convincing consumers that their breath stinks" — the Wikipedia article on toothpaste carries nothing on the advertising campaigns that made brushing a habit, and the campaign usually credited with that is a different company's, so the attribution could not be confirmed either way.
- **The Rapaport Price List as a benchmark in diamonds (Chapter 8, p. 138).** Holds. It is still described as "a diamond industry standard" whose prices "are used as the basis for standardization and negotiation of diamond prices around the world". One episode since publication is worth naming: the list was suspended during a 2020 price fall and later resumed weekly (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapaport_Diamond_Report).
- **The compound-interest arithmetic: $50 a week for 50 years deposits $130,000 and, at 8 percent compounded monthly, ends at $1,718,541 (Force Multiplier 3 intro, p. 171).** The arithmetic checks: $50 × 52 × 50 = $130,000, and a monthly annuity of $216.67 at 0.6667 percent a month for 600 months computes to about $1,718,300, within rounding of the printed figure.
- **The S&P 500's "inflation-adjusted annual return of about 8 percent" (Force Multiplier 3 intro, p. 171).** Wrong as labeled. The index's compound annual growth rate since 1926, including dividends, is about 9.8 percent nominal and "6% after inflation" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%26P_500). Eight percent is neither the nominal figure nor the real one; at a real 6 percent the same deposits end well below the printed total.
- **The 1930 campaign against recorded music, "the union of American singers", the equivalent of $10 million, 24,000 theater musicians in 1927, and 30 percent unemployed by 1930 (Chapter 6, p. 116).** The campaign is real and the attribution is wrong: it was the American Federation of Musicians, a musicians' union, which "founded the Music Defense League in 1930 to gain public support against canned music in movie theaters" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Federation_of_Musicians). The same source puts the loss at 20,000 theater musicians within two years of the 1927 talkie, which is far more than 30 percent of the book's 24,000. **Unchecked:** the $10 million campaign spend, for which the AFM article gives no budget figure.
- **David Autor's finding that 60 percent of 2018 employment was in jobs that did not exist in 1940 (Chapter 6, p. 115).** Partly checked. The NBER working paper's abstract states that "the majority of current employment is in new job specialties introduced after 1940", which supports the direction; the specific 60 percent figure is not in the abstract and could not be confirmed from it (nber.org/papers/w30389).
- **The smiley face: Harvey Ball created it in 1963, did not trademark it, Franklin Loufrani did, and the mark "has now generated over $500 million in licensing fees" (Chapter 10, pp. 164–165).** The story holds — Ball's 1963 design, for which he was paid $45, and Loufrani's trademark and the Smiley Company. **The $500 million is unchecked**: the source reachable gives the company a 2012 turnover of US$167 million and no licensing-fee total, so the figure could neither be confirmed nor refuted (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smiley).
- **The Michelin Guide, "In 1900, the Michelin brothers… launched the Michelin Guide, which awards stars to fine-dining establishments" (Chapter 8, p. 136).** Two dates are compressed into one. The guide was first published in 1900, but stars were not awarded until 1926, and the zero-to-three-star scale the book describes arrived in 1931 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelin_Guide). The argument the example carries — a tire company profiting by getting customers to drive more — is unaffected.
- **Akamai's State of the Internet reports as the source behind articles ranking countries' internet speed (Chapter 8, p. 137).** Half of this is gone. Akamai still publishes State of the Internet reports, but they are security research — "attack trends by region", API and bot threats, a 2025 year in review, AI risk — not connectivity measurement (akamai.com/security-research/the-state-of-the-internet). The country speed rankings the book points at are no longer what the report is.
- **SPF, DKIM, and DMARC presented as deliverability best practice that "most crm systems support… out of the box" (Chapter 12, p. 194).** Since publication they became mandatory rather than advisable for anyone at newsletter scale: from February 1, 2024, Google requires senders of more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail accounts to set up all three, DMARC enforcement policy included, where standard senders need only SPF or DKIM (support.google.com/a/answer/81126).
- **TikTok as one of five platforms with a characterized subculture (Chapter 13, pp. 215–216).** Its ownership and legal status both changed after the book. The Protecting Americans from Foreign Adversary Controlled Applications Act was signed April 24, 2024; the platform went briefly dark on January 19, 2025; and the de jure ban ended on January 22, 2026 when US operations were divested into a new company, TikTok USDS, with Oracle, Silver Lake, and MGX at 15 percent each and ByteDance retaining 19.9 percent (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restrictions_on_TikTok_in_the_United_States). The platform's character, which is what the book actually rests on, is a separate question this check does not answer.
- **Unchecked, no reachable source.** The David Ogilvy headline quotation and the "80 cents out of your dollar" figure (Chapter 5, p. 98) — the Wikipedia article on Ogilvy carries neither line and names no book for them, and the primary sources are his own books, which were not to hand. Tiffany & Co.'s $165 paper clip and $1,500 gold version (Chapter 3, p. 71) — tiffany.com returned HTTP 403 to the fetch.
- **Not checked, because each is the author's own account or an unattributed anecdote with no outside source to check against:** the 3 percent of people who are "real action-takers" (Introduction, p. 12); the 3 percent of prospects ready to buy today, used twice as a load-bearing figure (Chapter 9, p. 146; Chapter 12, p. 205); the León conference and the Luis Socconini conversation that named the method (Chapter 1, pp. 18–19); the coaching-program pivot into a recruitment service (Chapter 3, p. 63); the massage-therapy clinics with "hundreds of therapists" treating "over 10,000 patients every month" (Chapter 12, p. 200); the McDonald's claim that since the healthy menu launched, "sales of their classic menu items like the Big Mac and fries have skyrocketed" (Chapter 5, p. 102); Peter Thiel's one-metric-per-employee rule at PayPal (Chapter 11, p. 186); the Delhi cobra bounty (Chapter 11, p. 187); the Einstein compound-interest quotation (Force Multiplier 3 intro, p. 171); the John Wanamaker half-the-money quotation (Chapter 1, p. 25); and the author's own "top 0.01 percent of nonfiction authors" (About the Author, p. 264).

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Correlation presented as cause, including once by name.** The claim that "passion follows success rather than being a prerequisite for it" dismisses the opposite reading as "correlation, not causation" (Chapter 7, p. 126), while the author's own version rests on the same kind of evidence, a set of wealthy people he has met and a counterfactual about Steve Jobs and kitchenware. The McDonald's healthy menu is offered as instructive about emotion driving purchase, with "their sales data tells us" standing in for any citation (Chapter 5, p. 102). Nike's, Coca-Cola's, and Apple's advertising is asserted to be "a result of their sales success, not the cause of it" on the evidence that each founder once sold by hand (Chapter 7, pp. 129–130), which establishes a sequence, not a direction.
- **Figures without a source.** The two "3 percent" figures are the load-bearing ones: 3 percent of people are action-takers (Introduction, p. 12), and "typically about 3 percent" of prospects are ready to buy today (Chapter 9, p. 146), the second repeated in Chapter 12 (p. 205) with the further claim that the longer-to-mature pool "can be ten times larger or more". Both drive real recommendations — capture the other 97 percent, build the super signature — and neither carries a citation. The same is true of "about 99 percent of the stuff you need to do to build a business around your passion" (Chapter 7, p. 127), the estimate of "about 100 dots" in the author's competitive intersection (Chapter 2, p. 45), the recommendation to aim for "at least 50 variations" of a headline (Chapter 5, p. 98), and "reading this book makes you 37% sexier", which is a joke (Chapter 3, p. 60) but sits in a list where every other line is offered as fact.
- **Two factual errors and a compression.** The S&P 500's real return is about 6 percent, not the 8 percent the book labels "inflation-adjusted", and the whole compounding illustration is built on the higher number (Force Multiplier 3 intro, p. 171). The 1930 campaign against recorded music was the American Federation of Musicians' Music Defense League, not "the union of American singers", and the job-loss figures do not match the AFM's own (Chapter 6, p. 116). The Michelin Guide launched in 1900 but did not award stars until 1926 (Chapter 8, p. 136). Section 7 has the sources.
- **Examples drawn from the author's own clients, vendors, and products.** The recruitment service is his (Chapter 3, p. 63); the massage-clinic email program is a client's (Chapter 12, p. 200); the good referral request reproduced in full is from a podcaster who then sold the author an ad management service he "continue[s] to use… to this day" (Chapter 14, p. 242); the flagship-tool section closes on the author's own canvas (Chapter 8, p. 140); and the book's best-known framework is named in the copywriting chapter as proof of the naming commandment (Chapter 5, p. 100). The sample is not random, and no case is offered where the method did not work.
- **The book is itself a funnel for the product it describes.** The super signature section reproduces the author's actual super signature in full, whose three offers are a free strategy call, the courses, and the certification program (Chapter 12, pp. 206–207). Every technical chapter routes the reader to the Hub behind a registration form (Chapter 1, p. 30; Chapter 9, p. 155; Chapter 13, p. 225). Chapter 8 argues that a nonfiction book is "the gold standard flagship piece of content" and "the nuclear weapon of business cards" (p. 137). Each of those claims may be true; the reader is being shown the mechanism while standing inside it, and the book does not mark that.
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** The seven are "the only things people really buy or sell" and "drive all human behavior" is asserted, illustrated, and never tested (Chapter 3, p. 57). "the only possibilities" for a failing campaign rules out the product, the price, the offer, and the market by omission, since the list covers only the five steps between ad and order form (Chapter 15, p. 253). The author has "never seen a single instance" of giving away know-how in DIY form having "truly cannibalized service revenue" is one person's absence of observation offered as a general rule (Chapter 8, p. 136). And the whole lean derivation — the claim that gives the book its title and its nine principles — rests on a single conversation at a conference in which someone told the author his earlier book was already lean marketing (Chapter 1, p. 19); nothing after that point tests the analogy against a lean-manufacturing result.
- **A 2024 prediction presented as a design fact.** The author "doubt[s]" they will ever "produce great writing" because the limit is "inherent in their design", argued from next-word prediction and the temperature setting (Chapter 6, pp. 117–118). The hedge is there, but the recommendation built on it — use them as assistants, never for the writing — is stated without it.
- **Internal tension.** Brand marketing is defined as unmeasurable and given the Wanamaker line, then conceded a page later as the thing successful companies move toward at scale (Chapter 1, pp. 23–25). Fixating on brand visuals is dismissed as what people "obsess about" (Chapter 7, p. 129) and then style guides are made an intellectual-property asset because consistency matters (Chapter 10, pp. 165–166). Social media is characterized as a treadmill on rented land that almost nobody wins (Chapter 13, pp. 212–214), and the same chapter's action items ask the reader to pick a platform and resource it.
- **Sourcing that reads as unsourced.** The Avianca Flight 52 passage reproduces the casualty sentence word for word as the English Wikipedia article states it (Chapter 5, p. 100), and the book names no source for it. The book carries no notes, no bibliography, and no reference list of any kind, so a reader who wants to check a figure has only the name in the sentence to go on. That is a structural choice, consistent with the stated refusal to print anything that would date, and it is also the reason section 7 above has as many "unchecked" lines as it does.
- **Deferred content.** Everything technical — CRM comparisons, home-page samples, style-guide templates, email templates, digital-advertising how-tos, the hiring guide for a marketing coordinator — lives behind a registration form on the author's site rather than in the book (Chapter 4, p. 89; Chapter 9, p. 155; Chapter 10, p. 166; Chapter 11, p. 182; Chapter 12, p. 206; Chapter 13, p. 225). The stated reason is that books cannot be updated (Chapter 1, p. 30). A reader of the book alone therefore has the shape of every tactical recommendation and none of its execution, and the online material carries no dates.
