*Flexible Typesetting*, Tim Brown, first edition, 2018, with a foreword by Jessica Hische. Publisher: A Book Apart, New York. ISBN 978-1-937557-71-3. Read 2026-09-10 from the `pdftotext -layout` extraction of the 227-page PDF, front to back.

## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, page by page, with its own exceptions, what has changed since 2018, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that typesetting — preparing text for reading, as distinct from the arranging and calibrating branches of typography — is typography's most important job, and that the web has produced the single biggest change in typographic history (Introduction, p. 1; Ch. 1, p. 5) by moving typographic decisions from a fixed, author-made choice to a reader-participated, conditional continuum: what used to be "a set of fixed decisions" is now, in the author's words, a "continuum of conditional logic" (Ch. 1, p. 21). The book teaches a repeatable process — prepare text and code, select typefaces, shape text blocks, craft compositions, then evaluate and relieve pressure — demonstrated throughout on one worked example, a website for the St. Remy Volunteer Fire Department (Ch. 2, p. 24).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not about arranging type (headlines, calls to action) or calibrating type (tables, navigation, code); those, he says, "deserve their own" books (Ch. 1, p. 8). It is not a prescription: the workflow shown is "just an example," a "walk-through" of the author's own process, not a set of rules (Introduction, p. 3). And the example project itself is explicitly a stand-in, worth considering even where "not directly applicable" to a reader's own kind of site (Introduction, p. 3).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction (pp. 1–4)

The author gives two reasons for the book: to help readers understand and enjoy typesetting, and to show how the web has transformed typography by "returning to our typographic roots" (p. 1). The audience is web designers and developers, but also print-background readers, students, teachers, bosses, and managers (pp. 1–2). The St. Remy Volunteer Fire Department site, edited live on CodePen across the book's chapters, is the one running example project (p. 2), and the book asks the reader to have Chrome installed for its Developer Tools in later chapters (p. 3). The stated structure: Chapters 1–2 cover philosophical and technological underpinnings, Chapters 3–5 build balance and relationships in text and layout, and Chapter 6 introduces a recursive "ladder," pictured on a closing figure, for revisiting and refining earlier decisions (pp. 3–4).

### Chapter 1 — What Is Typesetting? (pp. 5–23)

Typesetting is defined as preparing text for reading, and the reader is told they already do it — choosing typefaces, font sizes, line spacing, margins, and breakpoints (p. 5). The chapter sorts typographic work into three job categories — setting type (the book's own focus: body text, small text), arranging type (headlines, hero text), and calibrating type (tables, navigation, infographics) — and says the latter two "deserve their own" books (pp. 8–9). Typesetting decisions are framed as the foundation that gives a reader "a gut feeling" about the work, even though the decisions are individually subtle (pp. 8–9). "Pressure" is introduced as what disrupts a text block's balance among typeface, font size, measure, and line spacing, illustrated by how a new typeface, a new font size, a narrower measure, or looser line spacing each force the others to change in response (pp. 10–14); the "em box" is introduced as the bounding box a font's glyphs sit within, mapped to the CSS content area at a given font size (p. 12). The chapter quotes Tim Berners-Lee naming "universality" as the web's founding design principle (p. 15) to argue that typographers now "make suggestions" rather than decide (p. 15), and frames web typography as multidimensional (many devices and conditions at once), relative to each reader, and optional — a reader may "opt out" of any typographic suggestion, including by never loading a webfont (pp. 16–19). Performance matters because a slow or sloppy experience is itself a reason people opt out (p. 20). The chapter ends by framing typesetting as a "continuum of conditional logic" rather than a set of fixed decisions, enabled by evolving, collaboratively made web standards — quoting Jeff Veen's phrase "rough consensus and running code" (pp. 21–23).

### Chapter 2 — Preparing Text and Code (pp. 24–54)

A typographer takes on "responsibility for the text" before typesetting it: reading it repeatedly, noting its structure, tone, and evoked adjectives, and placing it culturally against comparable genres (pp. 25–28). Handling the text means declaring UTF-8 encoding as early as possible in the `<head>`, and declaring `lang` and `dir` on the `<html>` element (pp. 30–31); a cleanup punch list covers double spaces, stray line breaks, and correcting dashes, quotation marks, and accents, drawing on Tschichold, Dowding, and Bringhurst's differing opinions on en versus em dashes (pp. 32–34) and Hochuli on word spacing (p. 38); special strings (abbreviations, numerals, ampersands, no-break strings, fractions) are marked up with `<abbr>` and `<span>` for later OpenType styling (pp. 34–36). The chapter recommends a reset stylesheet, specifically Eric Meyer's, over selectively overriding browser defaults (pp. 37–39). Framing the composition starts from each reader's own default font size rather than fixed page dimensions, calling default font size "a rock" — the one reliable constant in flexible design (p. 40); the chapter sets `:root { font-size: 100%; }` plus a `@viewport`/meta-tag pair for actual-size rendering (pp. 41–43), and argues at length that pixel font sizes, viewport units, and `calc()` combinations of the two all fail to respect a reader's own "font-size preference," while percentages, ems, and rems succeed (pp. 44–46); `text-size-adjust` is flagged as an accessibility risk and avoided (p. 47). The chapter's final section covers webfont logistics: preferring paid sources like Typekit or Fontstand over free repositories, whose free-font "spacing is sloppy" and character sets are often incomplete (p. 48), obtaining WOFF2 files with a EULA (p. 50), defining fonts with `@font-face` (pp. 51–52), declaring them in `font-family` (p. 53), and deferring optimization work like subsetting to a later pass (pp. 53–54).

### Chapter 3 — Selecting Typefaces (pp. 55–85)

Text is first inventoried into "text blocks" — chunks sharing formal qualities — and each is classified by the typographic "job" it does: body text (paragraphs, subheads, decks, block quotes, p. 58), display text (headlines, hero text, logotypes, p. 60), signage/UI copy (navigation, buttons, p. 60), tabular data (p. 60), technical accuracy (code, math, poetry, p. 62), and small text (captions, labels, p. 62). A single body-text "anchor typeface" is chosen first, because body text is called "a structural foundation" for the whole composition (pp. 64, 78). Good body-text type is judged, in order, for sturdy shapes (no thin strokes or delicate serifs, pp. 65–67), even typographic color (no white spots or dark patches when the text block is squinted at, pp. 68–70), and active-but-not-overactive texture (p. 70). Appropriateness is judged separately from goodness: reading the actual project text, checking OpenType features such as proportional oldstyle figures and small caps (p. 72), testing rendering across environments (p. 80), and, where relevant, choosing an instance and axes of a variable font (pp. 73–74, 78, 92). Building a type palette around the anchor proceeds by making six to eight "loose," fast choices (p. 78), a quick technical check (p. 79), and then a slower study of graphic harmony — texture, rhythm, proportion, and shape compared side by side (pp. 80–84) — ending with advice against pruning the palette too early (p. 85).

### Chapter 4 — Shaping Text Blocks (pp. 86–142)

The book's longest and most technical chapter. Four essential, interdependent text-block properties are adjusted in a fixed order — typeface, then font size, then measure, then line spacing — because changing one forces the others to be reconsidered (pp. 89–91). Font size is derived from the reader's own default size; the chapter's worked method measures the x-heights of historically common "core fonts" (Times New Roman, Verdana) at sizes the author judges have grown "familiar" over roughly fifteen years of screen use, converts those to an em range, and finds where a new typeface's own x-height falls within that range, landing on `1.15rem` for the example project — by the author's own account "anecdotal" (pp. 93–98, quote p. 94). A modular scale is then generated from that body size and a chosen ratio (3:5 for the example), on the premise that related numbers "feel harmonious" (p. 99), with the ratio itself left to taste (p. 101). Measure (line length) limits are found empirically, by resizing a live text block until it feels right, rather than by counting characters or applying a grid mechanically, since the author calls measure "a visual decision" (p. 103) — the chapter quotes Tobias Frere-Jones on reading "with our eyes," not rulers (p. 103). Line spacing ideally flexes between a tighter value at a text block's narrowest and a looser one at its widest, described through a canal-lock metaphor and called a "CSS lock" (pp. 106–109); the book settles for one static `line-height: 1.5` because the lock is, in the author's words, "complicated to implement" today (p. 109). White space around and inside a text block — margins, indents, letter and word spacing — is treated as "part of the text block" and set in em units so it scales with the type (pp. 110–112), under Cyrus Highsmith's concept of "tempo": all of a text block's white space should condense or loosen together as it flexes (p. 113). Fallback-font handling builds a tiered stack — ideal, fit, common, generic — and matches the fallback's x-height and copyfit to the intended font by measurement, to minimize the "jarring shift" when the webfont loads, scoping the intended font's specific measurements behind a `.wf-active` class (pp. 116–125). The chapter's closing sections enable kerning, ligatures, and contextual alternates through paired high- and low-level OpenType CSS properties (pp. 128–131); enable small caps for abbreviations and proportional oldstyle figures for body-text numerals, quoting Jason Santa Maria on why lowercase numerals "cause less visual disruption" (p. 132); recommend restrained use of bold and italic for emphasis (pp. 135–136); state that links "should be underlined" because color alone is a WCAG contrast failure (p. 136); and, on hyphenation and justification, conclude that because browsers lack sophisticated H&J algorithms, setting text flush-left with basic hyphenation is the "only acceptable option" today (pp. 138–141).

### Chapter 5 — Crafting Compositions (pp. 143–173)

Layouts are roughed in from the earlier text-block sketches, working outward from already-shaped text blocks rather than inward from fixed page dimensions (pp. 143–145). A dedicated warning against changing body-text font size as a composition widens: it moves readers away from their familiar, comfortable size, and em-based media queries do not recalculate when the root font size changes (p. 146). Tailoring layout to text means invoking a breakpoint only when a text block's own established limits are exceeded, found from text-block limits, character counts used contextually rather than prescriptively, or modular-scale numbers (pp. 148–150) — and the chapter lists non-breakpoint alternatives to try first: doing nothing, widening margins, slowing tempo, or (as a deliberate, considered exception to the earlier warning) increasing font size (pp. 150–154). Compositional contrast — size, color, weight, and tempo differences — establishes hierarchy, and the chapter's rule is to use strong contrast in wide, busy layouts and reduce it as a composition narrows and focuses (pp. 159–166). Sass or CSS custom-property variables are recommended for the modular scale, measure limits, the line-spacing range, font stacks, and descriptively named (not numerically named) breakpoints (pp. 166–168). Pixel-based breakpoints are rejected for the same font-size-preference reason as Chapter 2's pixel type sizes (p. 168), but the chapter then shows that an em-based media query always resolves against the browser's *default* root font size and ignores any change made to "the root font-size value" — called "confusing and frustrating" (p. 169) — and looks to container queries as the future fix, quoting Ethan Marcotte's case for querying the space available to "the module" being designed, rather than the shape of the viewport (pp. 169–170). Grid measurements are urged to share "proportional harmony" with the same modular scale used for type (p. 170). A flexible "baseline grid" is presented as aspirational: quoting Jason Santa Maria that current CSS makes baseline grids "more trouble than they're worth," the author nonetheless hopes the (then-draft) CSS Rhythmic Sizing specification and its `line-height-step` property will eventually let one flex rather than fix line spacing (pp. 171–173).

### Chapter 6 — Relieving Pressure (pp. 174–204)

A "ladder" metaphor frames the chapter's method: step down to fix a text block, step up to fix the composition, and re-evaluate from the top (pp. 175, 181). Evaluation proceeds across several axes — width, height, aspect ratio, closeness/zoom, screen coarseness, and network conditions — and several "ways of looking" — resizing, reorienting, comparing, squinting, waiting, and getting outside feedback (pp. 178–180) — logged in a spreadsheet that doubles as a to-do list (pp. 180–181). The bulk of the chapter is a "pattern language" of named pressures and fixes: text that feels too tight (loosen line spacing or reduce font size, p. 182), too loose (loosen letter spacing, tighten vertical spacing, or choose a better text typeface, p. 184), unwieldy (respect a typeface's own size limits, adjust margins, p. 184), or dull (choose a more active-texture typeface, adjust size or weight, p. 184); type that renders poorly or too lightly on coarse screens (choose a typeface designed for small sizes on low-resolution screens, pp. 188–189); a stretched layout, a rough rag, or gappy justified text (enable hyphenation, widen the measure, tighten tempo, or prefer flush-left, pp. 190–193); misaligned text blocks or headings (use a small negative `text-indent` for optical rather than precise stem alignment, pp. 194–195); an empty, crowded, or top-heavy composition (widen text and slow tempo, or loosen spacing and strengthen contrast, or work upward from the smallest subhead changing as little as possible per level, pp. 196–201); and distracting or clashing text blocks, which send the reader back to reassess the type palette from Chapter 3 (pp. 201–203).

### Conclusion (pp. 205–206)

The book closes by urging the reader to practice, to read further into typographic history and craft (naming Bringhurst, Lawson, Tracy, Kinross, Coles, Hyndman, Santa Maria, Stein, Rutter, and Pamental, pp. 205–206), to try and build new tools, and to participate in shaping the still-forming shared vocabulary and standards of web typography (p. 206).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **No correct answers.** There are no "correct" fonts, sizes, measures, or line heights (Ch. 1, p. 18) — only relationships among them that feel balanced or pressured.
- **Scales are optional.** A modular scale is offered as a tool an author can "eyeball" instead (Ch. 4, p. 102); the chapter that introduces it says there is no single "correct" way to use one (Ch. 5, p. 150).
- **Character counting is fine, conditionally.** Counting characters to pick a measure is acceptable only once the visual decision has already been made — using the advice prescriptively, without that judgment first, is what the chapter warns against (Ch. 5, p. 149).
- **Font-size growth is allowed, with care.** The chapter that warns against widening body-text font size as a composition grows (Ch. 5, p. 146) lists increasing it anyway as one of several legitimate responses to pressure, "a respectable choice" if made deliberately (Ch. 5, p. 152).
- **Indents are acceptable.** Using indents rather than vertical space between paragraphs is called "uncommon" on the web but not a bad practice (Ch. 4, p. 111).
- **Full justification is discouraged, not forbidden.** The book prefers flush-left text but gives full-justified text its own remedies (hyphenation, a tightened tempo) when a project requires it (Ch. 4, pp. 138–140; Ch. 6, pp. 192–193).
- **Baseline grids: a partial dissent.** The book quotes Jason Santa Maria calling baseline grids "more trouble than they're worth" on the web today, and the author agrees they cause pressure, but he still hopes a future CSS mechanism resolves the conflict rather than abandoning the goal (Ch. 5, pp. 171–173).
- **The whole workflow is one example.** The author states the process is "just an example," not a prescription, and that a reader's own project may call for a different order of decisions (Introduction, p. 3; Ch. 4, p. 86).
- **`font-size-adjust` is unfinished.** Presented as the eventual fix for fallback-font mismatch, but flagged as working in only one browser "as of this writing" (Ch. 4, p. 122).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter, then nothing else.

1. **Root font size is set in a relative unit, never pixels, so it tracks the reader's own font-size setting** (Ch. 2, pp. 41–46). Record: `app/src/lib/styles/tokens.css`, whose type scale is `rem`-based throughout, and `CONTEXT.md`'s **Fluid step** entry: "Its floor and ceiling are `rem`, so a person's own font-size setting still moves it (WCAG 1.4.4)."
2. **A font size is judged by whether it feels familiar, derived from measuring a typeface's own x-height rather than picked arbitrarily** (Ch. 4, pp. 94–98). Record: [#417](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/417), whose resolution replaced the placeholder `--text-*` ramp with `docs/design/brief.md`'s eight hand-set sizes and weights, closed 2026-08-28.
3. **A single body-text anchor typeface, chosen first, around which the rest of a project's type is built** (Ch. 3, pp. 64, 78). Record: `docs/design/brief.md`'s Typography section: "One family: Hanken Grotesk," used for "headings, body, labels, buttons, tables," with "no second family."
4. **A fallback font stack ordered ideal, fit, common, generic, with metrics matched to minimize a jarring shift when the webfont loads** (Ch. 4, pp. 116–128). Record: `docs/design/brief.md`'s fallback stack, `"Hanken Grotesk", ui-sans-serif, system-ui, sans-serif`, and its Smoothness section: "a font is loaded with a metric-compatible fallback"; `tokens.css`'s `--font-family-base` carries the same stack.
5. **A mismatch between the fallback font and the intended font is a real, measurable defect, not a cosmetic one** (Ch. 4, pp. 116–121). Record: [#550](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/550), where the repo's own layout check measured a component in the fallback face because it never awaited `document.fonts.ready`, hid a real 50px overflow the drag surface showed, and was fixed by waiting for the real face before measuring.
6. **A modular scale — a ratio-based system of related numbers, generated from the body-text size — sizes headings, margins, and breakpoints together** (Ch. 4, pp. 99–101; Ch. 5, pp. 166–170). Record: [#531](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/531), which adopted a different mechanism for the same problem — a `clamp()` between a floor and ceiling on a shared "ramp," growing with `cqi` — and explicitly declined a modular scale's ratio-based step selection as "reopening the ratio," a question against #417, not #531.
7. **Line-length limits (measure) are found by visual trial and error and capped so a text block never reads too wide** (Ch. 4, pp. 102–110). Record: `tokens.css`'s `--measure: 65ch`, carried into `docs/design/brief.md`'s "Prose gets a measure" rule, whose own record is a different, already-landed book — `docs/research/every-layout-css-primitives.md`'s citation of the axiom "the measure should never exceed 60ch."
8. **Line spacing should ideally flex continuously between a tight value at a text block's narrowest and a loose value at its widest** (Ch. 4, pp. 106–109). Record: `tokens.css`, where every `--text-*-leading` (e.g. `--text-body-leading: 1.55`) is one fixed number per type-scale step, not derived from a text block's own measure.
9. **Named Sass variables for breakpoints, chosen from measure limits or a modular scale, read more clearly than raw numbers** (Ch. 5, pp. 166–168). Record: [ADR-0024](../../adr/0024-layout-is-intrinsic-and-320px-is-a-conformance-commitment.md), "No named set of widths, ever... a shared width name is a breakpoint, and naming a set is how a codebase drifts back to designing for device sizes."
10. **A media query's width breakpoint should be measured in `em`, not `px`, because pixel breakpoints ignore a reader's font-size preference — though `em`-based ones carry their own root-font-size confusion** (Ch. 5, p. 168). Record: [ADR-0024](../../adr/0024-layout-is-intrinsic-and-320px-is-a-conformance-commitment.md)'s source gate rule 1: "A media query that measures width fails, anywhere, with no exception."
11. **Container queries are named as the correct future mechanism for a text block to respond to its own container rather than the viewport** (Ch. 5, pp. 169–170). Record: [ADR-0024](../../adr/0024-layout-is-intrinsic-and-320px-is-a-conformance-commitment.md) rule 2, which adopts `@container` as "the right escape hatch" and "the only sanctioned one."
12. **A flexible baseline grid, tying line spacing to a shared vertical rhythm, is named as a still-unbuilt CSS mechanism the author hopes for** (Ch. 5, pp. 171–173). Record: none — no baseline-grid mechanism has been built or decided in this repo; the underlying `line-height-step` property the book points to remains unshipped (see section 7).
13. **Proportional oldstyle figures are recommended for body-text numerals so they blend with lowercase letters rather than disrupt reading** (Ch. 4, pp. 132–134). Record: [#509](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/509), which decided the opposite figure style for compared numbers — `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` on any `DataTable` column marked `numeric: true`, so digits align by place value, settling the brief's own "tabular figures wherever numbers are compared" rule for table columns, in #509's own words.
14. **A text block's typographic jobs — body text, display, signage/UI, tabular data, small text — each call for a different typeface treatment** (Ch. 1, pp. 8–9; Ch. 3, pp. 58–63). Record: `docs/design/brief.md`'s eight-step type scale, decided on #417, whose own "Used for" column names a purpose per step (`display`, `heading`, `body`, `label`, `meta`, and others) within the one family the brief adopts.
15. **Links should be underlined, not denoted by color alone, because color-only links fail WCAG contrast guidance** (Ch. 4, pp. 136–138). Record: `app/src/lib/components/atoms/Link.svelte`, whose default `a { text-decoration: underline; }` is overridden only for an icon-bearing link, which the component's own comment says "reads as a control, not inline prose."

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, or company example the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure.

- **Adobe Typekit, the book's example webfont host (Ch. 2, pp. 48, 53; Ch. 4, throughout).** Renamed. Typekit became Adobe Fonts on October 15, 2018, months after the book's publication (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Fonts). The book's Typekit-specific mechanics — the `.wf-active` class from Typekit's font events, the Kit Editor — describe a product that no longer exists under that name.
- **`font-size-adjust`, said to work "only in Firefox" (Ch. 4, p. 122).** Changed substantially. As of 2026 it has full cross-browser support — Chrome 127+, Edge 127+, Safari 17+, Firefox 118+ — per caniuse.com/font-size-adjust. The fallback-font-matching problem the book calls mostly unsolved (Ch. 4, p. 128) now has a broadly usable tool it did not have in 2018.
- **Container queries, hoped for as a future mechanism for both dynamic line spacing and component-relative breakpoints (Ch. 4, p. 109; Ch. 5, pp. 169–170).** Shipped. Chrome 106, Firefox 110, and Safari 16.0 all ship `@container` size queries, with 94.87% global browser usage as of the check (caniuse.com/css-container-queries). What the book could only gesture at is now this repo's own adopted mechanism (section 6, entries 11–12).
- **The CSS Rhythmic Sizing spec and its `line-height-step` property, cited as the hoped-for path to a flexible baseline grid (Ch. 5, pp. 172–173).** Stalled. The property exists only as a draft in the CSS Rhythmic Sizing Module Level 1 specification; MDN states plainly that no browser supports the feature as of the check (developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/line-height-step). The mechanism the book hoped would resolve the baseline-grid conflict never shipped anywhere.
- **modularscale.com, the calculator the author co-built and uses throughout Chapter 4 (p. 99).** Still live in 2026, offering the same ratio-based calculator plus Sass and JavaScript plugins, and stating it was relaunched in 2015 by Tim Brown and Scott Kellum (modularscale.com).
- **flexibletypesetting.com, the book's own companion site (Resources, p. 210).** Still live, offering the complete book as a free download alongside its table of contents and endorsements (flexibletypesetting.com).
- **stremyfd.com, the book's one running example project (Introduction, p. 2, and throughout).** Still live in 2026 as the real St. Remy Volunteer Fire Department site, serving Esopus, NY (stremyfd.com).
- **Not checked.** Zach Leatherman's fontfamily.io (Ch. 4, pp. 117–118), Typography.supply (Ch. 3, p. 76), Axis-Praxis (Ch. 3, pp. 74, 79), CrossBrowserTesting.com (Ch. 3, p. 75), Charcod.es (Ch. 2, pp. 32, 35), the Font Face Observer library (Ch. 4, p. 120), and the many `bkaprt.com` short links the book uses for nearly every citation were not individually re-verified; each is a small tool or redirector rather than a load-bearing figure, and checking dozens of short links individually was out of scope for this pass.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Correlation presented as cause, without a control.** The chapters on choosing a font size, a measure, and line spacing repeatedly direct the reader to "eyeball" or "go with your gut" (Ch. 4, pp. 94, 102, 106) and to judge success by squinting at typographic color and texture (Ch. 3, pp. 68–70). These are craft judgments the author is well positioned to make, but the book offers no way for a reader to check a resulting decision against anything but the same subjective method that produced it.
- **A figure built from an unpublished dataset.** The book's central font-size method — a "familiar" x-height range of roughly .419em to .579em — is derived from the author's own fifteen years of watching "the most popular fonts" on the web (Ch. 4, p. 96), which he calls "anecdotal data" himself (Ch. 4, p. 94). The underlying measurements are not published, so the range cannot be independently checked or reproduced.
- **The whole method rests on one small project.** Every technique in the book is demonstrated on a single site with a handful of text blocks — a fire department's homepage (Ch. 2, p. 24) — and the author says as much: the project is "just an example" (Introduction, p. 3). The book never shows its own process applied to a dense, tabular, multilingual, or highly hierarchical site, which is where several of its techniques (modular scales, contrast rules, breakpoint tailoring) would be harder to apply.
- **An unresolved internal tension.** Chapter 5 opens with a dedicated warning against changing body-text font size as a layout widens, because it moves a reader away from a "familiar" font size (Ch. 5, p. 146), then a few pages later lists increasing font size as one of several legitimate ways to relieve pressure, calling it "a respectable choice" if made deliberately (Ch. 5, p. 152). The book names the tension but does not fully resolve it.
- **Deferred content, demonstrated only in theory.** The "CSS lock" for dynamic line spacing is presented as the correct solution to a problem the book spends several pages establishing (Ch. 4, pp. 106–109), but the worked example never uses it, settling for a static `line-height: 1.5` because the technique is "complicated to implement" (Ch. 4, p. 109). A reader following the book's own project never sees the recommended technique work.
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not fully reach.** The modular-scale chapter argues that related numbers "feel harmonious" (Ch. 4, p. 99) and shows three worked comparisons, but offers no account of why a 3:5 ratio suits the example project beyond the author's own notecards being that size (Ch. 4, p. 101) — a reason the author immediately flags as beside the point if the resulting numbers were impractical.
