## 3. The claim

The book has two theses, one per part, and both are stated as skills rather than outcomes. Part I opens with the line that making money is a skill you learn, not a thing you do (Building Wealth, p. 29), and the skill is to get rich "without getting lucky" by combining specific knowledge, accountability, and leverage, then applying them over a long horizon so that compound interest does the work (Building Wealth, pp. 31–37, 83–84). Wealth is defined as assets that "earn while you sleep", distinct from money, which is how wealth is transferred, and from status, a person's place in the social hierarchy (Building Wealth, pp. 31, 38). Part II holds that happiness is a choice and a skill (Learning Happiness, p. 134), a "default state" that appears when the sense of something missing is removed, reached mostly through fewer desires, presence, and acceptance rather than through success (Learning Happiness, pp. 129, 131, 139–140). The two parts are ordered on purpose: wealth, health, and happiness are pursued in that order while their importance runs the other way (Part II, p. 125), and the wealth section closes with the line to get rich first (Building Wealth, p. 90).

The compiler and the subject both say what the claim is not. Jorgenson's disclaimer says the book was built entirely from transcripts, tweets, and talks, that not every source is primary, that everything in it is "taken out of context", and that phrasing is to be checked against a primary source before it is cited (Important Notes, pp. 9–10). Ferriss's foreword says to take "nothing as gospel" (Foreword, p. 15). Naval says he cannot teach anything, only inspire and offer "a few hooks" (Now Here Is Naval, p. 25). The wealth thesis is not a get-rich-quick scheme, which he calls someone else getting rich off you (Building Wealth, p. 37), and it is not a claim that hard work is optional: hard work matters and "you can't skimp on it", but it has to be directed (Building Wealth, p. 30). The happiness thesis is not universal: there are "no glib answers", and the answers that exist are personal (Learning Happiness, p. 128). And the book calls itself a choose-your-own-adventure, meant to be jumped around in rather than read as an argument (Important Notes, p. 11).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Front matter (pp. 9–26)

**Important Notes on This Book (pp. 9–11).** Jorgenson's disclaimer: the transcripts were edited for clarity "(multiple times)", some excerpts are other writers quoting Naval, the authenticity of every source cannot be certain, and errors are "(very) possible" (pp. 9–10). Formatting conventions: tweets are set as pull quotes but are unique content, tweetstorms are chained with arrows, interviewers' questions are bolded and not distinguished from one another, and a bracketed number marks the end of an excerpt and points to the Sources appendix (pp. 10–11).

**Foreword by Tim Ferriss (pp. 13–15).** Ferriss says he breaks a rule against forewords because the book is free, because he has known Naval for more than a decade, and as a joke about naming Naval's next child (p. 13). He names Naval as CEO and co-founder of AngelList, co-founder of Vast.com and Epinions, and an early investor in Twitter, Uber, Yammer, and OpenDNS (p. 14), and lists the traits he takes seriously: first-principles thinking, changing his mind regularly, not fooling himself (pp. 14–15).

**Eric's Note (pp. 17–19).** The compiler's case: Naval founded Epinions during the 2000 dot-com crash and AngelList in 2010, invested early in Uber, Twitter, and Postmates, and is followed because he is both successful and happy, "a rare combination" (p. 17). The book collects a decade of Naval's tweets, blog posts, and podcasts in his own words and is meant to be consulted by topic (pp. 18–19); Jorgenson says he rereads sections before making an investment (p. 19).

**Timeline (p. 21).** Born in Delhi in 1974; Queens at nine; Stuyvesant at fourteen; Dartmouth in computer science and economics at twenty-one; Epinions founder and CEO in 1999; venture partner at August Capital in 2001; Vast.com in 2003; called "Radioactive Mud" in Silicon Valley in 2005; Hit Forge and the VentureHacks blog in 2007; AngelList and the Uber investment in 2010; lobbied for the JOBS Act in 2012; named "Angel Investor of the Year" in 2018.

**Now Here Is Naval in His Own Words (pp. 23–26).** A single-parent immigrant upbringing in an unsafe part of New York, with the library as the after-school center and books as his only real friends (p. 24). Stuyvesant as an "intelligence lottery" that led to the Ivy League and tech (p. 25). Present position: a personal investor in about two hundred companies, a small partner in a cryptocurrency fund, and founder and chairman of AngelList (p. 25). He says he was born poor and miserable, is now well-off and very happy, and worked at both (p. 25).

### Part I: Building Wealth (pp. 29–91)

**Understand How Wealth Is Created (pp. 30–39).** Naval says that dropped on a random street in any English-speaking country he would be wealthy again within five or ten years, because it is a skillset anyone can develop (p. 30). Getting rich is not mainly about hard work but about knowing what to do, with whom, and when (p. 30). The core is the "How to Get Rich" tweetstorm, which he says he formed at thirteen or fourteen and has carried for thirty years (p. 30). Its shape, in order (pp. 31–37): seek wealth, not money or status; ethical wealth creation is possible and despising wealth makes it elude you; ignore status players; equity, not rented time; give society what it wants and cannot yet get, "At scale"; long-term games with long-term people; compound interest in every domain; partners chosen for intelligence, energy, and "above all, integrity", never cynics; learn to sell and to build; specific knowledge, which cannot be trained for, is found through curiosity and passion, is taught by apprenticeship, and cannot be outsourced or automated; accountability under one's own name; leverage from capital, people, and products with "no marginal cost of replication", meaning code and media; capital and labor are "permissioned leverage", code and media permissionless; there is no skill called business; study microeconomics, game theory, psychology, persuasion, ethics, mathematics, and computers; set an aspirational personal hourly rate; work as hard as you can, though who and what matter more; become the best in the world at what you do and keep redefining it until that is true; no get-rich-quick schemes. The summary is "Productize Yourself", where the second word carries uniqueness and accountability and the first carries leverage and specific knowledge (p. 37); finding what one can uniquely provide may take most of a decade (p. 38). Money is "social credits", an IOU from society; wealth is the factory, the robots, the program running at night (p. 38). Technology democratizes consumption and "consolidates production", technology is the set of things that do not quite work yet, and the example of giving society what it wants and scaling it is Apple and the smartphone (p. 39).

**Find and Build Specific Knowledge (pp. 40–46).** Sales skill as an example of specific knowledge: a natural learned it in the school yard, but anyone can improve by reading Cialdini or doing door-to-door sales (p. 40). Specific knowledge is what a person did as a child almost effortlessly; the list of examples runs from sales and music to obsession, science fiction, games, and gossip (p. 41). Naval's own: reading, technology, learning fast, getting bored fast, which he says matches venture investing (p. 42); his mother named business as his path when he wanted to be an astrophysicist (p. 43). Specific knowledge sits "at the edge of knowledge", and someone fully into it will outperform a half-committed rival by a lot because compound interest and leverage apply to ideas (pp. 43–44). A tweet on escaping competition through authenticity: competing means copying, and nobody can compete with a person at being that person; Joe Rogan, Scott Adams, and Bill Watterson are the examples (pp. 41, 44). The most important skill is to be a perpetual learner, able to master a new profession in nine months that is obsolete four years later (p. 45). Foundations over depth: arithmetic over calculus, plain English over vocabulary, persuasion over being an expert digital marketer (p. 45); mastery is possible in one or two things (p. 46).

**Play Long-Term Games with Long-Term People (pp. 46–49).** Compound interest applies to relationships and reputation, which can become thousands of times more valuable than talent alone (p. 47). The example is Elad Gil, who always rounds off in Naval's favor, so Naval sends him every deal (pp. 47–48). Ninety-nine percent of effort is wasted in the goal sense, though not in the learning sense; the point is to find the one percent that will not be wasted and go all-in (pp. 48–49).

**Take On Accountability (pp. 50–52).** Leverage in labor and capital must be given by others, so credibility is built by taking risks under one's own name (pp. 50–51). Naval's own example: talking about philosophy in public around 2014–2015 despite back-channel warnings that he was ending his career, and early crypto (p. 51). The captain-goes-down-with-the-ship image; in a business context the risk is being the last to get capital back out (pp. 51–52). The downside risk is "not that large", personal bankruptcy can wipe debts clean in good ecosystems, and people should take on far more accountability than they do (p. 52).

**Build or Buy Equity in a Business (pp. 53–54).** Without a piece of a business there is no path to financial freedom (p. 53). Even lawyers and doctors renting out time do not earn nonlinearly; doctors who get really rich open a private practice or build a device or process with intellectual property (pp. 53–54). Stock options at a tech company are "a fine way to start", but real wealth comes from starting companies or investing (p. 54).

**Find a Position of Leverage (pp. 55–67).** This is "an age of infinite leverage", and curiosity is a better career foundation than whatever is making money now (p. 55). Doing things for their own sake produces the best work; the year Naval generated the most wealth was the year he worked least hard (p. 56). Skills society cannot yet train others to do are the ones it pays for; anything trainable will eventually be done by a computer (p. 57). His advice is to build a brand on Twitter, on YouTube, and by "giving away free work" while waiting for the moment (p. 57). Three classes of leverage: labor, the oldest and "the worst form", because managing people is messy and one step from mutiny (p. 58); capital, the dominant form of the last century, which scales because managing more money is easier than managing more people (p. 58); and products with no marginal cost of replication, books, media, movies, and code, of which code is the most powerful permissionless form (pp. 58–59). The new fortunes are all made through code or media: Joe Rogan earning fifty to a hundred million dollars a year from his podcast, PewDiePie being "bigger than the news", and Bezos, Zuckerberg, Page, Brin, Gates, and Jobs (pp. 59–60). Permissionless leverage is egalitarian; every developer has "an army of robots" working at night (p. 60). His rule is to optimize for independence over pay (p. 60). A leveraged worker can out-produce an unleveraged one by a thousand or ten thousand times, and "1,000x programmers" exist (p. 61); one engineer's right piece of code can create half a billion dollars of value while ten working harder waste their time (p. 61). He says to be paid on outputs, so that time comes back; the forty-hour week is "a relic" of the industrial age (p. 62). Sales and building any product disconnect input from output; customer service does not (p. 62). Learning to sell and to build is expanded: building includes design, development, logistics, and operating a service; selling includes marketing, recruiting, fundraising, and PR (pp. 63–64). The real estate ladder illustrates leverage rising with accountability and specific knowledge: a laborer at ten to twenty dollars an hour, a general contractor keeping the difference on a $50,000 job, a developer making a million on a flip, a fund manager, and finally a Trulia, Redfin, or Zillow that combines code, capital, and labor (pp. 64–66). The one thing to avoid is "the risk of ruin": jail, catastrophic loss, physical danger, losing all capital; take "rationally optimistic bets" with big upsides (pp. 66–67).

**Get Paid for Your Judgment (pp. 67–69).** Naval says he would love to be paid purely for judgment and have a robot, capital, or computer do the work (p. 68). Someone right 85 percent of the time instead of 75 is worth fifty to two hundred million dollars steering a $100 billion ship; CEOs are paid for leverage (p. 68). Buffett is the example of demonstrated judgment with credibility; nobody asks how hard he works (p. 68). Buffett spends "a year deciding" and a day acting (p. 69).

**Prioritize and Focus (pp. 69–76).** Naval lost his first fortune in the stock market and was cheated out of the second by business partners; his wealth accrued "a few chips" at a time (pp. 69–70). Thanks to the internet he has too many ways to make money and not enough time (p. 70). Set a very high personal hourly rate and factor time into every decision; his own figure was $5,000 an hour before he had real money, which in hindsight was about $1,000 (pp. 70–71). His rule is to outsource anything cheaper than the rate, including cooking (p. 71). Despising wealth makes it elude you because people sense the relative mindset; optimists do better long term (p. 72). Two games: money, positive-sum, and status, a zero-sum game played since monkey tribes; attacks on wealth creation are status plays (pp. 73–74). The three big early decisions are where to live, whom to be with, and what to do, and each deserves one to two years of deciding (pp. 74–75). His answer on surrounding oneself with successful people is to help them and not to measure (p. 75). Naval was embarrassed into starting his first company in 1996 after telling everyone at @Home Network he would (p. 76).

**Find Work That Feels Like Play (pp. 76–82).** Humans worked for themselves as hunter-gatherers; the internet returns to that, and Naval would rather be "a failed entrepreneur" than someone who never tried (pp. 76–77). Money was a means to an end; end goals lead to more goals; he wants off "the hedonic treadmill" (p. 77). Retirement is when a person stops sacrificing today for an imaginary tomorrow, reached by passive income, a zero burn rate, or loving the work (p. 78). Authenticity is the way out of "the competition trap" (p. 78). Creating businesses is now an art, done in three months, with money almost as a side effect (p. 79). He is just playing, sixteen hours a day, which is why nobody can compete (p. 80). The lust for money is "a bottomless pit"; the defense is to hold lifestyle fixed (pp. 80–81). Freedom is his "number one value" (p. 81). Asked whether one must start a company: the consistently most successful in Silicon Valley are venture capitalists and the people who join companies just after product/market fit, with Zuckerberg calling Jim Breyer for a head of product as the example; early promotions matter and the alumni network is the most important thing about a job (pp. 81–82).

**How to Get Lucky (pp. 82–87).** In a thousand parallel universes, be wealthy in 999 (p. 83). Four kinds of luck, from a conversation with co-founder Babak Nivi: blind luck, luck from hustle, luck from being skilled enough to spot it, and luck from "a unique character", illustrated by the deep-sea diver whom treasure finders must hire (pp. 83–84). Reputation makes people bring deals; Buffett gets offered deals because of it (p. 85). Nivi's formulation: in a long-term game everybody makes each other rich (p. 85). Business networking is "a complete waste of time", because someone building something interesting will always have more people wanting to know them (pp. 85–86). A trust signal: someone who talks a lot about their own honesty is probably dishonest (p. 86). The closer someone wants to get to him, the better their values have to be (p. 87).

**Be Patient (pp. 87–91).** Great people have great outcomes, given a long enough timescale (p. 87). Counting the time means running out of patience before success arrives (p. 88). The most common bad advice is "You're too young" (p. 88). A real résumé is "a catalog" of suffering (p. 89). Money solves external problems but not happiness, health, family, or calm; the person one becomes to make money is high-anxiety and cannot turn it off (pp. 89–90). Buddha "was a prince"; money lets a person buy the freedom monks once bought by renouncing everything, so the order is rich first, then fit, then happy (pp. 90–91).

### Part I: Building Judgment (pp. 93–124)

**Judgment (pp. 94–95).** The section opens with the line that there is "no shortcut to smart" (p. 93). To get rich in a deterministically predictable way, stay on the edge of trends and study technology, design, and art (p. 94). Hard work is "really overrated"; judgment is underrated, defined as wisdom, meaning knowledge of the long-term consequences of one's actions, applied to external problems (p. 94). In an age of leverage one correct decision can win everything, and direction matters more than speed (pp. 94–95).

**How to Think Clearly (pp. 95–100).** Real knowledge is built from the ground up; anyone using a lot of fancy words probably does not know the subject, and a person who cannot explain a thing to a child does not know it; Feynman's *Six Easy Pieces* is the example (p. 95). Advanced concepts in a field are "less proven" (p. 96). The obstacle to seeing reality is preconceived notions of how it should be; a moment of suffering is the moment of seeing things as they are, which is also a moment of truth from which progress starts (pp. 96–97). Advice to a friend is obvious because desire is not in the way; Naval says he acknowledges bad business news publicly so he cannot hide it from himself (pp. 97–98). He recommends empty space, a day or two a week with no meetings, because great ideas come only after boredom (p. 98). Smart people tend to be weird; a contrarian reasons independently from the ground up, and "Optimistic contrarians" are the rarest breed (pp. 99–100).

**Shed Your Identity to See Reality (pp. 101–103).** Egos are built in the first two decades and then served for life (p. 101). Naval says habits are necessary but are to be taken apart and asked whether they still serve, and that he is less habitual than most (p. 101). Any belief taken "in a package" is suspect; he stopped identifying as libertarian after catching himself defending positions he had not thought through (p. 102). Suffering can force an identity change, as with an injured athlete like Bruce Lee (pp. 102–103). There are no permanent solutions in a dynamic system (p. 103).

**Learn the Skills of Decision-Making (pp. 103–106).** The classical virtues are decision heuristics for the long term (p. 103). Self-serving conclusions get a higher bar; biases are time-saving heuristics to discard for important decisions (p. 104). Radical honesty, citing Feynman on being the easiest person to fool, combined with a rule he credits to Buffett, praise specifically and criticize generally (pp. 104–105). Charisma is confidence and love projected at once (p. 105). Someone right 80 percent of the time rather than 70 is valued "hundreds of times more"; managing a billion dollars and being right 10 percent more often creates a hundred million of value on one call (p. 105). Farnam Street is named as the blog for better decisions (p. 106). The more one knows, the less one diversifies (p. 106).

**Collect Mental Models (pp. 106–113).** The brain is "a memory prediction machine"; principles beat extrapolating from what happened last time (p. 106). Sources of models: evolution, game theory, Munger, Taleb, Franklin (p. 106). Tweets are pointers and mnemonics that only work with underlying experience; otherwise they read as "a collection of quotes" (pp. 106–107). Short entries follow: evolution as civilization's answer to who mates (p. 107); inversion, success as avoiding mistakes rather than having correct judgment (p. 107); complexity theory, which taught him that people are "fundamentally ignorant" and bad at prediction (p. 108); microeconomics and game theory as fundamentals (p. 108); the principal-agent problem as the most fundamental problem in microeconomics, with a line attributed to Caesar on going yourself versus sending, and the rule that tying compensation to the value created turns an agent into a principal (pp. 108–109); compound interest, 30 percent for thirty years yielding thousands of times the money, and a business of a hundred users growing 20 percent a month stacking "very, very quickly" to millions (pp. 109–110); basic math, meaning arithmetic, probability, and statistics, not calculus (p. 110); black swans via Taleb (p. 110); calculus as a principle rather than a skill (p. 110); falsifiability, with macroeconomics called "corrupted" because it makes no falsifiable predictions (p. 111). The heuristic that an undecidable choice means no: modern society is full of options while humans evolved in "tribes of 150", decisions lock in for years, and a pros-and-cons spreadsheet is itself the answer (pp. 111–112). "Run uphill": his heuristic for an even split is the path with short-term pain, because the brain overvalues short-term happiness; working out and confusing books are the examples (pp. 112–113). To build mental models, read a lot; a tweet says an hour a day of science, math, and philosophy puts a person in the upper echelon within seven years (p. 113).

**Learn to Love to Read (pp. 114–124).** A genuine love of reading is "a superpower" in "the age of Alexandria"; the scarce thing is desire, not means (p. 114). Reading began with a grandfather's *Reader's Digest* and comic books, unforced, including "mental junk food" (p. 114). His advice is to read what you love until you love to read, to reread the hundred great books for you, and to absorb a better book more slowly (p. 115). Naval says he skims, speed-reads, jumps around, and cannot quote passages; a book half-remembered was one he was ready to reread (p. 115). He reads one to two hours a day, which he says puts him in "the top .00001 percent" and accounts for any material success he has had (p. 116). What one reads almost does not matter (p. 116). His notetaking is Twitter, distilling insights into aphorisms and then being attacked for the exceptions (pp. 116–117). Process: start at the beginning, move fast, drop or skip if the first chapter does not grab (p. 117). The number of books completed is "a vanity metric" (p. 117). Most nonfiction books have one point followed by examples; he reads maybe two chapters of "pseudoscience bestsellers" and says yes when asked whether he read them (pp. 117–118). Books written to make money are not worth reading (p. 118). Teaching forces learning (p. 118). For sixty days of clearer thinking: read the greats in math, science, and philosophy, ignore contemporaries and news, avoid tribal identification (p. 118). Naval says no book in the library is to be feared, and that confusion is the mental version of gym pain (p. 119). Well-read people who are not smart read the wrong things in the wrong order and built a worldview on weak axioms (p. 119). Foundations: mathematics, hard sciences, "Microeconomics 101"; read originals, Darwin before Dawkins, Smith, von Mises, Hayek, and Marx before their interpreters (pp. 120–121). A perpetual learning machine is never out of options for making money (p. 121). Two contradictory trainings, a short attention span and the rule to finish every book, leave everyone he knows stuck on some book (p. 121). Naval's own switch from books to blogs to Twitter left him taking "dopamine snacks", and his fix was to treat books as throwaway blog posts, reading ten to twenty at once with no obligation to finish (pp. 121–122). The older the problem, the older the solution: modern books for driving a car, old books for health, calm, values, and family, because a book that survived two thousand years has been filtered by many people (pp. 122–123). A tweet warns to be careful what you read, since thoughts stick like songs (p. 123). The part closes with a tweet that a calm mind, a fit body, and a house full of love cannot be bought and must be earned (p. 124).

### Part II: Learning Happiness (pp. 127–156)

**Happiness Is Learned (pp. 128–133).** The section opens with the line not to take oneself seriously, being "a monkey with a plan" (p. 127). Ten years earlier Naval rates himself two or three out of ten; now nine, and money is a very small piece of it (p. 128). Happiness is a personal skill that can be learned like fitness, with personal rather than universal answers (p. 128). His current definition: a default state present when the sense of something missing is removed; humans are "survival-and-replication machines" (p. 129). Every positive thought holds a negative one, per the Tao Te Ching, so happiness is about the absence of desire, not positive thinking (pp. 129–130). Latching onto a happy feeling drops one out of it (p. 130). Reality "is neutral"; nature has no concept of happiness, and interpretation is a choice (pp. 130–131). The "insignificance of the self" removes expectations of how life should be; the neutral state is how children live, and is "a perfection state" (pp. 131–132). Lives are a firefly's blink (p. 132). Meditation helps, though "amazing how little"; real happiness comes as a side effect of peace, mostly from acceptance (p. 132). The list of his own practices: lowered identity, no politics, no unhappy people, philosophy, meditation, happy people, and it works (p. 133).

**Happiness Is a Choice, Requires Presence, Requires Peace (pp. 134–136).** The mind is as malleable as the body; memory and identity are burdens from the past (p. 134). Most of the brain is planning or regretting; he says he believes in nothing from his past (p. 134). Enlightenment is "the space between your thoughts" (p. 135). Happiness is more about peace than joy, and an externally inflicted purpose will not bring it (p. 135). Pervasive anxiety is "nexting", and the test is to sit and do nothing; his method is to ask whether he would rather have the thought or his peace (pp. 135–136). A happy person interprets events so as not to lose innate peace (p. 136).

**Every Desire Is a Chosen Unhappiness (pp. 137–139).** The most common human mistake is expecting happiness from an external circumstance, credited to Buddhism; the example is waiting for a new car (p. 137). Doing external things is still necessary, since a living creature locally reverses entropy; the delusion is that they bring lasting peace (p. 138). Desire is "a contract" with oneself to be unhappy until the wanted thing arrives, so he keeps at most one big desire at a time (p. 138). He says it matters more to perfect a desire than to pursue one not fully wanted (p. 139). Young people have time and health but no money; the middle-aged money and health but no time; the old money and time but no health (p. 139).

**Success Does Not Earn Happiness (pp. 139–143).** A tweet sets satisfaction against success, which "comes from dissatisfaction", and says to choose (p. 139). An interviewer's question attributes to Confucius the saying about two lives; Naval's answer is hedonic adaptation and the conclusion that real success is internal (pp. 139–140). Success as usually defined means winning a game; he names Jobs, Andreessen for Netscape, Satoshi Nakamoto, and Musk, but the real winners step out of the game entirely: Jerzy Gregorek, the Buddha, Krishnamurti (p. 141). Pascal on sitting quietly in a room: thirty happy minutes alone is success (pp. 141–142). Happiness is "an emergent property" of peace, and most people seek peace through war, including starting a business (p. 142). Peace is happiness at rest and happiness peace in motion (p. 142). Peace comes from giving up the idea of problems (p. 143).

**Envy Is the Enemy of Happiness (pp. 143–145).** The word "should" is guilt or social programming (p. 143). Working out and making money are multiplayer competitive games; training oneself to be happy is "a single-player game", and so is life (pp. 143–144). Buffett's inner scorecard: the real scorecards are internal (p. 144). Jealousy dissolved once he saw he could not take parts of another life, only a wholesale swap (pp. 144–145).

**Happiness Is Built by Habits (pp. 145–151).** Peace and happiness are skills, with a genetic range and conditioning that can be undone (p. 145). A tweet: work with people more successful than you, play with people happier (p. 145). Finding what works is trial and error across meditation styles, yoga, kite-surfing, cooking (p. 146). For medicines of the mind the placebo effect is "100 percent effective", so approach practices positively; Tolle's body-energy exercise felt good (p. 146). Habits that stabilize mood: no alcohol, no sugar, no Facebook, Snapchat, or Twitter; video games and caffeine trade long term for short (pp. 146–147). A person is their habits plus the people they spend time with; the "five chimps theory" (p. 147). His first rule of conflict is not to be around people who constantly engage in it (p. 148). A tweet: no working for a day with someone you could not work with for life (p. 148). Behzad's line to stop asking why and start saying wow (p. 148). Happiness habits list: insight meditation, finding the positive interpretation "sub-second", sunlight, asking whether a desire is worth being unhappy over, dropping caffeine, daily exercise (p. 149). Judging separates and reflects back (p. 150). His hack is to tell friends you are a happy person, to invoke consistency bias (p. 150). Tweets: minimize the phone, calendar, and alarm clock apps; fewer secrets; reset a funk with meditation, music, and exercise; hedonic adaptation is stronger for man-made things; screen activities are linked to less happiness with "No exceptions"; a personal metric of time spent out of obligation; the news's job is to make people anxious; positive-sum games make positive people; serotonin from sunlight, exercise, positive thinking, and tryptophan (pp. 150–151). A tweetstorm on changing habits: pick one, visualize, plan a sustainable path, identify triggers and substitutes, tell friends, to track meticulously, bake in the new self-image (p. 151).

**Find Happiness in Acceptance (pp. 151–156).** Three choices in any situation: change it, accept it, or leave it; wanting to change is a desire, so pick one big desire at a time (pp. 151–152). The word he uses most to himself is "accept" (p. 152). Two hacks: write down past sufferings and trace the growth from them, and ask what the positive of an annoyance is, which became instantaneous after a year (pp. 152–154). Acceptance of the unchangeable rests on "embracing death": there is no legacy, the universe has been around ten billion years, and life is a firefly's blink (p. 154). Unhappiness does nobody any favors; the Sumerians are the example of a civilization nobody can name a member of (p. 155). The closing instruction is to enjoy oneself, project love, laugh, appreciate the moment, and do the work (p. 155).

### Part II: Saving Yourself (pp. 157–192)

**Choosing to Be Yourself (pp. 158–160).** The section opens with a list, doctors will not make you healthy and so on, ending in the instruction to save yourself (p. 157). Nobody needs to do anything; the only thing to do is what one wants (p. 158). Nobody can beat a person at being that person; emulating is "a fool's errand", and each person is unique from "the combinatorics" of DNA and development (p. 158). The goal in life is to find the people, business, project, or art that needs you most, and to avoid checklists and frameworks built on what others do (p. 159). An original contribution needs irrational obsession (p. 160).

**Choosing to Care for Yourself (pp. 161–167).** Priority order: physical health, mental health, spiritual health, family's health, family's wellbeing, then everything else (p. 161). Modern life diverges from evolution: a diet closer to paleo, play instead of treadmills, shoes, warmth, sterile environments and "the hygiene hypothesis", small tribes, phones every five minutes, evolved for scarcity and living in abundance (pp. 161–162). Outside math, physics, and chemistry there is little "settled science" on diet (p. 162). The ketogenic diet seems hard to follow; the interplay of sugar and fat matters, the sugar effect dominates, and any sensible diet avoids the two together; he is "not an expert" (pp. 163–164). Tweets: quality control over quantity, fasting easier than portion control, Wonder Bread, ditch the extremists and recently invented foods, subtract before adding, the more processed the less to eat (pp. 164–165). Exercise: the daily morning workout is the habit with the most impact; not having time means "not a priority"; he does not start the day until he has worked out and takes breaks he can count on one hand (pp. 165–166). What the workout is almost does not matter; the best one is the one a person is excited to do daily (p. 166). A tweet lists the merits of walking meetings (p. 166). Jerzy Gregorek's "Easy choices, hard life" and its reverse, applied to food, values, savings, relationships (pp. 166–167).

**Meditation + Mental Strength (pp. 168–178).** A tweet defines an emotion as evolved biology predicting the impact of a current event, usually "exaggerated or wrong" in modern settings (p. 168). Breath is where the autonomic and voluntary systems meet; relaxed breathing frees resources for the immune system, with the caveat that this does not beat an illness (p. 168). Wim Hof, described as the ice-bath record holder, inspired cold showers; the lesson is that most suffering "comes from avoidance" (pp. 169–170). Meditation is "intermittent fasting" for the mind (p. 170). Everyone claims to meditate and few do; of four forms tried, Choiceless Awareness works best for him, and watching thoughts shows most of them to be fear-based (pp. 170–171). Quieting the mind pops one out of one's own story and shows how far up Maslow's hierarchy life already is (pp. 171–172). The method he recommends: sit with eyes closed for an hour a day, surrender, let thoughts run; unresolved things surface "like barnacles" and like "a giant inbox" of old email until inbox zero arrives; sixty days of one hour each morning (pp. 172–173). Meditation is not hard; it is sitting and doing nothing (p. 173). Osho's lecture on the spiritual attraction of drugs: drugs, thrills, and flow states are attempts to escape the voice in the head (p. 174). Meditation's advantage is seeing how out of control the mind is, a monkey "flinging feces"; awareness alone calms (pp. 174–175). Running the brain "in debug mode" while brushing teeth; most of what the brain runs off to do need not be tackled in that moment (pp. 175–176). The mind as a kernel-level OS of calm awareness with a "monkey mind" application on top, to be activated only when needed (pp. 176–177). The mind is a muscle that can be trained and its program rewritten (p. 178). Tweets: meditation works only when done for its own sake; hiking, journaling, praying, showering, and sitting quietly as forms of it (p. 178).

**Choosing to Build Yourself (pp. 178–182).** The greatest superpower is the ability to change oneself (p. 178). Biggest mistake: an early company that did well for others, a lawsuit pursued with angst and anger; the outcome was fine and the regret is the emotion (pp. 179–180). Habits are everything; a daily light workout from his trainer showed him so, and sustainable change is "a ten-year journey" of six-month steps (pp. 180–181). Krishnamurti's internal state of revolution: saying one will try is "wimping out"; commit externally, as with quitting smoking, or set a smaller goal honestly (pp. 181–182). Impatience with actions and patience with results: do things now and well, then wait, because markets take a long time to adopt products and inspiration "is perishable" (p. 182).

**Choosing to Grow Yourself (pp. 183–187).** He does not believe in specific goals and cites Scott Adams's line on systems rather than goals; the aim is to be successful in 999 of 1,000 lives, not a billionaire but failing in few places (p. 183). If he could make it from a poor childhood in India, anybody can, given a functioning body and mind (p. 184). Learning: stay on the basics; science is "the study of truth" and the most devotional thing he does (pp. 184–185). Most reading is designed for social approval; the returns are outside the herd, and the smartest people he knows "started out as losers" (pp. 185–186). Self-improvement without self-discipline comes from updating the self-image (p. 186). Motivation is relative; find the thing (p. 186). To his kids: read everything, there is no such thing as junk; then mathematics and persuasion, with statistics and probability known inside out (pp. 186–187).

**Choosing to Free Yourself (pp. 187–192).** The hardest thing is knowing what you want; there are no "adults" (p. 187). Freedom moved from "freedom to" to "freedom from": from reaction, anger, sadness, being forced (p. 188). He does not measure his effectiveness and calls self-measurement a form of self-punishment (p. 188). Others' expectations are their problem; an agreement is yours (p. 188). Courage is "not caring" what other people think (p. 189). Naval is impatient and willful, leaves events the moment they waste time, and says time is all a person has (p. 189). Nobody is responsible for making other people happy (p. 189). Anger is a precursor to violence and a contract with oneself to stay in turmoil until reality changes (pp. 189–190). Living far below one's means buys freedom; a taste of freedom "can make you unemployable" (p. 190). The monkey mind begins at puberty with desire; the mind is meant to be "a servant", not a master (pp. 190–191). Self-awareness has no endpoint; being aware of being "a rat in a race" is as far as most get (p. 191). The modern struggle: lone individuals with willpower against scientists and statisticians weaponizing food, screens, and medicine (p. 192).

### Part II: Philosophy (pp. 193–203)

Three answers to the meaning of life: it is personal and must be found by digging with the question; there is none, since a person is dead for the seventy billion years to the heat death and any meaning is "turtles all the way down"; and a physics answer, that living systems locally reverse entropy while accelerating the heat death that makes everything one thing (pp. 194–196). Rational Buddhism: keep what can be verified (meditation, a base layer of awareness), reject what cannot (past lives, chakras), reconcile with evolution; try everything, test it, keep what is useful (pp. 199–200). Wisdom is understanding the long-term consequences of one's actions (p. 200). If wisdom could be imparted in words, everyone would be done (p. 201). The present is all there is; his past "is dead"; a line credited to Homer's *Iliad* on beauty and doom (p. 202). Inspiration is perishable, so act on it at once (pp. 202–203).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the compiler or the subject limits the claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **The text itself.** Jorgenson says the excerpts were edited "(multiple times)", that some are secondhand, that he cannot be certain of every source, that everything is "taken out of context", and that errors are "(very) possible" (Important Notes, pp. 9–10). Ferriss tells the reader to follow the advice only if it survives scrutiny and stress-testing in their own life (Foreword, p. 15). Naval says he cannot teach, only offer "a few hooks" (Now Here Is Naval, p. 25), and that a tweet without the experience behind it reads as "a collection of quotes" (Building Judgment, p. 107).
- **Hard work.** The skill matters more than the hours, but hard work matters and "you can't skimp on it" (Building Wealth, p. 30); the tweetstorm says to work as hard as you can (p. 36); and without hard work there is neither judgment nor leverage (Building Judgment, p. 95).
- **Not everyone founds a company.** Naval concedes some people are not ready to be entrepreneurs (Building Wealth, p. 76); stock options at a tech company are "a fine way to start" (p. 54); and the consistently most successful people in Silicon Valley are venture capitalists and those who join a company just after product/market fit, not founders (pp. 81–82).
- **The 99 percent.** He is not saying to skip the 99 percent, because the one percent cannot be identified in advance (Building Wealth, p. 49).
- **Labor and capital.** Labor is the worst leverage, yet the top of his own real estate ladder is a company with "the highest-quality labor" and investors' capital beside its code (Building Wealth, p. 66), and the developer on that ladder takes a loan or raises money (p. 65).
- **Networking.** Business networking is a waste, but the compound-interest chapter rests on a decade-long deal relationship with Elad Gil (Building Wealth, pp. 47–48), the advice to a young employee is that the alumni network is the most important thing about a job (p. 82), and once someone is met, trust is judged by signals (p. 86).
- **Status games.** They are "a necessary evil" that society uses to decide who is in charge, and they will always exist (Building Wealth, p. 74).
- **Accountability.** The downside is real and the discussion is limited to "a business context", where the founder is the last paid (Building Wealth, pp. 51–52); the ruin to avoid includes jail, catastrophic loss, and the body (pp. 66–67).
- **The hourly rate.** Even with a $5,000 figure he still did "stupid things" like arguing with the electrician (Building Wealth, p. 71).
- **Specific knowledge is not for everyone in every field.** If a person is not already good at selling or into it, maybe it is not their thing (Building Wealth, p. 43).
- **Prerequisites.** There are prerequisites a person "can't get past": limbs, mental faculties, an education (Saving Yourself, p. 184).
- **Happiness is personal.** No answer applies to everybody, and his own definition "keeps evolving" (Learning Happiness, p. 128); readers are free to disagree (p. 129).
- **Meditation's limits.** It helps, but "amazing how little"; a wrong word returns anyone to the ego (Learning Happiness, p. 132). His acceptance hacks are not "totally successful" (p. 152). He does not expect to reach enlightenment and calls himself a rat who might look at the clouds (Saving Yourself, p. 191).
- **External action is still required.** Naval says people are meant to do something and to self-actualize (Learning Happiness, p. 138); it is "glib" to say the drive can be switched off (p. 140).
- **The monkey mind has a job.** It "serves incredible purpose" (Saving Yourself, p. 177) and is good for planning and problem-solving (p. 191). Habits are necessary to function (Building Judgment, p. 101).
- **Depression.** The point about Indian family life making sadness hard excludes people with "chemical depression" (Saving Yourself, p. 162), and family closeness trades away privacy (p. 162).
- **Diet and health.** There is little "settled science" on diet (Saving Yourself, p. 162); he is "not an expert" (p. 163); relaxed breathing does not mean beating an illness (p. 168); he is "strongly against" ice baths and only takes cold showers (p. 169); psychedelics are not recommended (Recommended Reading, p. 216).
- **Money's share of happiness.** Money is "a very small piece" of his own score (Learning Happiness, p. 128), yet it solves external problems and buys freedom (Building Wealth, pp. 89–90).
- **Reading.** Modern problems need modern books (Building Judgment, p. 123); the one-point-per-book rule is for nonfiction only (p. 117); and the disclaimer says interpretations will change over time (Important Notes, p. 9).
- **Anger and values.** Anger "was good" when he was young (Philosophy, p. 197); his values are things he will not compromise on rather than a classical definition (p. 198).
- **Rational Buddhism.** What cannot be verified might be true or false and is set aside as "not falsifiable" rather than denied (Philosophy, p. 199).
- **Not all-or-nothing.** Doing things for their own sake is a direction, not "100 percent-or-nothing" (Building Wealth, p. 79); a smaller change one can carry out beats a commitment one cannot (Saving Yourself, p. 182).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, example, or source the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes it, and the reason is given.

- **The book is free and Naval earns nothing from it (copyright page, p. 4).** Holds. navalmanack.com states in September 2026 that the whole book is free to read on the site with PDF and e-reader downloads, and that Naval does not earn money from the project. Retailer and summary pages report more than a million copies sold; no first-party sales figure was found, so the count is unchecked.
- **Naval as CEO and co-founder of AngelList (Foreword, p. 14) and as founder and chairman (Now Here Is Naval, p. 25).** Changed before the book was printed. Avlok Kohli has been AngelList's CEO since 2019; Naval is listed as co-founder and chairman (AngelList's Confidential 2023 speaker page for Kohli; Kohli's December 2024 written testimony to the House Financial Services Committee names him CEO). The foreword's title was stale at publication.
- **A personal investor in about two hundred companies (Now Here Is Naval, p. 25).** Unchecked; no later first-party count was found.
- **Twitter, the handle @Naval, and Twitter.com/Naval (copyright page, p. 4; Next on Naval, p. 227), and Twitter throughout.** Twitter was rebranded X on July 23, 2023, and twitter.com became x.com on May 17, 2024 (Wikipedia, "Twitter"). Every reference to tweeting and to the platform names a product that no longer carries that name.
- **Joe Rogan earning fifty to a hundred million dollars a year from his podcast (Building Wealth, p. 59).** Later, and larger: Spotify's 2020 exclusive was reported above $200 million over three and a half years, and the February 2024 renewal is reported at up to $250 million over several years with the show no longer exclusive (Variety, "Joe Rogan's Spotify Deal Renewal Worth Up to $250 Million"; Forbes, February 2, 2024). The direction of the example holds.
- **PewDiePie as "bigger than the news" (Building Wealth, p. 59).** Aged. PewDiePie holds about 109 million subscribers in September 2026 and ranks around sixteenth by subscribers, having semi-retired in 2020; MrBeast has been the most-subscribed channel since June 2024 and passed 500 million subscribers in June 2026 (Epidemic Sound's 2026 ranking; communitrics.com channel ranking). The leverage point stands on a different name.
- **Charlie Munger described as Buffett's partner (Building Judgment, p. 106); Buffett as the standing example of judgment and reputation (Building Wealth, pp. 68, 85; Building Judgment, p. 105).** Munger died on November 28, 2023, at 99 (Wikipedia, "Charlie Munger"). Buffett stepped down as Berkshire Hathaway's CEO at the end of 2025, with Greg Abel taking over and Buffett staying on as chairman (CBS News; CNBC, January 2, 2026).
- **Wim Hof as holder of the world record for the longest ice bath (Saving Yourself, p. 169).** Already broken at printing and many times since. Hof's 2010 record was 44 minutes; Josef Köberl of Austria set 2 hours 8 minutes in August 2019 and 2 hours 30 minutes in 2020, and the record stood above five hours as of 2025 (CBS News, August 10, 2019; Wikipedia, "Wim Hof").
- **Breathing and cold exposure redirecting energy to the immune system (Saving Yourself, pp. 168–169).** The one controlled study is Kox et al., PNAS, March 2014: trained volunteers using Hof's breathing, cold exposure, and meditation showed increased epinephrine, more anti-inflammatory mediators, and a dampened pro-inflammatory response to injected endotoxin. The study measured attenuation of an innate immune response in a lab setting, not resistance to illness; Naval's own caveat on p. 168 covers the gap.
- **"the hygiene hypothesis" (Saving Yourself, p. 162).** The framing has moved. Strachan's 1989 hypothesis was refined by Graham Rook's 2003 "old friends" hypothesis, and current reviews hold that early exposure to diverse friendly microbes, not to infectious pathogens, trains the immune system (Wikipedia, "Old Friends hypothesis"; PNAS, "Cleaning up the hygiene hypothesis", 2017).
- **Screen activities linked to less happiness with "No exceptions" (Learning Happiness, p. 150).** Contested. Twenge and Campbell's 2018 population study found the association; Orben and Przybylski's 2019 study in *Psychological Science*, across three national datasets (N = 17,247) with time-use diaries, found associations below their preregistered threshold of r = .10, and meta-analyses put the global effect near r = −0.10 to −0.15. The no-exceptions wording is not what the later evidence shows.
- **Serotonin from sunlight, exercise, positive thinking, and tryptophan (Learning Happiness, p. 151).** These are the four factors in Simon Young's 2007 review, "How to increase serotonin in the human brain without drugs", *Journal of Psychiatry and Neuroscience* 32(6):394–399. The tweet does not cite it; the source exists.
- **Humans evolved in "tribes of 150" (Building Judgment, p. 111).** Contested. Lindenfors, Wartel, and Lind, *Biology Letters*, May 2021, reran the primate analysis with updated data and got 95 percent confidence intervals from 2 to 520 people, concluding the number has a limited theoretical foundation and lacks empirical support.
- **The universe's age given as ten billion years with ten billion to go (Learning Happiness, p. 154), and as ten billion years past with seventy billion until the heat death (Philosophy, p. 194).** Neither figure matches the accepted values: the Planck 2018 release puts the age at 13.8 billion years (Wikipedia, "Age of the universe"), and heat-death timescales run to about 10^100 years, when the largest black holes evaporate (Wikipedia, "Heat death of the universe"). The two passages also disagree with each other.
- **The line credited to Homer's *Iliad* about everything being more beautiful "because we're doomed" (Philosophy, p. 202).** Misattributed. The line is Achilles's in the 2004 film *Troy*, from David Benioff's screenplay (IMDb, *Troy* quotes). It is not in the *Iliad*.
- **The saying attributed to Confucius about two lives (Learning Happiness, p. 139).** The interviewer's attribution, not Naval's. No source in the Analects or any classical text was found; the saying circulates online under Confucius and other names (Patheos, "Two Lives. One Life. A Small Meditation On Attribution"). Unchecked beyond that.
- **Sources on Periscope: [1], [2], [10], [73], [74] (Sources, pp. 233, 234, 239).** Periscope shut down on March 31, 2021 (9to5Mac, March 31, 2021; Wikipedia, "Periscope (app)"). - **The Navalmanack shorts on education, AngelList, investing, startups, crypto, and relationships (Next on Naval, p. 227).** Unchecked; the site was fetched for the free-download statement only.
- **Eric Jorgenson on Zaarly's founding team, writing the Evergreen blog (About the Author, p. 241).** Moved on. ## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **The evidence base is one person's testimony, at second hand.** Every claim is Naval's recollection or maxim, assembled by a compiler from edited transcripts, some of them other writers quoting him, with the questions of several interviewers merged into one voice (Important Notes, pp. 9–11). No figure in the book comes from a study the book cites; the Sources appendix lists interviews, tweets, and blog posts (Sources, pp. 233–239). Where the claims meet outside evidence in section 7, three are wrong (the *Iliad* line, the age of the universe, the Wim Hof record) and two are contested (screen time, the tribe of 150).
- **Correlation presented as cause.** Reading one to two hours a day is said to account for "any material success" (Building Judgment, p. 116); the daily workout is credited with a physical and mental transformation (Saving Yourself, p. 180); optimists are said to do better in the long run (Building Wealth, p. 72); and all screen activity is linked to less happiness with "No exceptions" (Learning Happiness, p. 150). None carries a control or a source, and the last is contradicted by the larger later dataset.
- **Survivorship.** The examples of leverage are Rogan, PewDiePie, Bezos, Zuckerberg, Page, Brin, Gates, and Jobs (Building Wealth, pp. 59–60); the example of judgment is Buffett (pp. 68, 85); the example of joining at the right time is a call from Zuckerberg to Jim Breyer (p. 82). The claim that great people have great outcomes rests on the people Naval met twenty years earlier who succeeded (p. 87); the capable people who did not are not counted. The claim that the skill can be developed by anyone (p. 30) rests on one life, and the concession that prerequisites exist (Saving Yourself, p. 184) is not carried back into Part I.
- **Figures without a source.** Judgment right 85 percent of the time instead of 75 is worth fifty to two hundred million dollars (Building Wealth, p. 68); right 80 percent instead of 70 is worth "hundreds of times more" (Building Judgment, p. 105); a single engineer can create half a billion dollars of value (Building Wealth, p. 61); 99 percent of effort is wasted (p. 48); 90 percent of thoughts are fear-based (Saving Yourself, p. 171); 95 percent of what the brain runs off to do is unnecessary (p. 176); the placebo effect is "100 percent effective" for the mind (Learning Happiness, p. 146). Each is stated as a fact and none is measured.
- **Facts that are wrong or dated at printing.** A screenplay line is credited to Homer (Philosophy, p. 202). The universe's age is given as ten billion years and the heat death as seventy billion years away, on two pages that also disagree with each other (Learning Happiness, p. 154; Philosophy, p. 194). Wim Hof's ice record had been beaten in 2019, before the 2020 printing (Saving Yourself, p. 169). The foreword names Naval CEO of AngelList (Foreword, p. 14) while the body names him chairman (Now Here Is Naval, p. 25), and the CEO title had passed to someone else in 2019. The Confucius attribution is the interviewer's and has no classical source (Learning Happiness, p. 139).
- **Arithmetic understated.** A business of one hundred users growing 20 percent a month is said to reach millions "very, very quickly" (Building Judgment, p. 110); at that rate one million users takes about fifty months.
- **Internal tensions.** Hard work is "really overrated" (Building Judgment, p. 94) beside the instruction to work as hard as you can (Building Wealth, p. 36) and the statement that without hard work there is no judgment or leverage (Building Judgment, p. 95). Self-measurement is rejected (Saving Yourself, p. 188; Building Wealth, p. 75) beside a habit-change tweetstorm whose fifth step is to track meticulously (Learning Happiness, p. 151). Networking is a waste (Building Wealth, p. 85) beside a chapter built on a decade of deals with Elad Gil (pp. 47–48) and the advice that a job's alumni network is its most important feature (p. 82). Naval reads one to two hours a day and credits it with his success (Building Judgment, p. 116) and also reads maybe two chapters of many books and says he has read them (pp. 117–118). He avoids self-identifying "on almost any level" (p. 102) and names his philosophy "Rational Buddhism" (Philosophy, p. 199). Readers are told to ignore their contemporaries and the news (Building Judgment, p. 118) and then given a list of blogs and Twitter accounts (Recommended Reading, pp. 220–222). Labor is the worst leverage (Building Wealth, p. 58) while the top rung of his own example is a company of engineers, designers, and marketers (p. 66) and he is founder and chairman of a company (Now Here Is Naval, p. 25).
- **Claims the argument does not reach.** The wealth section assumes the reader has, or can build, code or media leverage; the laborer, contractor, and customer-service worker in its own examples are told only where they stand (Building Wealth, pp. 62, 64–65). The sample is Silicon Valley, by the author's own note that it is the ecosystem he is "most familiar with" (p. 52), and the forgiveness of failure and the wipe-clean bankruptcy are asserted for "good ecosystems" only (p. 52). The diet chapter opens by disclaiming expertise (Saving Yourself, p. 163) and then calls the sugar-and-fat combination "really deadly" (p. 163) and paleo "probably correct" (p. 162) with no source. The principal-agent chapter says small companies make everyone feel like a principal (Building Judgment, p. 109) without saying at what size that stops.
- **Repetition and padding.** Several tweets appear twice in the body: the desire-as-contract line twice on one page (Learning Happiness, p. 138), the working-for-life line in the body and again in Naval's Rules (p. 148; Naval's Writing, p. 224), and the perishable-inspiration line three times across three pages (Saving Yourself, p. 182; Philosophy, pp. 202–203). The reading section runs eleven pages on how to read (Building Judgment, pp. 114–124) and the recommended-reading appendix another sixteen (Recommended Reading, pp. 207–222); the wealth argument proper is about forty.
- **The compiler's own warning is the strongest critique in the book.** A maxim without the experience behind it reads as "a collection of quotes" (Building Judgment, p. 107), and the disclaimer asks that nothing be cited from the text without a primary source (Important Notes, p. 9). Five of those primary sources sit on a platform that closed in 2021 and one on a domain that no longer resolves (section 7), so for a reader in 2026 the warning cannot be followed for much of the book.
