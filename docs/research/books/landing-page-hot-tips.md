First published September 2020, last updated April 2021; copyright 2021 One Page Love. ISBN 978-1-64999-225-3. ## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, tip by tip, with its own exceptions, what has changed since April 2021, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that a landing page is a "single canvas" for persuading a visitor to do "one thing" (Tip #09, PDF p. 29), and that it is strengthened by understanding rather than by any single trick: "It Depends" is called the best and worst advice there is, and "context is everything" (Foreword, PDF p. 10). Before any tip applies, the reader is told to answer three questions and "set them in stone": who the target demographic is, what their problem is, and how the offering solves it (Foreword, PDF p. 10). The hundred tips that follow are offered as things to read "a handful at a time" and implement where they resonate (Foreword, PDF p. 10). The evidence throughout is the author's own practice, his curated collections of landing pages at One Page Love, screenshots of named products, and a handful of results reported to him by other founders.

The author says what the claim is not. The goal is not a "money-maker overnight"; it is stronger pages "through understanding. And context." (Foreword, PDF p. 11). A hundred tips "can be overwhelming", so it is not a checklist to apply whole (Foreword, PDF p. 10). It is not a book about traffic: copy that speaks to the visitor's problem is "a much smarter play" than chasing search rankings, summed up as "Word of mouth > SEO" (Tip #84, PDF p. 156). And it is not a book about polish for its own sake: a reader who is new to landing pages or "not making sales" is told to skip flair and spend the time on a demo or "a remarkable product" (Tip #96, PDF p. 178).

## 4. The argument, tip by tip

The book has no chapters and no grouping of its own beyond the numbered order, so this section follows that order in runs of roughly ten tips. Each tip is one to five PDF pages: a heading, a few paragraphs, usually a screenshot, and often a list of tools and of the author's own curated collections at One Page Love.

### Foreword (PDF pp. 10–11)

"It Depends." Context decides everything in landing page optimization, so the reader answers three questions first: who the target demographic is, what their problem is, how the offering solves it (Foreword, PDF p. 10). Then the question of what that demographic would "need to see and read" to be "persuaded to go all the way" (PDF p. 10). The book exists for the reader who is unsure of that answer, and it is meant to be read a handful of tips at a time (PDF p. 10). The goal is strengthening through understanding, "And context" (PDF p. 11).

### Tips #01–#10 (PDF pp. 12–32)

**#01, utilize customer testimonials** (PDF pp. 12–13): select testimonials that highlight a feature and answer a doubt. Two invented customers are contrasted: "Gavin Jenkins", whose quote is generic, and "Kim Davis", whose quote names "superb quality" and "thoughtful packaging" and so answers whether postage would damage the item (PDF pp. 12–13). Keep "only the choice few" (PDF p. 13). **#02, testimonials from a similar demographic** (PDF pp. 14–16): an enterprise buyer wants to see other enterprises took the risk; the example testimonial gains "significant weight" once "Dave Lewis" is labeled VP of Customer Relations at Starbucks (PDF p. 15). Avataaars is offered for missing customer photos, with the rule not to mix illustrated and real faces (PDF p. 16). **#03, fewer images, better images** (PDF pp. 17–19): good imagery builds trust, and trust is "the foundation for conversions"; a professional photoshoot's return is "pretty much guaranteed" (PDF p. 17). Tools: ImageOptim, Optimage, Stocksy, Squareshot, Noun Project Photos "around $33/image", Unsplash, Pixabay, Pexels, IMGIX (PDF pp. 17–19). **#04, spice up call-to-action buttons** (PDF pp. 20–21): "Click Here" and "Sign Up" are "too bland"; use actionable phrases such as unlocking creativity "for only $19" (PDF p. 20). The screenshot on PDF p. 21 pairs three plain "Sign Up" buttons with what "the big dogs" use: Treehouse's "Claim Your Free Trial", Netflix's "Create An Account", Basecamp's "Give Basecamp a Try". **#05, when in doubt, double the padding** (PDF pp. 22–23): whitespace is "breathing room" for the potential customer, not only for the content; an overwhelming page is fixed by doubling the padding (PDF p. 22). **#06, empathize with the visitor's problem** (PDF pp. 24–25): open with the problem and put the plain description in the subtext; a "most innovative" invoicing headline plus a jargon paragraph is set against a one-line question about "chasing late client payments" plus one sentence (PDF p. 24). Title advice from Annie Maguire and the Headlime headline generator are linked (PDF p. 25). **#07, avoid center-aligned or justified paragraphs** (PDF pp. 26–27): both alignments fatigue a reader in long text; the same Neil deGrasse Tyson quotation is set four ways, and the rule of thumb is that any paragraph over two lines is left- or right-aligned (PDF p. 27). **#08, replace, don't add** (PDF p. 28): eight testimonials do not need a ninth; persuade "with as little as possible". **#09, set a single objective** (PDF p. 29): selling an ebook and promoting a job board, against selling the ebook alone; a landing page is a "single canvas" for "one thing". **#10, create a text color hierarchy** (PDF pp. 30–32): black text at "maximum contrast" on white is "the biggest tell" of an inexperienced builder; soften it with an off-white ground and a gray hierarchy (PDF pp. 30–31). Contrast for Mac and an accessible color generator keep the hierarchy "within Accessibility standards" (PDF pp. 31–32).

### Tips #11–#20 (PDF pp. 33–50)

**#11, remove your main nav** (PDF p. 33): a landing page inside a bigger site hides the site navigation, because sending a visitor to another page is not its one objective. **#12, use footer for pre-sale** (PDF pp. 34–36): a visitor who has not committed by the end of the scroll "still has doubts", so the footer carries FAQs and support details; that area can be the difference between a visitor and a customer (PDF pp. 34, 36). **#13, highlight USP among feature list/grid** (PDF pp. 37–38): a grid of twelve features overwhelms a first-time visitor; lead with the unique selling propositions (PDF p. 37). **#14, offer more pricing tiers** (PDF pp. 39–41): tier up with more benefits rather than down by stripping the core, with Adam Wathan's line from Refactoring UI about giving willing customers the chance "to pay you more money" (PDF pp. 40–41); Help Scout's pricing-psychology piece and the author's pricing-table collections are linked (PDF p. 41). **#15, use specific coupons** (PDF p. 42): platform-specific codes such as "Twitter10", "Insta10", "LinkedIn10", "NYTimes10" reveal which channels work "while feeling exclusive". **#16, embed your lead forms** (PDF pp. 43–44): an inline email field and button beats a link to a signup page, because every page load costs conversion (PDF p. 44). **#17, if you market to everyone, it resonates with no one** (PDF pp. 45–46): copy for a Lord of the Rings fan club reads "Enter Hobbiton for $9/month", and the test is "These are my people?" (PDF pp. 45–46). **#18, sit with someone and see what it takes to convert** (PDF p. 47): watching someone use the page is "truly horrifying", and the learnings "worth the trauma". **#19, set a max-width typography** (PDF p. 48): full-width paragraphs are hard to read on large screens; aim for "55–100 characters per line"; the author's email drip runs about 60 and the PDF about 75. **#20, highlight a testimonial from an opinion leader** (PDF pp. 49–50): an invented icon pack, "ZenIcons" at $49, is endorsed by "Klaudia", a designer at Spotify, and this form of social proof is "the game-changing Joker card" (PDF pp. 49–50); the tip comes from the author's WordCamp Berlin talk (PDF p. 50).

### Tips #21–#30 (PDF pp. 51–65)

**#21, avoid animation overkill** (PDF p. 51): gratuitous scroll transitions "no longer impress"; being able to does not mean one should. **#22, consider a color scheme** (PDF pp. 52–54): a crafted scheme signals an effort the visitor "will assume carries over" to the product. **#23, avoid the word cheap** (PDF p. 55): "More affordable", "Economical", "Cost-effective"; positioning as cheaper invites the worry that the competition is "more quality". **#24, create haste** (PDF p. 56): a discount "within a diminishing timeframe"; the book's own pre-order ran a $30 saving for 30 hours with a countdown under the button. **#25, integrate a sticky header navigation** (PDF p. 57): on a long page it keeps the CTA "visible at all times". **#26, use fewer fonts** (PDF p. 58): several typefaces in several weights add load time; pair one primary face with a system font. **#27, step into your visitor's shoes** (PDF p. 59): load on mobile, read aloud, use the navigation, check out. **#28, space using ratios** (PDF pp. 60–61): a base of 8px, gaps at 8, 16, 32 and 64, citing Elliot Dahl's 8-point grid piece (PDF pp. 60–61). **#29, add personality** (PDF pp. 62–63): a portrait, a desk photo, an animated signature; "People want to support people" (PDF p. 62); Fiverr, VideoTouch and Seth Godin's "Sign your work" are linked (PDF pp. 62–63). **#30, curate your FAQs** (PDF pp. 64–65): "Frequently asked only", "Ordered by popularity", pre-sale questions only, with post-sale moved to a support page (PDF pp. 64–65).

### Tips #31–#40 (PDF pp. 66–83)

**#31, replace GIFs with video** (PDF p. 66): better performance, more colors, smaller files, pausable; GIFs being easier to produce is "not a reason". **#32, offer an annual discount** (PDF p. 67): a saving on a twelve-month commitment helps "boost cash flow" for a SaaS; Unbounce's switcher carries a "Save 10%" badge; keep the switcher legible and "tested on mobile thoroughly". **#33, deconstruct your About paragraph** (PDF p. 68): strip an invented studio's "multi-faceted" mission sentence to one that says it "builds goal-tracking software", then add one adjective. **#34, run a regular speed test** (PDF p. 69): Google PageSpeed Insights and Pingdom; poor results "act as a to-do-list". **#35, put your name on it** (PDF pp. 70–72): visitors ask whether they will get help and "Who actually built this?"; taking ownership "eases fears and shows confidence" (PDF pp. 70–72). **#36, align with a grid** (PDF pp. 73–76): Material Design on columns, gutters and margins; the CSS Grid guide; the 1200px and 960px systems "we all used to reference" (PDF pp. 75–76). **#37, add a splash of color** (PDF pp. 77–78): a burst commands attention, and when excited "we tend to try new things" (PDF p. 78). **#38, invest in an icon library** (PDF pp. 79–80): Iconfinder, Streamline's "30,000+" icons in three weights, Font Awesome (PDF pp. 79–80). **#39, place copy before images on mobile** (PDF pp. 81–82): intro copy before the main image, feature copy before the demo image; images or text that add nothing on mobile may be hidden; flexbox `order` is the mechanism (PDF p. 82). **#40, offer a free teaser** (PDF p. 83): a 14-day trial, two of fourteen chapters, 80 of the icons; teasers let visitors experience more "without having to pay upfront".

### Tips #41–#50 (PDF pp. 84–97)

**#41, code your own social share buttons** (PDF p. 84): embedded share widgets "add unnecessary requests"; the author's vote is "removing them completely", otherwise hard-code them with pre-composed text. **#42, add Open Graph meta tags** (PDF p. 85): links with images and text are "clicked more" than links without; fifteen minutes here "is worth it"; the book's own card adds "Subscribe Free" to the description. **#43, emphasize time-saving** (PDF p. 86): the first three of nine features on Ghost's Memberships page concern time; time is "the most valuable commodity". **#44, tighten your big type** (PDF pp. 87–88): kerning versus tracking, and reducing letter-spacing in large intro copy (PDF pp. 87–88). **#45, don't neglect Retina optimization** (PDF pp. 89–90): `<picture>` and `srcset` for @2x images, SVG for icons (PDF pp. 89–90). **#46, remove the obstacles to demo** (PDF p. 91): Carrd lets a visitor build a whole site before it asks for an email address. **#47, lower the risk to commit** (PDF p. 92): "Free trial", "No credit card needed", "24/7 support", "30-day money-back guarantee"; Squarespace shows two beside its button. **#48, keep brand capitalization consistent** (PDF p. 93): "Starbucks", "Star Bucks", "starbucks", "StarBucks", "STARBUCKS"; "Avoid ALL CAPS if you can". **#49, keep vertical spacing even** (PDF p. 94): one consistent rule between sections. **#50, suggest a pricing option** (PDF pp. 95–97): many visitors do not want "to spend time investigating"; Lee Robinson says a green-highlighted tier "hugely impacted his course sales", Refactoring UI enlarges a tier, Google draws an on-brand border (PDF pp. 95–97).

### Tips #51–#60 (PDF pp. 98–113)

**#51, increase demand by limiting supply** (PDF pp. 98–99): Headlime sold a limited number of $129 lifetime spots, and founder Danny said it "worked wonders for the launch" after the $49 and $89 tiers sold out; the same applies to limiting time and to showing stock remaining (PDF pp. 98–99). **#52, add social proof** (PDF p. 100): "herd mentality"; ratings, rankings, testimonials, client logos, awards, press, critic reviews; Shoutout's embeddable "Wall of Love". **#53, focus on solving copy first** (PDF p. 101): polishing a badly worded page is "a waste of time"; what will you solve, where will you take them, who will they become. **#54, consider dark mode** (PDF pp. 102–104): there is "something remarkable" about a strong dark scheme; try it for thirty minutes (PDF pp. 102–104). **#55, align your marketing** (PDF p. 105): an ad for freelance time-tracking that lands on a page about teams is confusing; the aim is not to lose visitors "upon arrival". **#56, increase the contrast of your button text** (PDF pp. 106–107): beginners keep the global link color inside buttons, producing low-contrast CTAs; Contrast for Mac keeps text "within accessibility guidelines" (PDF pp. 106–107). **#57, only A/B test high traffic pages** (PDF p. 108): low data yields "inconclusive and even misleading results"; optimize and market the primary page until traffic grows; Google Optimize is the linked primer. **#58, minimize the options** (PDF p. 109): five plans, seven required fields, sixteen colors; Hick's Law, decision time rising with "the number and complexity of choices", via Laws of UX. **#59, try font smoothing** (PDF p. 110): the CSS `font-smooth` property trims "chunky" fonts slightly; links an eleven-year-old explainer and Mozilla's property guide. **#60, liven with illustrations** (PDF pp. 111–113): the book's own fire illustration cost $4 on Creative Market; character illustrations are "on-trend" and "becoming generic but definitely still work"; Streamline UX, Blush, Humaaans, unDraw (PDF pp. 111–113).

### Tips #61–#70 (PDF pp. 114–131)

**#61, trim the fat** (PDF pp. 114–115): paste the `<head>` into an editor and ask of each line what it does and whether it is needed; this uncovers scripts, font weights and snippets that add "nothing but load time" (PDF p. 115). **#62, include an email within error messages** (PDF pp. 116–117): a support address in a payment error can turn "disgruntled visitors into happy customers" (PDF p. 117). **#63, add a radial burst behind product imagery** (PDF pp. 118–120): a CSS gradient and a drop shadow add depth; before-and-after screenshots of the book's own page (PDF pp. 118–120). **#64, delay the chatbot** (PDF p. 121): fire it only once the visitor reaches pricing, FAQs or the footer; a chat notification on arrival "wrecks concentration". **#65, offer a demo down-sell** (PDF p. 122): a buyer's guide download for the visitor who does not want a call; Kevin Mead reported it did "wonder for additional revenue". **#66, open non-essential links in a new tab** (PDF p. 123): documentation, support, privacy and demos open in a new tab; the tip links "a strong Counterargument" by Jesse and keeps its vote for demos, support portals and blogs. **#67, seek hero images with negative space** (PDF pp. 124–125): shoot or crop for copy space; Photoshop's content-aware fill; Unsplash and Stocksy tags (PDF pp. 124–125). **#68, hint to scroll** (PDF pp. 126–127): a down arrow, a Learn More button, avoiding a full-height hero, or letting hero elements flow into the next section, as Ghost's Memberships page did (PDF pp. 126–127). **#69, make it accessible** (PDF pp. 128–129): five tasks, a clear `:focus` state, text contrast, browser font-size zoom, semantic heading order, `alt` text on non-decorative images; one hour on these could save others "hundreds of frustrating hours" (PDF p. 128). Lighthouse, Contrast for Mac, Eric Bailey on focus styles, a screen-reader round-up, and the UserWay widget "for ADA & WCAG Compliance" are linked (PDF p. 129). **#70, demo in-page** (PDF pp. 130–131): an in-page product demo produces "spectacular first impressions"; links an article of twelve examples (PDF pp. 130–131).

### Tips #71–#80 (PDF pp. 132–150)

**#71, don't shortcut hosting** (PDF pp. 132–133): cheap shared hosting costs more through downtime and hacks, though hosting advice is "subjective" to where a reader is in the journey (PDF p. 132). The author's FAQ macros: Carrd free on a subdomain "then $9/year" for a custom domain; Bluehost at "$3/month" for a free WordPress product; Flywheel at "$100/month" for his network; Digital Ocean at "$5/month" if you know a server (PDF pp. 132–133). **#72, reassure during checkout** (PDF pp. 134–135): a star rating, client logos, a payment FAQ, a short testimonial; LearnUX shows how the charge appears on a statement (PDF pp. 134–135). **#73, personalize the success message** (PDF pp. 136–137): a personal thank-you at the moment of purchase; the author shared HEY's CEO message "with around 5 people" and saw it shared "a dozen times on social media" (PDF p. 136). **#74, steer clear of a header carousel** (PDF pp. 138–139): intro copy is not meant to "dance off the side" mid-sentence; a carousel can work mid-page but the header is "out of bounds"; Brad Frost's carousel piece (PDF pp. 138–139). **#75, boost confidence with payment method logos** (PDF pp. 140–141): an SSL certificate is "non-negotiable"; logos near pricing, CTAs and footer (PDF p. 140). **#76, soft launch with a discount** (PDF p. 142): go live without announcement, then email a "healthy discount" to a select audience, which yields "kinder" and more detailed feedback before the announcement. **#77, implement smooth scroll** (PDF pp. 143–144): CSS `scroll-behavior` on the body, with the caveat that Safari "still needs to come to the party" (PDF p. 144). **#78, define a visual hierarchy** (PDF pp. 145–146): squint and check what is prominent; a visitor's eyes are trained to "follow a Z-pattern" (PDF p. 146). **#79, remove inactive social media accounts** (PDF p. 147): a profile "last updated 3 years ago" reads as abandoned, which matters most on subscription pages. **#80, wrap it in a device** (PDF pp. 148–150): device mock-ups with a subtle shadow; a family of devices "emphasizes the remote-working possibilities"; Facebook Design's device set, Shotsnapp, Lstore, Creative Market (PDF pp. 148–150).

### Tips #81–#90 (PDF pp. 151–166)

**#81, define container width** (PDF pp. 151–152): "over 20%" of the author's desktop visitors use a 1920px monitor, and W3Schools' statistics show bigger resolutions "on the rise", so a max-width keeps elements from floating (PDF p. 152). **#82, prove you are established** (PDF p. 153): Harvest's four figures, "Founded in 2006", "Powering 50,000 businesses", "400 million hours tracked", "$13 billion paid invoices". **#83, strategically position testimonials** (PDF pp. 154–155): the best testimonial above the fold, feature-specific ones beside the feature (CleanShot X pairs a saving-and-dragging feature with a "Swiss Army knife" quote), and a value-for-money one near checkout (PDF pp. 154–155). **#84, focus on people, not search engines** (PDF p. 156): Google is "an unpredictable beast right now"; "Word of mouth > SEO". **#85, show them how it works** (PDF pp. 157–158): the first question a SaaS visitor asks is "How much is it?", answered "with full transparency, no tricks"; the second is the steps from sign-up to "smooth sailing", answered with numbered, visual lists (PDF pp. 157–158). **#86, include an explainer video** (PDF p. 159): under 30 seconds, subtitled, with a CTA at the end; a rushed video with no budget or script "will hurt your brand". **#87, focus on form UX** (PDF p. 160): one column, minimum required fields, labels top-left, correct tab order; Crazy Egg's form guideline. **#88, consider a lifetime discount** (PDF pp. 161–162): a once-off price if running costs and support are low; Danny from Headlime said it "significantly boosted short-term cash flow" (PDF p. 161). **#89, focus on benefits, not features** (PDF p. 163): how the calendar app saves time, not its technology; Sahil Lavingia's line that customers "care about their problems", not the product. **#90, alternate section background colors** (PDF pp. 164–166): a slightly darker or lighter ground per section, or wavy and diagonal separators (PDF pp. 164–166).

### Tips #91–#101 (PDF pp. 167–185)

**#91, bring it to life** (PDF pp. 167–171): skeuomorphic mock-ups, a book template from Creative Market that "made a huge difference" to the book's own page, a framed print, an aerial view of plans on a desk, drop shadows; ## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a tip does not apply.

- **The whole book.** "It Depends" is the opening line, the best and worst advice there is; every landing page "has a different objective", and the tips are to be read a handful at a time and applied where they "resonate the most" (Foreword, PDF p. 10). The book is not for making a page "a money-maker overnight" (Foreword, PDF p. 11).
- **Testimonials.** Only "the choice few adding value" survive the round-up (Tip #01, PDF p. 13); illustrated and real customer images are not mixed (Tip #02, PDF p. 16); a page with eight does not need a ninth (Tip #08, PDF p. 28).
- **Alignment.** The rule against centered and justified text applies to "long paragraphs" and to any paragraph "with more than two lines"; shorter runs are outside it (Tip #07, PDF pp. 26–27).
- **Image CDNs.** IMGIX is "a bit excessive" for most landing pages and used by the author only for pages inside his bigger sites (Tip #03, PDF p. 19).
- **Navigation.** Removing the main nav applies to a landing page that sits "within a bigger website" (Tip #11, PDF p. 33); a sticky header is for a page that "is long" (Tip #25, PDF p. 57).
- **Hiding on mobile.** Images or text that "add nothing" on mobile may be hidden (Tip #39, PDF p. 82).
- **Share buttons.** The author's own vote is to remove them entirely; hard-coding is the fallback "if you must have them" (Tip #41, PDF p. 84).
- **Illustrations.** Character illustrations are "becoming generic" but still "brighten a stale page"; custom commissions are for those "with the budget" (Tip #60, PDF pp. 112–113).
- **The chatbot.** The delay applies only if the reader feels the page "benefits from one" (Tip #64, PDF p. 121).
- **New tabs.** The tip links "a strong Counterargument" by Jesse with several use cases, calls questioning UX decisions "always healthy", and keeps its own vote only for demos, support portals and blogs, which in the author's experience "offer poor navigations" back to the primary page (Tip #66, PDF p. 123).
- **A/B testing.** Only on high-traffic pages; with low data the results are "inconclusive and even misleading" (Tip #57, PDF p. 108). Testing narratives is "the most advanced Hot Tip" and worth doing only "with the traffic and budget" (Tip #98, PDF p. 180).
- **Hosting.** Hosting advice is "subjective" to where the reader is in the journey, and the tip applies to pages with a product or service "people are buying"; a free product on a budget is sent to Bluehost, which "is not the best hosting" (Tip #71, PDF pp. 132–133).
- **Checkout.** Where checkout cannot be customized, the reassurance moves to the pricing table or final CTA (Tip #72, PDF p. 135).
- **Carousels.** A product slideshow or feature carousel "can work mid-page"; only the header is "out of bounds" (Tip #74, PDF p. 138).
- **Smooth scroll.** Safari "still needs to come to the party" (Tip #77, PDF p. 144).
- **Explainer videos.** Longer than 30 seconds can work "but only if brilliant"; "Conversely", a rushed video with no budget or script "will hurt your brand" (Tip #86, PDF p. 159).
- **Lifetime pricing.** Only where running costs are "very low" and support demands "minimal" (Tip #88, PDF p. 161).
- **Parity pricing.** With remote working, location-based parity "gets more tricky", so it is offered "if you can" (Tip #94, PDF p. 174).
- **Flair.** Parallax, scroll transitions, custom cursors and color switchers have "their time and place"; the exclusion is for the reader who is new to landing pages or "not making sales", and once sales start the reader may "indulge in a little bit of flair" (Tip #96, PDF p. 178).
- **Specialists.** Outsourcing begins once the page "is converting" and the reader is pleased with "solo optimization efforts" (Tip #97, PDF p. 179).
- **Realignment.** The annual check-in also asks whether the page needs to "realign with new goals", because industries evolve and businesses "need to adapt" (Tip #101, PDF p. 185).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each tool, figure, or company example the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure, and the reason is given.

- **Headlime, the headline generator (Tip #06, PDF p. 25) and the lifetime-deal example whose founder Danny reported it "worked wonders" (Tips #51 and #88, PDF pp. 98, 161).** Changed before the edition was finished: Headlime was acquired by Conversion.ai, since renamed Jasper, with the deal announced on 30 March 2021 and closed on 31 March 2021 (headlime.com/blog, "Headlime and Conversion.ai Join Forces"; theygotacquired.com; pitchbook.com), a month before Edition 1.1's April 2021 update. The lifetime tiers described in the book are no longer on sale, and the two results attributed to Danny are his own report to the author, unchecked.
- **Google Optimize as the A/B testing primer (Tip #57, PDF p. 108).** Gone. Google announced on 20 January 2023 that Optimize and Optimize 360 would end on 30 September 2023, and they did (bounteous.com; cardinalpath.com; seroundtable.com). The linked overview is of a discontinued product.
- **Carrd: free on a subdomain, "then $9/year" for a custom domain (Tips #71 and #95, PDF pp. 132, 177).** Changed. Carrd's 2026 tiers are Free, Pro Lite at $9 a year, Pro Standard at $19 a year, and Pro Plus at $49 a year, and a custom domain needs Pro Standard; Pro Lite does not include one (nocode.mba; niftysite.co; linke.ro). The $9 figure now buys a plan without the feature the book names it for.
- **Safari and smooth scroll: Safari "still needs to come to the party" (Tip #77, PDF p. 144).** Resolved. Safari 15.4, released in 2022, supports the CSS `scroll-behavior` property and the `behavior: "smooth"` option on `scrollTo` (github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data issue 22889; developer.mozilla.org, scroll-behavior). The caveat no longer applies.
- **`font-smooth` as a CSS property with a Mozilla guide (Tip #59, PDF p. 110).** Still non-standard. MDN records that `font-smooth` was removed from the CSS Fonts specification and is "not on the standard track", that WebKit's `-webkit-font-smoothing` works only on macOS, and its non-standard notice tells readers not to use it on production sites (developer.mozilla.org, font-smooth; caniuse.com/font-smooth).
- **UserWay, an accessibility solution "for ADA & WCAG Compliance" whose widget sits "in the corner of the browser" (Tip #69, PDF p. 129).** Contested since. The FTC approved a final order in April 2025 requiring accessiBe, a competing overlay vendor, to pay $1 million over claims that its widget made sites WCAG-compliant, which the FTC's complaint called "false, misleading, or unsubstantiated" (ftc.gov, April 2025 press release; lflegal.com; adrianroselli.com). UserWay was not the defendant; it is now part of Level Access and still sells the widget. The tip's five hand-done tasks are unaffected; the closing recommendation points at a product category a federal regulator has since acted against.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Invented examples presented as the evidence.** The testimonial tips rest on customers who do not exist: "Gavin Jenkins" and "Kim Davis" (Tip #01, PDF pp. 12–13), "Dave Lewis", "VP of Customer Relations at Starbucks" (Tip #02, PDF p. 15), and "Klaudia", a designer at Spotify, endorsing "ZenIcons" (Tip #20, PDF pp. 49–50). The tips describe what a persuasive testimonial would look like; they show no page where one moved a conversion rate.
- **Figures without a source.** The claim that every page load costs conversion (Tip #16, PDF p. 44), that Open Graph links are "clicked more" and fifteen minutes on them "is worth it" (Tip #42, PDF p. 85), that eyes are trained to "follow a Z-pattern" (Tip #78, PDF p. 146), that a photoshoot's return is "pretty much guaranteed" (Tip #03, PDF p. 17), and that an accessibility hour saves others "hundreds of frustrating hours" (Tip #69, PDF p. 128) carry no citation. The only quantified results are reports to the author by other founders, Danny at Headlime (Tips #51 and #88), Lee Robinson (Tip #50), Kevin Mead (Tip #65), and one critique customer's "8%" (PDF p. 188), none with a baseline, a period, or a control.
- **Correlation presented as cause.** Lee Robinson's highlighted tier is said to have "hugely impacted his course sales" (Tip #50, PDF p. 95), Headlime's limited lifetime spots "worked wonders for the launch" (Tip #51, PDF p. 98), and the critique customer's page "almost doubled the conversion rate" after "split testing a lot" (PDF p. 188). None separates the named change from everything else that changed at the same time; the book's own Tip #57 says low-data results are "inconclusive and even misleading" (PDF p. 108).
- **The author's own products as the corpus.** Roughly forty tips close on a One Page Love collection the author curated, from "1000+ Landing Pages" with whitespace (Tip #05, PDF p. 23) to "2000+" with smooth scroll (Tip #77, PDF p. 144); the book's own landing page is the worked example for haste (Tip #24), the radial burst (Tip #63), the fire illustration (Tip #60), the book mock-up (Tip #91), and the Open Graph card (Tip #42); and the book ends by selling a $299 critique (PDF pp. 187–188). Unbounce is thanked for being "super supportive" of the book in the tip that recommends it (Tip #95, PDF p. 177), and the Bluehost link is disclosed as a discount link (Tip #71, PDF p. 133). The sample of pages is the author's taste, not a measured population.
- **Internal tension.** Tip #10 tells the reader to soften "maximum contrast" black-on-white into "a subtle grey/color text hierarchy" (PDF pp. 30–31), while Tips #56 and #69 tell the reader to raise contrast and cite an "AAA accessibility score of 8.99" (PDF pp. 106, 128); the reconciliation, that the hierarchy stays "within Accessibility standards", is one line pointing at a tool (PDF p. 31). Tip #84 argues "Word of mouth > SEO" (PDF p. 156) while Tip #99 argues for speed partly because "Google favor[s] fast websites" (PDF p. 182). Tip #66 recommends opening links in new tabs and links a counterargument it calls "strong" without answering it (PDF p. 123). <!-- spelling:ignore: "grey/color" is a verbatim quotation of the book, PDF p. 31 -->
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** The book is about a single page selling one thing to a visitor arriving cold, and its examples are books, icon packs, courses, prints, and self-serve SaaS trials (Tips #20, #40, #51, #91, #93). Its business buyers are an enterprise support tool's customers (Tip #02, PDF pp. 14–15) and a high-end sale by phone (Tip #65, PDF p. 122), the latter treated as an exception to route around with a download; every priced example is a plan, a lifetime deal, or a one-off purchase; and no example describes a page for a product that is not yet available, the case the Foreword's three questions do not cover.
- **Dated mechanics.** Calling an SSL certificate "non-negotiable" (Tip #75, PDF p. 140) describes a 2010s decision; the Safari smooth-scroll caveat (Tip #77, PDF p. 144), the Google Optimize primer (Tip #57, PDF p. 108), the `font-smooth` property (Tip #59, PDF p. 110), the "1200px & 960px" grid systems "we all used to reference" (Tip #36, PDF p. 76), and flexbox `order` as the way to reorder copy and images (Tip #39, PDF p. 82) are each a snapshot of 2020 front-end practice; section 7 has the later state of each.
- **The accessibility tip ends on an overlay.** Tip #69's five tasks are hand-done fixes to the page, and its resource list ends with UserWay, a browser-corner widget sold "for ADA & WCAG Compliance" (PDF p. 129). The tip does not say which of the two the reader is meant to rely on, and section 7 records the regulatory action that has since been taken against the same product category.
- **The format hides its own reasoning.** Each tip is a page or two with a screenshot, and the book says so: the tips were meant to lead up to courses and "organically turned into this book" (PDF p. 186). Where a tip has a reason, it is one sentence ("herd mentality", Tip #52, PDF p. 100; "Hicks Law", Tip #58, PDF p. 109); where it has none, the instruction stands alone (Tips #25, #37, #49, #90). A reader who wants to know when a tip does not apply has, for most of them, only the Foreword's "It Depends".
