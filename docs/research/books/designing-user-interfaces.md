# Designing User Interfaces, read back

## 1. Source

*Designing User Interfaces*, Michal Malewicz and Diana Malewicz, first edition, 2020. Self-published by the authors' studio HYPE4 (hype4.com) and sold through designingui.com; the file carries no publisher imprint and no ISBN anywhere in its front or back matter. Read 2026-09-10 from the `pdftotext -layout` extraction of the 408-page PDF, front to back, opening the PDF itself only for the few pages where the text extraction lost a figure's numbers.

**Page rule.** The book prints page numbers, and this file cites the printed page. Printed page = PDF index, offset zero, confirmed by opening PDF pages 4, 5, 15, 46, 107 and 407, each of which prints exactly that number in its footer. The front matter -- a cover mockup and the table of contents -- occupies PDF pages 1-2 and carries no printed number; printed numbering begins at 3 on PDF page 3. The book has 29 numbered chapters (no "Chapter" word, just "1." through "29." on each title page) grouped under three unnamed parts the table of contents itself labels INTRO, DESIGN BASICS and BONUS CONTENT. A citation reads "(Layout & Grid, p. 61)", naming the chapter rather than the part.

## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, page by page, with its own exceptions, what has changed since 2020, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the authors' own frame, is that good UI design is a "craft" governed by strict rules rather than an art (Design and Art, p. 22), and that fifteen years and "over 500" design projects can be condensed into good practices, rules of thumb, and "quick-and-dirty" hacks a reader applies directly (Introduction, p. 4; Who is this book for?, pp. 9-10). The book states its own method plainly: start with the tested good practice, and only bend or break the rule once experience is behind you (What makes UI good?, p. 27). It is pitched at five named audiences -- designers, front-end developers, startup founders, marketing/social-media people, and "curious types" -- on the claim that the same underlying precision serves all five (Who is this book for?, pp. 10-13).

The authors say what the claim is not. It is not an art book: UI design is "definitely NOT art" (Design and Art, p. 23), and Dribbble-style visual exploration is named explicitly as inspiration to be judged critically, not a finished product (Design and Art, p. 24). It does not claim to be the only right way to work, calling itself simply "the way we do it" (Introduction, p. 5), and it invites reader feedback to extend it. It is not a UX-research or motion-engineering text: research methods are mentioned only in passing under UI vs UX (p. 18), and the Animation chapter opens by admitting that covering motion "in a static book format" is an odd fit, before covering only the basics (Animation, p. 300).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction; Who is this book for?; UI vs UX; Design and Art (pp. 3-25)

The authors introduce the book as the condensed output of HYPE4, a "design-driven software house" co-founded in 2013 (Introduction, p. 6), and define UI, UX and CX as nested categories: UI is "a part of" the UX process, and CX is the top-level process governing not just the product but the whole company (UI vs UX, pp. 15, 19). A UI designer owns "the final look & feel," a UX designer owns "how it will work" (p. 18). Design and Art then draws the book's central line: interfaces are a "precise, visual blueprint" for a working product, not artworks, and Dribbble-style work is inspiration to filter critically rather than a target (pp. 22-24).

### What makes UI good?; Perception (pp. 26-46)

Good UI is defined by an adapted version of Dieter Rams's "10 commandments," reframed for screens: innovative but not novel for its own sake, useful, aesthetic, long-lasting, understandable, unobtrusive, honest, thorough to the last detail, environmentally considerate, and "as little design as possible" (What makes UI good?, pp. 27-32). Perception catalogs Gestalt-derived rules a designer can apply consciously: proximity, similarity, closure, symmetry, continuity, common fate, figure/background, the aesthetic-usability effect, serial position, and Von Restorff's isolation effect, plus Hick's Law (more options make a choice "harder" to reach) and Miller's Law (roughly seven items at once), with the advice to keep navigation and choice sets to four or five (Perception, pp. 37-46).

### Screens (pp. 47-54)

Covers the history of screen resolution from CRT to Retina, defines 1x/2x/3x design and the difference between a pixel and a point, and gives thumb-reach zones for phones and tablets -- bottom tabs are called the "easiest way" to design menus for nearly every product, and the hamburger menu is called out as sitting in the worst spot "for right-handed users" (Screens, pp. 50-53).

### Layout and grid (pp. 55-76)

Defines horizontal and vertical grids, fluid vs. fixed grids, and walks through building 10-point and 8-point grid systems, arguing the 8-point grid is "currently the most popular" because its halves and doubles avoid awkward numbers like 15 (pp. 61-65). Introduces the "soft grid" (spacing multiples of a base number rather than a rigid column grid) and the authors' own "Red Square Method" for auditing alignment (pp. 70-74). Closes with F-pattern and Z-pattern scanning and the rule that body copy on a 12-column grid should not exceed eight columns, roughly ten words a line (pp. 75-76).

### Objects (pp. 77-93)

Defines the box model (fill, border, inner/outer margin), size, position, angle, border-radius, fill types, border styles, and shadow parameters, stating a drop shadow's opacity should "never be higher than 40%" and should be colored from the object's own hue rather than pure black for a natural look (pp. 80-90). Also covers inner shadow, Gaussian/background/motion/zoom blur.

### Colors (pp. 94-124)

Opens with a claim that "over 90%" of product preference comes down to color (p. 95, uncited), then covers the RGB model, WCAG contrast levels (a "4.5:1" ratio minimum for essential elements, p. 98), per-hue psychology and industry associations for blue/green/red/yellow/orange/pink/purple/black-gray/white (pp. 100-108), the eight standard palette types (monochromatic through square, pp. 111-117), the 60/30/10 balancing ratio, and a recurring rule against pure black -- "#000000" is discouraged, dark gray is preferred (pp. 122-123). Closes with a four-color system-state convention: positive, negative, neutral, warning (p. 124).

### Gradients (pp. 125-138)

Argues gradients read as natural because most of what we see in the physical world is itself "a gradient" (p. 128), covers linear/radial/angular gradient types, and gives best practices: two colors, matched warm-with-warm or cold-with-cold, kept subtle, and built by shifting only the Hue value 15-30 degrees between two stops rather than picking unrelated colors (pp. 134-136).

### Typography (pp. 139-166)

Defines font-vs-typeface, font anatomy (baseline, cap-height, x-height, ascenders, descenders), kerning and leading (leading = font size x 1.618, the Golden Ratio, p. 144), and a hierarchy method using the same 1.618 multiplier or the grid's own base number for font sizes (p. 148). States mobile body copy should sit at 16-18pt (p. 159), recommends left-aligned labels over centered or justified text (pp. 150, 239-240), and gives detailed font-pairing rules, closing with a call to "keep it simple," one or two fonts and 3 to 5 sizes (p. 165).

### Icons (pp. 167-177)

Warns that icon meaning is not universal, citing the authors' own client research where 67% of one farmer-focused app's users read a heart icon as "the product is healthy" against 21% who read it as "add to favorites" (p. 168, uncited beyond that anecdote). Gives rules for icon construction from simple shapes, style consistency (roundness, fill vs. outline, stroke weight), legibility at scale, and a bounding-box method for equalizing optical size across square, round and irregular icon shapes (pp. 171-177).

### Buttons (pp. 178-202)

The chapter's throughline is that "a button should look like a button" (p. 180): rectangles are the safest shape, buttons need a minimum 44x44pt mobile target with a 50pt sweet spot, and CTA height should sit between 40 and 60 points (pp. 184, 187). Defines a button-type hierarchy -- primary, CTA (one per screen), secondary, tertiary, ghost/outline -- and states that using more than one strong (CTA-weight) button per page lowers conversion (p. 189). Also covers corner-radius consistency, dark/light background visibility testing, icon alignment inside buttons, and title-case over all-caps for longer labels (pp. 192-202).

### Cards (pp. 203-212)

Defines a card as an excerpt of a detail page, recommends "one button" and three lines of text, horizontal/vertical/stack/masonry display patterns, cutting the last card in a carousel to signal scrollability, and a content hierarchy of photo, title, secondary info, action, closing with a rule against pure Neumorphism-only interfaces reused later in Design Styles (pp. 204-212).

### Tables and Graphs (pp. 213-228)

States that data, not decoration, is a table's point, so "unnecessary decoration" should go (p. 215): remove borders and backgrounds where whitespace can separate rows, left-align text and right-align numbers, keep side margins from the grid base number, and avoid stretching a narrow table to the full viewport width (pp. 216-222). For narrow (mobile) screens it gives two options -- a side-scrolling table, or one small "mini table"/definition-list per row -- and calls the side-scroll option "the lesser of two evils" (p. 224). The Graphs section asks for thick, high-contrast lines, a legible tooltip, and a pattern (not color alone) to distinguish pie/bar-chart segments for colorblind users (pp. 226-227), while separately showing, as a caveat, how a "fashionable... not real" smoothed line chart is built and admitting it "bends the honesty rule... a bit" (p. 228).

### Forms (pp. 229-260)

The chapter's core rules: left-aligned labels directly above left-aligned single-column fields are "always the best choice" for both desktop and mobile (pp. 239-241); forms should stay narrow, 320-500 points wide, and longer ones should be split into steps with a visible step or percentage indicator (pp. 242, 259); an error message belongs "directly under each field" in red, never bundled under the whole form (p. 233); a field's five states (normal, active, completed, unsuccessful, disabled) should each be designed; optional fields, not required ones, should be labeled, and a form should carry only one submit button (p. 256); and a subtle inner shadow on a text field is said to "slightly increase conversion" per unnamed "studies" (p. 238). Dropdowns of five options or fewer should become radio buttons instead, and long dropdowns should get a search box (pp. 245-246); checkboxes read as multi-select and switches as single instant toggles, and each needs at least a 44pt (mobile) or 30pt (desktop) active zone including its label (pp. 249-251).

### Modals, popups (pp. 261-279)

The chapter's summary line is definitional: these modules are a "last resort" (p. 262). Popups need both an X and a labeled action button, a ~70-80% dark overlay, and a clearly differentiated primary/secondary button pair; overlays (e.g. cookie banners) do not darken the background and stay 600-800px wide on desktop; action sheets are a native-feeling mobile pattern that should slide in to signal swipeability; tooltips are for short, non-interactive hints and should be avoided on touch devices entirely (pp. 263-277). States plainly that pop-up sign-up forms are the product of "statistics meeting" poor user experience, and recommends an on-page form instead (p. 268).

### Navigation (pp. 280-298)

Names three navigation types -- visible, hidden, contextual -- and recommends visible bottom-tab navigation as the default, capped at five items on mobile and seven on desktop (pp. 281, 283). Gives a full construction method for tab bars, text tabs, sidebars, the floating-action button, and the hamburger-menu drawer, including 44pt minimum touch heights throughout, and closes flatly: the best navigation is the one that stays "always visible" (p. 298).

### Animation (pp. 299-310)

Covers transition types (position, scale, rotation), easing curves, a bounce effect capped at "never... larger than 20%" of the final value (p. 304), progress bars and spinners, directionally consistent screen transitions, microinteractions as user-triggered state changes (the toggle switch is the model example, p. 307), and parallax scrolling with at least three depth layers used sparingly (p. 310).

### Photos (pp. 311-322)

Recommends real, DIY photography over stock imagery, gives three techniques for keeping text readable over a photo (flat dark overlay, gradient overlay, hard rectangle overlay), and a psychology note: a photographed person looking toward a CTA, rather than at the camera, draws the viewer's gaze the same way (pp. 313-321).

### Illustration (pp. 323-328)

Recommends original illustration in a brand's own consistent style (stroke, fill, roundness) for onboarding, mascots and empty states, citing Duolingo's mascot and the Office paperclip as the two named examples (pp. 324-327).

### Language (pp. 329-336)

Micro-copy should be short, precise and consistent; negative-action confirmations should carry one unambiguous button label rather than double negatives ("Cancel my choice" beats a bare "OK") (pp. 331-332); switch and checkbox labels that invert their own visible meaning are named a "Dark Pattern" and condemned outright (p. 334); and Lorem Ipsum is rejected in favor of in-context placeholder copy, closing with "Don't use Lorem Ipsum. Ever!" (p. 335).

### Design Styles (pp. 337-350)

Walks Skeuomorphism, Flat Design, Material Design, "Modern Design," Neumorphism, Dark Mode, and Ultra-Minimalism as a lineage, each with its problems: Flat Design is measured at "up to 22% slower" to parse than layered UI (uncited, p. 341); Neumorphism is called "terrible for buttons" because of low edge contrast on active elements (p. 344); Dark Mode's energy saving only applies on OLED screens with true black, and is otherwise "purely an aesthetic choice" (p. 347). The chapter claims Neumorphism's name was coined by co-author Michal Malewicz in late 2019 (p. 344) and closes urging designers to mix styles deliberately rather than chase trends (p. 350).

### Design Process (pp. 351-357)

Practical file hygiene: name layers and components in English, grade names with "/" or "-", group top-level sections, name text and layer styles after their CSS/HTML role (H1, H2, P, Span), and export an early style guide as a bridge to a design system (pp. 352-357).

### Design Systems (pp. 358-372)

Defines a design system as "a documented library" of design components, ready to use in code, explicitly citing Brad Frost's Atomic Design (atoms through pages) as the organizing frame (p. 359). Argues a system exists to preserve internal consistency as a team grows past informal hallway communication (p. 360), should be built in code with a developer as its gatekeeper rather than a CMS anyone can edit (p. 367), should be described down to CSS-ready values via design tokens on a soft-grid base (pp. 365-366), and should carry a visible research/change history so later team members see why a rule exists (p. 370). Closes stating a minimum team of a designer, a product owner and a front-end developer (p. 372).

### UI Audit (pp. 373-393)

A method for finding design-debt: count typefaces/sizes/weights per screen, colors and gradients (including hover states) with a color picker, grid deviations, and inconsistent border/radius/shadow values across otherwise similar objects, prioritizing "the easiest fixes first" (pp. 375-386). Closes with a two-page printable checklist covering typography, interactive elements, grid/layout, and colors/gradients (pp. 392-393).

### Developer Handoff (pp. 394-403)

Argues the designer should stay involved through implementation QA rather than handing off and moving on (p. 395), recommends building a component library early, transferring it through a handoff tool (Zeplin, Avocode, inVision Inspect are named, p. 398), and argues both disciplines benefit from crossing over -- a CSS-literate designer communicates "a lot easier" with developers (p. 399), and a developer who understands design produces fewer QA rounds (p. 401). Gives asset-format guidance -- SVG for simple shapes/icons, PNG for transparency, JPG for photos -- and names TinyPNG, Compressor, ImageOptim and ImageAlpha as compression tools (pp. 402-403).

### Summary (pp. 404-405)

A one-page closing note thanking the reader and pointing to the authors' plan to keep extending the book with new chapters and video content (p. 405).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the authors limit their own claim, admit a counter-case, or say a rule does not apply.

- **Grid base number.** The 8-point grid is called "currently the most popular," not the only correct choice; the 10-point grid is walked with equal care and called a "best starting" point for any project in its own right (Layout and grid, pp. 61-62, 65).
- **Corner radius and pill buttons.** A fully rounded ("pill") button is fine, but only when its text is "centered or removed" -- left-aligned text over a heavily rounded corner is shown visually breaking (Buttons, p. 195).
- **CTA count.** One CTA per screen is the rule, with a named exception: a long page may repeat the primary CTA near the bottom as a "catch-all" (Buttons, p. 189).
- **Serif fonts.** Sans-serif is the base recommendation, but text "longer than ten sentences" may read better in a serif font instead (Typography, p. 153).
- **Font weight extremes.** Both super-thin and super-thick fonts are flagged as legibility risks at small sizes, not banned outright -- a thin font is readable only "at the right size" (Typography, p. 160).
- **Dark mode's energy claim.** The OLED battery saving "only works" with pure black (#000000); a dark-gray dark mode is "purely an aesthetic choice" with no energy benefit (Design Styles, p. 347).
- **Neumorphism's use.** Ruled out for buttons and switches by name, but kept as acceptable, purely decorative background treatment for cards, "when it's not necessary" to the card's own legibility (Design Styles, pp. 344-345).
- **Table side-scrolling on mobile.** Offered as a real option but immediately qualified: it raises "accessibility issues" and is harder to use, "the lesser of two evils" (Tables and Graphs, p. 224).
- **"Unreal" smoothed graphs.** Shown as a technique, with the caveat that it "bends the honesty rule... a bit" and should not be overdone (Tables and Graphs, p. 228).
- **Material-style vs. classic text inputs.** Either style is acceptable -- Material-style inputs are "OK" -- the only hard rule is not mixing the two within one product (Forms, p. 237).
- **Placeholder-as-label.** Allowed "in rare cases... (search bar)" but called a real risk on longer forms, since the label disappears once typing starts (Forms, p. 241).
- **Icon-only buttons.** Acceptable "to condense more functionality within limited space" in apps, but the same page warns well-known symbols can be "interpreted differently," citing the heart-icon research (Buttons, p. 191).
- **Long-form illustration and mascots.** Recommended, with an explicit style mismatch warning: "cute, vibrant" characters won't suit a fintech or banking product (Illustration, p. 326).
- **Skeuomorphism.** Declared out of favor generally, but the book still frames every style as situational: "some groups," like senior citizens, prefer a more literal, real-world-metaphor interface (Design Styles, p. 350).

## 6. Where the book touches this repo

Each entry is the claim and its page, then the record that decided the matter or the ticket that holds it.

1. **A grid built on a small base number, multiplied and added to itself, is "currently the most popular" approach** (Layout and grid, pp. 61-65). Record: `app/src/lib/styles/tokens.css`'s `--space-*` scale and [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../design/brief.md)'s Density section: "The spacing scale stays on a 4px base. The existing `--space-*` ramp is correct and is kept."
2. **Slightly rounded corners (2-6p) read as friendlier than sharp ones, and the roundness should stay consistent across a project** (Objects, p. 84). Record: [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../design/brief.md)'s Depth and shape section: "Radius is `8px` on cards, panels, inputs and buttons. The `--radius-sm: 8px` token in `tokens.css` is already right."
3. **A drop shadow builds hierarchy between layers, should never exceed roughly 40% opacity, and should be colored from the object's own hue rather than pure black** (Objects, pp. 88-90), reinforced for cards ("cards should cast a shadow," Cards, p. 211), buttons ("buttons with shadows are also more 'clickable,'" Buttons, p. 194) and popups (a shadow signals "a top layer," Modals, p. 263). Record: [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../design/brief.md)'s Depth and shape section: "No drop shadows anywhere. Not on cards, not on the top bar, not on dropdowns. Depth is expressed by the surface ladder and by one-pixel borders."
4. **Pure black (`#000000`) is discouraged for both fills and text; a dark gray mixed toward the primary hue reads as more natural** (Colors, pp. 107, 122-123; Typography, p. 151). Record: `app/src/lib/styles/tokens.css`'s own header comment, "Nothing here is pure white or pure black, in either theme, on purpose," and [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../design/brief.md)'s Color section: "Nothing in this product is pure white or pure black, in either theme."
5. **Essential UI elements need at least a 4.5:1 (WCAG AA) contrast ratio, checked with a plugin rather than eyeballed** (Colors, p. 98). Record: [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../design/brief.md)'s Rules the palette must obey: "Contrast floors are non-negotiable, and are checked rather than claimed. Body text and its background meet WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 at 4.5:1," proved in CI by `app/src/lib/styles/tokens.spec.ts`.
6. **Interactive elements need a minimum touch target, "44 by 44 points"** (Buttons, p. 184; the same figure repeats for checkboxes/switches, p. 251, and the hamburger drawer, p. 293). Record: [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../design/brief.md)'s Density section: "Hit targets never fall below 44px in any pointer or touch context, whatever the visual height of the control."
7. **A CTA button should be "the only" uniquely colored element on the screen, and an anomalous, differently colored object is what the eye is drawn to** (Buttons, p. 186; Perception's Von Restorff effect, p. 46). Record: [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../design/brief.md)'s Rules the palette must obey: "The accent appears on primary actions and active state, and nowhere else," and the brief's Laws of UX table, Selective Attention and Von Restorff Effect rows.
8. **A project should limit itself to "one or two fonts"** and 3 to 5 sizes (Typography, pp. 165-166). Record: [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../design/brief.md)'s Typography section: "One family: Hanken Grotesk. Headings, body, labels, buttons, tables. There is no second family and no display serif."
9. **In a data table, text should be left-aligned and "numbers... should nearly always be right-aligned," so a reader can spot a larger number along the edge** (Tables and Graphs, p. 219). Record: [`docs/design/govuk-alignment.md`](../../design/govuk-alignment.md)'s Table row, built on [#509](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/509): a `Column`'s `numeric: true` "right-aligns the header with its body cells" and "turns on `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums`."
10. **A table too wide for a narrow screen becomes either a side-scrolling table or "a mini table"** for each row (Tables and Graphs, p. 224). 11. **A left-aligned label sitting directly above a left-aligned, single-column field is called "the best choice"** for both desktop and mobile (Forms, pp. 239-242). Record: [ADR-0018](../../adr/0018-templates-are-a-design-system-layer-with-two-named-exits.md)'s `FormPage`/`QuestionPage` Templates, and [`docs/design/govuk-alignment.md`](../../design/govuk-alignment.md)'s Fieldset and Text input rows.

Record: [`docs/design/govuk-alignment.md`](../../design/govuk-alignment.md)'s Error message row: `LabeledField`'s error "the message rendered *below* the control it refuses until `198953e` moved it between the hint and the control."

Record: [`docs/design/govuk-alignment.md`](../../design/govuk-alignment.md)'s Password input row: "Plain `TextInput` with `type=\"password\"`, no toggle, no stated rule" -- `Open`, [#470](https://github.com/markgoho/doula-cloud/issues/470).

Record: [`docs/design/brief.md`](../../design/brief.md)'s Dark section: "Derive, do not invent. Dark keeps the same hues -- accent at `339`, neutrals at `~350`. Only lightness moves."

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, or example the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure -- this session's web-search budget was exhausted partway through by twenty simultaneous read-back agents sharing one identity tonight, so some checks below rest on a single fetched page rather than a full search.

- **Dieter Rams's ten principles, adapted as the book's definition of good UI** (What makes UI good?, p. 27). Still an active reference: Vitsœ's own page on the principles (vitsoe.com/us/about/good-design) states Rams "is often cited by a new generation as a key influence on their work that is shaping the 21st century," and the principles remain published there under a Creative Commons license, more than 45 years after they were written.
- **Material Design, described as created by Google in 2014, with palettes built on a 500-number base color (Design Styles, p. 342).** The 2014 launch date holds -- Google announced it June 25, 2014 at I/O, originally codenamed "Quantum Paper" (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material_Design) -- but the system has moved well past the book's description since: a 2018 revamp, then Material You / Material 3 in 2021 introducing themes generated dynamically from a user's own wallpaper, then "Material 3 Expressive" in 2025. The book's static, numbered-palette description is the pre-2018 system.
- **Brad Frost's Atomic Design, cited as the organizing frame for a design system (Design Systems, p. 359).** Still live and available: atomicdesign.bradfrost.com continues to offer the book for order or free online reading. Independent evidence it is still an active reference: this very repository adopted the same atoms/molecules/organisms/Templates tiers for its own component library in 2026, twelve years after Material Design and six years after this book, per [ADR-0018](../../adr/0018-templates-are-a-design-system-layer-with-two-named-exits.md).
- **Neumorphism's name, claimed as coined by co-author Michal Malewicz in late 2019, "the first long article on the style"** (Design Styles, p. 344). Contested: Wikipedia's Neumorphism article (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neumorphism) credits **Jason Kelly** with coining the term in 2019, as a portmanteau of "neo" and "skeuomorphism," and does not name Malewicz as the coiner -- it cites him only as a source discussing the style. The book's authorship claim could not be independently corroborated from the source checked, and the source checked names a different person.
- **WCAG 2.0, the version the book's contrast guidance is written against ("check the WCAG 2.0 contrast," Colors, p. 98).** Superseded in currency though not formally deprecated: the W3C's own standards page (w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag) states WCAG 2.2, published October 2023, is the current version, and explicitly that "WCAG 2.2 does not deprecate or supersede WCAG 2.1, and WCAG 2.1 does not deprecate or supersede WCAG 2.0" -- so 2.0 remains a valid standard, but this repository's own contrast and reflow obligations are written against 2.2 (`tokens.css`, [ADR-0024](../../adr/0024-layout-is-intrinsic-and-320px-is-a-conformance-commitment.md)), two full revisions past the book's frame.
- **Marissa Mayer's "41 Shades of Blue" A/B test at Google, used as an example of link-color research (Buttons, p. 188).** Partially checked: Wikipedia's A/B testing article (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A/B_testing) independently confirms the general shape of the story -- "Google's A/B testing with hyperlink colors. In order to optimize revenue, Google tested dozens of hyperlink hues to determine which colors attract the most clicks" -- but uses "dozens" rather than the specific figure "41," and does not attribute the test to Marissa Mayer by name. Neither the "41" figure nor the personal attribution could be independently confirmed from the sources reached; Mayer's own Wikipedia biography does not mention the story at all.
- **Zeplin, Avocode and inVision Inspect, named as developer-handoff tools (Developer Handoff, p. 398).** Zeplin is confirmed still operating: its own homepage (zeplin.io) as of this check still markets "design delivery," version tracking and spec generation for developers. Avocode's and inVision Inspect's current status were not checked. Separately, and not from a web source: this repository's own design-tool history ([ADR-0019](../../adr/0019-pen-dev-is-the-working-surface-and-code-is-the-truth.md)) shows the underlying handoff problem the book describes -- a static design file translated by hand into code -- being addressed by a different generation of tool entirely, an AI agent reading a design canvas and writing Svelte directly, which none of the book's three named tools attempt.
- **Sketch, shown as the design tool exporting assets (Developer Handoff, p. 403, "Sketch (v. 60) Asset export panel").** Unchecked in any comparative sense: Wikipedia's own Sketch article was too sparse to confirm or refute a decline relative to Figma, and this session's search budget ran out before a second source could be reached.
- **HYPE4, the authors' own studio, cited throughout as the source of the book's practical experience (Introduction, p. 6).** Unchecked: hype4.com refused the fetch (HTTP 403) during this session.
- **The heart-icon farmer-app research, "67% responded... 21% said" (Icons, p. 168), and the "over 90%" color-palette figure (Colors, p. 95).** Both are the authors' own unpublished client research with no named study, sample size, or methodology given in the text, and neither could be checked against any outside source -- they are the authors' own account, the same category the SaaS Playbook read-back on this map flagged for its own unsourced figures.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Figures without a source, throughout.** The claim that "over 90%" of preference comes from the color palette (Colors, p. 95), that Flat Design is "around 20-22% slower" to parse (Design Styles, p. 341), the 67%/21% heart-icon split (Icons, p. 168), and the claim that a subtle inner shadow "slightly increases conversion" (Forms, p. 238) each carry no citation, no named study, and no stated sample size. The book's own chapter-by-chapter argument leans on these repeatedly as settled facts rather than as the authors' working impressions.
- **Nearly every worked example is the same fictional product.** "Random Soda," a mock beverage product card, recurs across the Objects, Colors, Cards, Buttons and Design Styles chapters (e.g. pp. 78, 100, 204, 339-348) as the demonstration object. Unlike a book that walks real, named products with disclosed outcomes, this book's own evidence is almost entirely a single invented example redrawn in each style -- it demonstrates that a technique can be applied, not that it worked anywhere.
- **A self-authorship claim that a source outside the book does not confirm.** The book credits co-author Michal Malewicz with coining "Neumorphism" and writing "the first long article on the style" (Design Styles, p. 344); Wikipedia's article on the term names a different person as its coiner (section 7). The book is the sole source for its own most specific historical claim about itself.
- **Rules stated as universal that are the authors' own studio practice.** The book repeatedly presents a specific numeric choice -- the 8-point grid "over" the 10-point (Layout and grid, p. 65), the Golden Ratio multiplier for type hierarchy (Typography, p. 148), the 1.6-times cap-height spacing rule for labels (Forms, p. 235) -- as the settled answer, while its own "book's own exceptions" material (section 5 above) shows each has a workable alternative the book itself demonstrates on the facing page. The confidence of the main text and the qualifications in the asides do not always match.
- **Claims the book's own evidence does not reach.** The book asserts that people "'buy' into a product" with their eyes first (Who is this book for?, p. 12) and that an interface, like a joke, fails if "you have to explain it" (UI vs UX, p. 17) as flat maxims, with no data, no citation, and no stated exception -- a stronger and less qualified claim than the book makes almost anywhere else in its own argument.
- **A dated technical detail treated as a permanent rule.** The book states font licensing "for apps" is "the most expensive way to use" a paid typeface and recommends defaulting to each platform's system font, San Francisco or Roboto (Typography, p. 158) -- true of the web-font licensing landscape in 2020, but the book gives no signal that this is a market condition rather than a design principle, and does not flag it as something a reader should re-check.
- **The book's own honesty rule, applied inconsistently to itself.** Section 4 above notes the Tables and Graphs chapter names its own "unreal" smoothed line chart as bending "the honesty rule... a bit" (p. 228) -- a rare moment of the book auditing its own advice. That same self-scrutiny does not appear anywhere the book cites an internal, unnamed study as a settled fact (see the first point above), which is the same category of claim.
