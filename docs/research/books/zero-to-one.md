## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that the only businesses worth building are ones that go from **0 to 1** — that create something new rather than copy something that works — and that such a business ends up a monopoly, meaning a company so good at what it does that no rival offers a close substitute (ch. 3, PDF p. 21). The author's stated definition of that word excludes the two kinds an antitrust lawyer means: he is not interested in the firm that eliminates rivals by force or that holds a license from the state (ch. 3, PDF p. 21). Competition, on his account, is not the healthy default of Economics 101 but the condition in which no company makes an economic profit, so that "capitalism and competition are opposites" (ch. 3, PDF p. 21), and monopoly is "the condition of every successful business" (ch. 3, PDF p. 28). The route to it is to start with a very small market that few or no competitors serve, dominate it, then expand into adjacent ones (ch. 5, PDF pp. 41–43), and the aim is to be the **last mover** rather than the first (ch. 5, PDF pp. 44–45).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a formula: the paradox of teaching entrepreneurship, he writes, is that a formula "necessarily cannot exist" because every innovation is new, so the book "offers no formula for success" and is "not a manual" but "an exercise in thinking" (Preface, PDF p. 6; ch. 1, PDF p. 11). It is not a defense of monopoly in a static world, where he grants that a monopolist "is just a rent collector" and that monopolies "deserve their bad reputation" (ch. 3, PDF p. 27). It is not a claim that the post-crash startup dogmas should simply be inverted: rejecting the crowd dogmatically is still not thinking for yourself (ch. 2, PDF p. 19). And it is not a book about technology meaning computers — any new and better way of doing things counts (ch. 1, PDF p. 9).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Preface: Zero to One (PDF pp. 5–6)

Copying a model takes the world from 1 to n; creating something new takes it from 0 to 1, and the act of creation is singular (Preface, PDF p. 5). American companies that only fine-tune inherited lines of business will fail however large today's profits are, because "best practices" lead to dead ends (Preface, PDF p. 5). Technology is what the author calls the human capacity to work miracles, and it lets people do more with less (Preface, PDF p. 5). The book's evidence base is stated here: co-founding PayPal and Palantir, then investing in hundreds of startups including Facebook and SpaceX (Preface, PDF p. 6). Its origin is a 2012 Stanford course whose class notes, taken by Blake Masters, were revised into the book (Preface, PDF p. 6).

### 1. The Challenge of the Future (PDF pp. 7–11)

The chapter opens with the author's interview question, asking what important truth very few people agree with you on, and says a good answer takes the form that most people believe x while the truth is its opposite (ch. 1, PDF p. 7). Two kinds of progress are distinguished: horizontal or extensive progress, which is copying what works and whose single word is globalization, and vertical or intensive progress, which is doing new things and whose single word is technology; a typewriter multiplied 100 times is the first, a word processor the second (ch. 1, PDF p. 8). China is offered as the paradigm of globalization, with a twenty-year plan to become what the United States already is (ch. 1, PDF p. 9). The two are independent, so a period can have both, either, or neither: 1815–1914 had both, the interwar-to-1971 span had technology without much globalization, and since 1971 there has been globalization with technological development largely confined to information technology (ch. 1, PDF p. 9). The author's own answer to the contrarian question is that technology matters more than globalization, argued from resources: if China doubles energy production without new technology it doubles its air pollution, and spreading old ways of creating wealth yields devastation (ch. 1, PDF p. 10). Progress is not automatic — after a long, slow advance, relentless progress ran from the steam engine of the 1760s to about 1970, and the four-day workweek and moon vacations the late 1960s expected did not arrive (ch. 1, PDF p. 10). New technology tends to come from startups, because big organizations move slowly and a lone individual cannot build an industry; the author's positive definition is that a startup is the largest group of people you can convince of a plan to build a different future (ch. 1, PDF p. 11).

### 2. Party Like It's 1999 (PDF pp. 12–19)

The chapter is a revisionist history of the dot-com bubble, offered as a case in how conventional wisdom forms. The 1990s are remembered as prosperous, but the decade opened with a US recession, unemployment rising until July 1992, and a general malaise through 1994; Ross Perot took nearly 20% of the popular vote in 1992, the best third-party showing since 1912 (ch. 2, PDF pp. 12–13). Mosaic was released in November 1993 and became Netscape, whose Navigator went from about 20% of the browser market in January 1995 to almost 80% within twelve months, letting Netscape go public in August 1995 while unprofitable; the stock ran from $28 to $174 within five months (ch. 2, PDF p. 13). Yahoo! went public in April 1996 at an $848 million valuation and Amazon in May 1997 at $438 million (ch. 2, PDF p. 13). Alan Greenspan's "irrational exuberance" warning came in December 1996, more than three years before the burst (ch. 2, PDF p. 13). The argument that investors were exuberant but not irrational rests on the state of the rest of the world: the East Asian crises of July 1997, the August 1998 ruble default and the Dow falling more than 10% in days, Long-Term Capital Management losing $4.6 billion in the second half of 1998 with more than $100 billion of liabilities, and the euro sinking from $1.19 on its first trading day to $0.83 within two years (ch. 2, PDF p. 14). Dot-com mania is dated narrowly, September 1998 to March 2000 (ch. 2, PDF p. 14).

The PayPal section is first-person evidence: a mission to create a new internet currency to replace the dollar; a first product beaming money between PalmPilots that journalists voted one of the ten worst business ideas of 1999; a switch to email payments; and a $10 signup and $10 referral bounty that bought hundreds of thousands of customers and an exponential growth rate the author concedes was unsustainable on its own, because paying for customers means an exponentially growing cost structure (ch. 2, PDF pp. 15–16). A *Wall Street Journal* story of February 16, 2000 valued PayPal at $500 million; the company raised $100 million the next month against that back-of-the-envelope figure, and one South Korean firm wired $5 million with no signed documents (ch. 2, PDF p. 16). The NASDAQ peaked at 5,048 in mid-March 2000, fell to 3,321 by mid-April, and bottomed at 1,114 in October 2002 (ch. 2, PDF p. 17).

The chapter's payload is the four lessons Silicon Valley took from the crash — make incremental advances; stay lean and flexible, which the author glosses as code for unplanned; improve on the competition rather than create a new market; and focus on product, not sales, because a product needing salespeople is not good enough — against which he sets four opposites: risk boldness over triviality, a bad plan beats no plan, competitive markets destroy profits, and sales matters as much as product (ch. 2, PDF pp. 18–19). He immediately qualifies the inversion: the opposite of a dogma is not automatically true, and the most contrarian act is to think for yourself rather than to oppose the crowd (ch. 2, PDF p. 19).

### 3. All Happy Companies Are Different (PDF pp. 20–28)

The business version of the contrarian question is what valuable company nobody is building, and the chapter's distinction is between creating value and capturing it (ch. 3, PDF p. 20). The evidence is a 2012 comparison: US airlines carried millions of passengers at an average one-way fare of $178 and made 37 cents per passenger trip, while Google took $50 billion in revenue against the airlines' $160 billion and kept 21% as profit, more than a hundred times the airline margin, leaving Google worth three times every US airline combined (ch. 3, PDF p. 20). Perfect competition is defined as the Economics 101 ideal in which undifferentiated firms sell at the market price and no company makes an economic profit in the long run; monopoly is its opposite, a company that owns its market and sets its own price (ch. 3, PDF pp. 20–21).

Both sides misdescribe their markets, and the chapter names the two lies. Monopolists conceal monopoly by framing their market as the union of large markets: Google held about 68% of search as of May 2014 against Microsoft's 19% and Yahoo!'s 10%, but recast as advertising it is 3.4% of a $495 billion global market, and recast as consumer technology less than 0.24% of a $964 billion one; Eric Schmidt's 2011 congressional testimony about a competitive landscape is quoted as the form this takes (ch. 3, PDF pp. 22–23, 25). Non-monopolists do the reverse, defining a market as the intersection of small ones — British food, restaurant, Palo Alto — and the author calls understating competition the biggest mistake a startup can make (ch. 3, PDF pp. 23, 25). The human cost of competition is argued from restaurants, where thin margins force minimum-wage pay and family labor, and from the 2003 suicide of three-star chef Bernard Loiseau after a rival guide downgraded him (ch. 3, PDF p. 26). A monopolist, by contrast, can afford to weigh things other than money, which is how the author reads Google's motto (ch. 3, PDF p. 26).

The defense of monopoly is dynamic rather than static. In a world where nothing changes a monopolist is a rent collector, but creative monopolists add new categories of abundance; the government grants patents with one hand and prosecutes antitrust with the other for this reason (ch. 3, PDF p. 27). The history offered is a chain of replacements — IBM's hardware monopoly overtaken by Microsoft's software monopoly, then Microsoft's operating-system dominance reduced by Apple's iOS, and AT&T's telephone monopoly dissolved into cheap cell plans — so that progress is a history of better monopolies replacing incumbents (ch. 3, PDF p. 27). Economists' attachment to competitive equilibrium is explained as a borrowing from nineteenth-century physics, whose long-run equilibrium was the heat death of the universe (ch. 3, PDF p. 28). The chapter closes by inverting Tolstoy: happy companies differ because each earns a monopoly by solving a unique problem, and failed ones are alike in failing to escape competition (ch. 3, PDF p. 28).

### 4. The Ideology of Competition (PDF pp. 29–34)

Competition is treated here as an ideology rather than a market condition, one that pervades society and distorts thinking (ch. 4, PDF p. 29). The educational evidence is grades as a precise measure of competitiveness and elite students climbing until the competition beats their ambitions out of them, ending in rivalries over conventional careers (ch. 4, PDF pp. 29–30). The author's own case is the Supreme Court clerkship he did not get, and a law-school friend's question in 2004 as to whether he was glad he had missed it (ch. 4, PDF p. 30).

Two models of conflict are contrasted. Marx says people fight because they differ; Shakespeare says combatants look alike and fight anyway, and the author holds Shakespeare the better guide to business (ch. 4, PDF p. 31). The worked example is Microsoft and Google as Montague and Capulet, two firms with nothing to fight about that produced Windows against Chrome OS, Bing against Google Search, Explorer against Chrome, Office against Docs, and Surface against Nexus, while Apple overtook both: Apple's market capitalization was $500 billion in January 2013 against $467 billion for Google and Microsoft combined, having been smaller than each three years earlier (ch. 4, PDF pp. 31–32). Imitation is illustrated by the mobile card readers that followed Square's white square of October 2010 — a NetSecure half-moon, an Intuit cylinder, and PayPal's triangle in March 2012 (ch. 4, PDF p. 32). Competition is said to make people hallucinate opportunities, the example being the online pet-store fight among Pets.com and its rivals, which ended with $300 million of investment capital gone (ch. 4, PDF pp. 32–33). The Ellison–Siebel and Oracle–Informix feuds are given as rivalry that is merely distracting, ending with Informix's accounting scandal and its CEO in federal prison (ch. 4, PDF p. 33). The author's own resolution was to merge: Confinity and Elon Musk's X.com, four blocks apart and in what he calls all-out war with 100-hour weeks, negotiated a 50-50 merger in early March 2000 because both founders feared the bubble more than each other (ch. 4, PDF p. 34).

### 5. Last Mover Advantage (PDF pp. 35–45)

Escaping competition yields a monopoly, but a monopoly is only valuable if it endures, and the chapter's measure of endurance is future cash flow (ch. 5, PDF p. 35). The comparison is Twitter, valued at $24 billion on its 2013 listing while losing money, against the New York Times Company, which earned $133 million in 2012 at less than a twelfth of that capitalization (ch. 5, PDF p. 35). Low-growth businesses hold most of their value in the near term; technology companies invert this, with most of a company's value arriving at least ten to fifteen years out (ch. 5, PDF pp. 35–36). The author's own projection from March 2001 put 75% of PayPal's present value in profits from 2011 onward and, he adds, still underestimated it (ch. 5, PDF p. 36). Growth is easy to measure and durability is not, so measurement mania fixes on weekly actives and quarterly earnings while missing the question of whether the business will exist in a decade; Zynga and Groupon are the named cases of fast growth masking a long-term problem (ch. 5, PDF p. 37).

Four characteristics of monopoly are then given, with the caveat that they are not boxes to check. **Proprietary technology** must be at least ten times better than its closest substitute in some important dimension, because anything less reads as marginal and is hard to sell; PayPal made buying and selling on eBay ten times better than mailing a check that took seven to ten days, Amazon claimed ten times as many books as any other bookstore by holding no inventory, and the iPad took tablets from unusable to useful by integrated design (ch. 5, PDF pp. 38–39). **Network effects** make a product more useful as more people use it, but they are unreachable unless the product is valuable to its first users while the network is small — Xanadu needed every computer to join at once and folded after three decades, while Facebook began with Harvard students, and the author notes that such initial markets are so small they often do not look like business opportunities (ch. 5, PDF pp. 39–40). **Economies of scale** favor software, where the marginal cost of another copy is near zero, and disfavor service businesses; a yoga studio can add instructors and locations and still never serve millions from a core group (ch. 5, PDF p. 40). **Branding** is a monopoly on your own name, Apple being the strongest tech brand, but the surface techniques are imitable and rest on substance: proprietary hardware and software, purchasing scale, and the network effect of its developer ecosystem; Yahoo! under Marissa Mayer is the counter-case of beginning with brand, and no technology company can be built on branding alone (ch. 5, PDF pp. 40–41).

The building instructions follow. **Start small and monopolize**: every startup is small and every monopoly holds a large share of its market, so a startup should start with a very small market and always err on the side of too small (ch. 5, PDF p. 41). Small is not the same as nonexistent — the PalmPilot product failed because its users were scattered, had little in common, and used their devices episodically — while eBay's few thousand high-volume PowerSellers were reachable, and PayPal served 25% of them within three months (ch. 5, PDF p. 42). The perfect target market is a small group of particular people concentrated together and served by few or no competitors, which is why talk of 1% of a $100 billion market is a red flag (ch. 5, PDF p. 42). **Scaling up** is then gradual and sequenced: Amazon began with books because they were uniform and easy to ship, moved to CDs, videos, and software, and only then to everything, and eBay went from Beanie Babies to small-time hobbyists to general trading; the chapter notes eBay's own limit, that auctions suit distinctive items like coins and stamps rather than commodities (ch. 5, PDF pp. 42–43). **Don't disrupt**: disruption was a term of art for a low-end product overtaking incumbents and has become a self-congratulatory buzzword that makes a startup see itself through older firms' eyes; Napster is the cautionary case, on the *Time* cover one year and in bankruptcy court eighteen months later, while PayPal expanded the payments market and gave Visa more business than it took (ch. 5, PDF pp. 43–44). **The last will be first**: moving first is a tactic, not a goal, and what matters is making the last great development in a market and holding it, which the author supports with Capablanca on studying the endgame first (ch. 5, PDF pp. 44–45).

### 6. You Are Not a Lottery Ticket (PDF pp. 46–62)

The chapter asks whether success comes from luck or skill, and opens with successful people crediting luck: Gladwell's *Outliers* on a patchwork of lucky breaks, Buffett on the ovarian lottery, Bezos on planetary alignment, Gates on being born with certain skills (ch. 6, PDF p. 46). Against them the author sets serial entrepreneurship — hundreds of people have started multiple multimillion-dollar businesses, and Jobs, Dorsey, and Musk several multibillion-dollar ones — which he argues should not exist if success were mostly chance (ch. 6, PDF p. 46). He concedes the debate cannot be settled objectively, because companies are not experiments: you cannot rewind to 2004, make a thousand copies of the world, and start Facebook in each, and statistics does not work at a sample size of one (ch. 6, PDF pp. 46–47). Earlier generations are quoted as holding the opposite view, Emerson on strong men believing in cause and effect and Amundsen in 1912 on victory awaiting the man who has everything in order (ch. 6, PDF p. 47).

The chapter's framework is a two-by-two over definite versus indefinite and optimistic versus pessimistic. **Indefinite pessimism** is the author's reading of Europe since the early 1970s, undirected bureaucratic drift with nobody in charge (ch. 6, PDF pp. 48–49). **Definite pessimism** is his reading of China: growth of about 10% a year since 2000 from a low base by copying what worked in the West, with leaders who experienced famine and a public that saves because it expects worse (ch. 6, PDF pp. 49–50). **Definite optimism** led the Western world from the seventeenth century to the 1960s, evidenced by a chain of dated projects — the Thames tunnel in 1843, Suez in 1869, Panama in 1914, the Empire State Building 1929–1931, the Golden Gate Bridge 1933–1937, the Manhattan Project 1941–1945, the Interstate Highway System from 1956 with 20,000 miles open by 1965, and Apollo 1961–1972 putting twelve men on the moon — and by the Reber Plan, a schoolteacher's proposal to dam San Francisco Bay that drew newspaper endorsements, congressional hearings, and a 1.5-acre Army Corps scale model before its technical shortcomings sank it (ch. 6, PDF pp. 50–51). **Indefinite optimism** is said to have dominated American thinking since 1982, when a long bull market began and finance eclipsed engineering; its practitioners rearrange what already exists rather than build, which is the author's account of banking, law, private equity, and management consulting as careers that keep options open (ch. 6, PDF pp. 52–53). Gladwell is given the same treatment he gives Gates: a Boomer born in 1963 whose account of chance reflects a generation that learned to overrate it (ch. 6, PDF p. 53).

The indefinite attitude is then traced through four domains. **Finance** is called the only way to make money without knowing how to create wealth, and the chapter follows a sold company's proceeds through bank, institutional investors, and stock portfolio, with nobody in the chain knowing what to do with the money in the real economy (ch. 6, PDF p. 54). **Politics** substitutes polling for vision, and entitlement spending has exceeded discretionary spending every year since 1975 (ch. 6, PDF p. 55). **Philosophy** produced Rawls and Nozick, treated as indefinite optimists who both focused on process rather than any concrete vision (ch. 6, PDF pp. 55–56). **Life**: nineteenth-century life tables reduced death to a probability, and modern drug discovery amplifies Fleming's 1928 accident by screening compounds at random, against which the chapter cites Eroom's law, that new drugs approved per billion dollars of R&D have halved every nine years since 1950 (ch. 6, PDF p. 57). Biotech startups are called an extreme case, where professors consult part-time rather than commit, and the author suggests indefinite optimism may hold the field back more than regulation does (ch. 6, PDF p. 58).

The closing argument is that of the four views, only indefinite optimism is unsustainable, since the future cannot improve if nobody plans it, and that the answer usually offered — progress without planning, by analogy to evolution — does not transfer: leanness is a methodology rather than a goal, and small changes to what exists reach a local maximum, not a global one (ch. 6, PDF pp. 59–60). The counter-model is Jobs, whose greatest design the author calls the business itself: multi-year plans rather than focus groups, with the October 2001 iPod dismissed by analysts as a Macintosh feature (ch. 6, PDF p. 60). Definite planning also explains acquisition pricing — founders sell when they have no further vision, so the buyer overpaid, and founders with plans do not sell, so the offer was too low — the example being Zuckerberg refusing Yahoo!'s $1 billion offer in July 2006 in a board meeting the author attended (ch. 6, PDF p. 61). A startup, the chapter ends, is the largest endeavor over which a person can have definite mastery (ch. 6, PDF p. 62).

### 7. Follow the Money (PDF pp. 63–71)

The chapter is about the power law, which the author calls the law of the universe and says defines our surroundings so completely that we usually do not see it (ch. 7, PDF p. 63). It opens by noting that the compound-interest quotations attributed to Einstein are apocryphal, then cites Pareto's 1906 observation that 20% of Italians owned 80% of the land, alongside earthquakes and cities as other severely unequal distributions (ch. 7, PDF p. 63). Venture capital is where the pattern becomes visible: funds take about 20% of returns, run about ten years, and lose money at first because most startups fail (ch. 7, PDF p. 64). The error is expecting normally distributed returns and assembling a diversified portfolio in the hope that winners offset losers; the author calls this approach spray and pray and says it usually produces a portfolio of flops (ch. 7, PDF p. 65).

His own fund is the evidence: at Founders Fund, Facebook returned more than every other investment in the 2005 fund combined, and Palantir was set to return more than everything except Facebook, a pattern he says repeats across their funds and states as a rule — the best investment in a successful fund equals or beats the rest of the fund combined (ch. 7, PDF p. 66). Two rules follow: invest only in companies that could return the whole fund, and, because that rule is so restrictive, have no other rules (ch. 7, PDF p. 66). The counter-example is Andreessen Horowitz's $250,000 in Instagram in 2010, netting $78 million two years later — a 312x return that would still need nineteen repetitions to break even on a $1.5 billion fund (ch. 7, PDF p. 66). Founders Fund is described as focusing on five to seven companies per fund (ch. 7, PDF p. 67). The reason professionals miss the law is that it only resolves over time, while investors spend their days among early-stage companies that all look average, and they give the most attention to the most troubled (ch. 7, PDF pp. 67–68). Scale figures are given: less than 1% of new US businesses each year receive venture funding and total venture investment is under 0.2% of GDP, yet venture-backed companies create 11% of private-sector jobs and revenues equal to 21% of GDP, and the twelve largest tech companies, all venture-backed, were together worth more than $2 trillion (ch. 7, PDF p. 69).

The chapter then generalizes beyond investors: a founder invests by spending her time, and an individual invests by choosing a career, so diversification is the wrong instinct in both cases — a person cannot run dozens of companies or hold dozens of careers in reserve (ch. 7, PDF pp. 69–70). Schooling is blamed for teaching the opposite through uniform course periods and course catalogs implying it does not matter what you do so long as you do it well (ch. 7, PDF p. 70). The practical conclusion is that not everyone talented should found a company, since 0.01% of Google was worth more than $35 million at the time of writing while 100% of a failure is nothing, and that within a company the most important things are singular: one market, one distribution strategy, and a few moments that matter far more than others (ch. 7, PDF pp. 70–71).

### 8. Secrets (PDF pp. 72–81)

A secret is defined against two neighbors: a conventional truth, which everybody already knows and gives no edge, and a mystery, which is impossible rather than merely hard (ch. 8, PDF pp. 72–73). Every correct answer to the question of what valuable company nobody is building is therefore a secret — important, unknown, hard but doable (ch. 8, PDF p. 73). The chapter's extreme case of disbelief is Kaczynski, whose manifesto divided goals into those satisfiable with minimal effort, those needing serious effort, and those unattainable, and argued that modern people are unhappy because the hard problems are solved; the author accepts the trichotomy while rejecting the conclusion, and finds the same loss of faith in hipster nostalgia and in fundamentalisms that leave no zone of hard truths between catechism and mystery (ch. 8, PDF pp. 73–74).

Four social trends are blamed for rooting out belief in secrets: incrementalism, which rewards doing exactly what is asked and explains academics chasing many trivial publications; risk aversion, since a secret has not been vetted and being lonely and wrong is unbearable; complacency among elites who can collect rents on what is already done; and flatness, the sense that in one global talent pool somebody smarter would have found it already (ch. 8, PDF pp. 75–76). The consequence he names is that today you cannot start a cult — fewer crazy cults, at the cost of a sense of wonder (ch. 8, PDF p. 76).

A world without secrets would have no hidden injustice, which the chapter tests against abolition, a moral truth held by a small minority in the early nineteenth century, and against efficient markets, refuted for him by the 1999 internet valuations and by Greenspan's 2005 statement that a national housing bubble did not appear likely (ch. 8, PDF pp. 76–77). The corporate case is Hewlett-Packard: worth $9 billion in 1990, then the DeskJet 500C in 1991, the OmniBook in 1993, and the OfficeJet in 1994, reaching $135 billion by mid-2000; then a services arm in 2001, the Compaq merger in 2002, a market capitalization of $70 billion by 2005 after a branding campaign about inventing replaced inventing, a board split between Tom Perkins's technology faction and chairwoman Patricia Dunn's night-watchman view, the leak investigation with its illegal wiretaps, and $23 billion by late 2012 (ch. 8, PDF pp. 77–78). The case for secrets is made from Wiles proving Fermat's Last Theorem in 1995 after 358 years, having worked in secret from 1986 until 1993 (ch. 8, PDF p. 78), and from businesses built on spare capacity hidden in plain sight: Airbnb's unoccupied rooms and Lyft and Uber connecting riders with drivers (ch. 8, PDF p. 79).

Finding them is split into secrets of nature and secrets about people, with the second called underappreciated and reached by asking what people are not allowed to talk about and what is taboo (ch. 8, PDF pp. 79–80). The author's own monopoly thesis is offered as a secret reachable either way, quantitatively through profits competed away or humanly through what company leaders may not say (ch. 8, PDF p. 80). The best place to look is where nobody else is, meaning fields that matter but have not been standardized; nutrition is his example, unavailable as a Harvard major, with most large studies done thirty or forty years ago and the food pyramid attributed more to lobbying than to science (ch. 8, PDF p. 80). What to do with a secret is neither tell nobody nor tell everybody: the golden mean is a company, which he describes as a conspiracy to change the world whose recipient becomes a fellow conspirator (ch. 8, PDF p. 81).

### 9. Foundations (PDF pp. 82–89)

The chapter's rule, which the author says friends call Thiel's law, is that a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed (ch. 9, PDF p. 82). Beginnings are argued to be qualitatively different, from the first microseconds of the universe to the Constitutional Convention, whose compromises have been amended only seventeen times since the Bill of Rights in 1791, leaving California and Alaska equal in the Senate (ch. 9, PDF p. 82). The first decision is whom to start with, likened to marriage: the author's example is an investment he made in a company Luke Nosek founded with an MBA type met at a networking event, which failed and lost his money, and the resulting rule is that founders should share a prehistory rather than roll dice (ch. 9, PDF p. 83).

Three concepts are then separated — **ownership**, who legally holds the equity; **possession**, who runs the company day to day; and **control**, who formally governs it — allocated in a typical startup to founders, employees and investors, to managers and employees, and to a board (ch. 9, PDF p. 84). The DMV is the extreme case of misalignment, where citizens' ownership is fictional and clerks' possession is real (ch. 9, PDF pp. 84–85). Large companies misalign ownership against possession: a General Motors chief executive owns a trivial share and is therefore paid to post good quarterly results, and even stock compensation tied to short-term performance rewards cutting costs over investing (ch. 9, PDF p. 85). In startups the founders usually hold both ownership and possession, so conflict erupts instead between founders and the board, whose interests diverge over time — a director wanting an early public offering against founders wanting to stay private (ch. 9, PDF p. 85). The board rule is that less is more: three is called ideal, five a maximum for a private company, and a very large board is said to exercise no effective oversight and to provide cover for whoever actually runs the organization; public companies average nine because regulation effectively requires it (ch. 9, PDF pp. 85–86).

Everyone involved should be full-time, the author argues, since anyone without options or a salary is biased toward near-term value, which is his case against consultants, part-time employees, and even remote work (ch. 9, PDF p. 86). On pay, he reports the clearest pattern he has seen from investing in hundreds of startups: a company does better the less it pays its chief executive, and no early-stage venture-backed one should pay more than $150,000 a year, because at $300,000 the holder starts defending the status quo; Aaron Levie of Box is the example, living in an unfurnished one-bedroom apartment four years in, and the alternative rule is that the founder's own salary sets the ceiling (ch. 9, PDF pp. 86–87). Equity is offered as the compensation that orients people to the future, with the difficulties stated: equal shares seem arbitrary, unequal shares seem unfair, and later employees can matter more than earlier ones who hold more — a secretary who joined eBay in 1996 might have made 200 times her 1999-joining boss, and the graffiti artist who painted Facebook's walls in 2005 held stock worth $200 million against $2 million for a talented engineer who joined in 2010 (ch. 9, PDF pp. 87–88). Since perfect fairness is impossible, the author advises keeping the allocation secret (ch. 9, PDF p. 88). He also notes that most people do not want equity, the example being a PayPal consultant who negotiated $5,000 a day in cash and refused options, and treats that illiquidity as the point: preferring equity reveals a preference for the long term (ch. 9, PDF p. 88). The chapter ends with a second sense of founding — it lasts as long as the company is creating new things and ends when creation stops (ch. 9, PDF pp. 88–89).

### 10. The Mechanics of Mafia (PDF pp. 90–95)

The chapter opens on the perks caricature of Silicon Valley culture and rejects it as substance-free, with the formulation that no company has a culture because every company is a culture (ch. 10, PDF p. 90). The evidence is the PayPal alumni network: PayPal sold to eBay for $1.5 billion in 2002, and its people went on to SpaceX, Tesla, LinkedIn, YouTube, Yelp, Yammer, and Palantir, each worth more than $1 billion at the time of writing (ch. 10, PDF pp. 90–91). The counter-case is the New York law firm where the author worked, whose lawyers were individually impressive and whose relationships were thin, which he argues is irrational rather than merely cold because time is the asset being spent (ch. 10, PDF p. 91).

On recruiting, the rule is that it is a core competency and must never be outsourced, and the diagnostic question is why the twentieth employee should join rather than take more money and prestige at Google (ch. 10, PDF pp. 91–92). Undifferentiated pitches about valuable stock, smart colleagues, and important problems are named as bad answers because every company makes them; the two kinds of good answer are about the mission and about the team, and the mission has to be something important that nobody else will get done (ch. 10, PDF p. 92). The advice on perks is not to fight that war: cover the basics and offer irreplaceable work on a unique problem, since anyone swayed by laundry pickup or pet day care would be a bad addition (ch. 10, PDF p. 92).

Two symmetrical rules follow, both stated by the author about his own company. From the outside, he writes, everyone should be different in the same way — the branded startup hoodie as uniform, and Max Levchin's view that early staff should be as personally similar as possible so that a small team with limited resources shares an understanding of the world (ch. 10, PDF p. 93). From the inside, every individual should be sharply distinguished by her work: the author's best act as a PayPal manager was making every person responsible for exactly one unique thing, which he adopted to simplify management and found reduced conflict, since most internal fights are over the same responsibility and internal strife is what leaves a company open to outside threats (ch. 10, PDF pp. 93–94). Culture is then plotted on a spectrum from cults to consulting firms such as Accenture, which he characterizes as lacking a mission of its own; the best startups sit near the cult end, the difference being that cults are fanatically wrong about something important and a successful startup is fanatically right about something others missed (ch. 10, PDF pp. 94–95).

### 11. If You Build It, Will They Come? (PDF pp. 96–105)

The chapter argues that distribution — its catchall term for everything it takes to sell a product — is systematically underrated, especially by engineers, and opens with the B Ship of salespeople from *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* (ch. 11, PDF p. 96). The figures given are a US advertising industry of $150 billion in annual revenue employing more than 600,000 people, a US sales industry of $450 billion, and 3.2 million Americans working in sales (ch. 11, PDF p. 97). Advertising is defended on the ground that it works, and that it does not exist to make an immediate sale but to leave impressions that drive later ones, so the person who thinks himself immune is doubly deceived (ch. 11, PDF p. 97). Engineering is transparent, where a solution works or fails; sales is the opposite, and what nerds miss is the work it takes to make sales look easy (ch. 11, PDF p. 97).

Sales is said to work best hidden, which the author reads in the job titles — account executive, business development, investment banker, politician — and in Tom Sawyer getting his friends to pay for the privilege of whitewashing the fence (ch. 11, PDF pp. 97–98). A product that sells itself is called an impossible claim, and the conclusion is that distribution is part of the design of the product: an invention with no effective way to sell it is a bad business however good the product (ch. 11, PDF p. 98).

The distribution methods are laid out as a continuum bounded by two numbers, customer lifetime value against customer acquisition cost, with higher-priced products justifying more spend (ch. 11, PDF p. 99). **Complex sales** covers seven-figure average deals needing months of relationship-building and a sale every year or two; SpaceX is the example, persuading NASA to sign billion-dollar contracts against an incumbent aerospace industry employing more than 500,000 people across all fifty states, and Palantir is the case of complex sales with no salespeople at all, its chief executive on the road twenty-five days a month against deal sizes of $1 million to $100 million; such businesses succeed on 50% to 100% year-over-year growth over a decade, and enterprise selling starts small because a new customer rarely signs a deal out of scale with what has been sold before (ch. 11, PDF pp. 99–100). **Personal sales** covers deals of roughly $10,000 to $100,000 where the problem is a repeatable process rather than any one sale; Box is the example, beginning in 2009 with a small account at the Stanford Sleep Clinic and growing to a university-wide offering, where an enterprise-wide first approach would have sold nothing, and ZocDoc is the case where the salespeople also build the network that makes the product more valuable (ch. 11, PDF pp. 100–101). **Distribution doldrums** is the dead zone between the two: a product priced near $1,000 sold to small businesses, for which advertising is too broad or too inefficient and personal sales too expensive, which the author offers as the reason so many small and medium-sized businesses do not use tools larger firms take for granted — distribution as the hidden bottleneck (ch. 11, PDF p. 101). **Marketing and advertising** suit low-priced mass-appeal products with no viral path; Warby Parker is the worked case at about $100 a pair and a lifetime value of a few hundred dollars, and the author's own failure is the PayPal launch event with James Doohan, against Priceline's later success with William Shatner, which he attributes to Priceline already being a major player (ch. 11, PDF pp. 102–103). **Viral marketing** requires the core functionality to invite more users; PayPal's own numbers are given as an initial base of 24 people, all employees, and a paid signup and referral strategy costing $20 per customer that produced 7% daily growth, nearly doubling the user base every ten days (ch. 11, PDF p. 103). The segment choice is part of the same argument: Western Union's emigrant remitters transacted too infrequently, while eBay's roughly 20,000 PowerSellers had a high velocity of money and a terrible incumbent payment option (ch. 11, PDF p. 103).

The chapter closes with two generalizations. Distribution follows a power law of its own: one method will be far more powerful than the rest, the kitchen-sink approach fails, most businesses get zero channels working, and poor sales rather than bad product is the most common cause of failure — one working channel is a great business, several attempted and none nailed is finished (ch. 11, PDF p. 104). And selling extends to non-customers: employees, investors, and the media, since a prospective hire will search for the company and what he finds matters (ch. 11, PDF p. 104). The last line generalizes it to everyone, including a company of one person: if you see no salespeople, you are the salesperson (ch. 11, PDF p. 105).

### 12. Man and Machine (PDF pp. 106–114)

The chapter's claim is that computers are complements for humans rather than substitutes, and that the most valuable businesses of the coming decades will be built by entrepreneurs who empower people rather than make them obsolete (ch. 12, PDF pp. 106–107). It opens with the substitution view it rejects: Deep Blue beating Kasparov in 1997, Watson beating Ken Jennings in 2011, self-driving cars already on California roads, Andreessen on software eating the world, and Andy Kessler on productivity coming from getting rid of people (ch. 12, PDF p. 106). The distinction drawn is that people compete for jobs and resources while computers compete for neither: globalization means substitution, because humans really can stand in for one another and also demand the same food, fuel, and shelter, whereas a computer needs only a nominal amount of electricity and is not even smart enough to want it (ch. 12, PDF pp. 107–109). On the supply side men and machines are called good at fundamentally different things — people form plans and make judgments, computers process data — and the illustration is a 2012 Google supercomputer that learned to identify a cat with 75% accuracy after scanning 10 million video thumbnails, against an average four-year-old who does it flawlessly (ch. 12, PDF p. 108).

The business case is PayPal's fraud problem: losses running above $10 million a month in mid-2000 against hundreds or thousands of transactions a minute, a fully automated detection system that the fraudsters adapted around within an hour or two, and the hybrid that replaced it — software flagging the most suspicious transactions on a designed interface for human operators to judge — which the author credits with PayPal's first quarterly profit in the first quarter of 2002 against a $29.3 million quarterly loss a year earlier (ch. 12, PDF p. 110). Palantir is described as the generalization of that pattern, founded in 2004 and on track for $1 billion of sales in 2014, positioned between the CIA's human bias and the NSA's computer bias, with its software flagging activity for a trained analyst (ch. 12, PDF pp. 110–111). LinkedIn is the same argument in recruiting: it did not poll recruiters for pain points and did not try to replace them, but changed how they work, and more than 97% of recruiters use it (ch. 12, PDF p. 112). The author's diagnosis of why complementarity is missed is professional formation — computer scientists are trained to reduce human capabilities into tasks a machine can be taught — and two buzzwords, machine learning, whose very name evokes replacement, and big data, which he calls usually dumb data because computers find patterns but cannot compare patterns across sources or interpret complex behavior (ch. 12, PDF pp. 112–113). Strong artificial intelligence is set aside rather than denied: he calls replacement by computers a worry for the twenty-second century, and says indefinite fears about the far future should not stop definite plans today (ch. 12, PDF pp. 113–114).

### 13. Seeing Green (PDF pp. 115–129)

The cleantech bubble is used as a worked failure against the book's own checklist. More than $50 billion went into thousands of cleantech companies, and more than forty solar manufacturers went bankrupt or out of business in 2012 alone (ch. 13, PDF p. 115). The author rejects the conservative explanation that government priority poisoned the sector and offers instead **the seven questions every business must answer**: engineering, whether the technology is a breakthrough rather than an increment; timing, whether now is right for this particular business; monopoly, whether you are starting with a big share of a small market; people, whether you have the right team; distribution, whether you can deliver and not only create; durability, whether the position is defensible ten and twenty years out; and secret, whether you have found a unique opportunity others do not see (ch. 13, PDF pp. 116–117). Nailing all seven is said to master fortune, five or six might work, and the striking thing about cleantech was companies started with zero good answers (ch. 13, PDF p. 117).

Each question is then failed in turn. **Engineering**: Solyndra's cylindrical cells are roughly a factor of pi less efficient than flat ones at receiving direct sunlight, and a laboratory gain of 20% does not survive real-world expense, risk, and the customer's practiced skepticism, which is why the book's threshold is ten times better (ch. 13, PDF pp. 117–118). **Timing**: SpectraWatt's chief executive likened solar in 2008 to microprocessors in the late 1970s, but the first silicon solar cell was made at Bell Labs in 1954 at about 6% efficiency and neither crystalline silicon nor thin film had exceeded 25% in the field, so the improvement had been slow and linear rather than exponential (ch. 13, PDF pp. 118–119). **Monopoly**: John Doerr's line that green is the new red, white and blue is answered with the observation that trillion-dollar markets mean bloody competition; MiaSolé's chief executive told a congressional panel in 2006 that his was one of several strong startups in one kind of thin film and predicted it would be the world's largest producer within a year, and it was sold in 2013 for hundreds of millions less than investors had put in; the same 100 megawatts of installed capacity is 10.53% of US solar, under 1% of global solar, 0.02% of global renewables, and a drop against 15,000 gigawatts of total global generation, depending on how the market is drawn (ch. 13, PDF pp. 119–121). **People**: failed cleantech companies are described as run by nontechnical salesman-executives, and Founders Fund's stated rule was to pass on any company whose founders wore suits to pitch meetings, on the reasoning that a chief executive who looks like a salesman is probably bad at sales and worse at technology (ch. 13, PDF pp. 121–122). **Distribution**: Better Place raised and spent more than $800 million from 2007 to 2012 and sold about 1,000 cars, each of which required approval, proximity to a swapping station, predictable routes, and a fueling subscription, and its assets went for $12 million in 2013 (ch. 13, PDF pp. 122–123). **Durability**: Evergreen Solar and Abound Solar blamed Chinese pricing, Energy Conversion Devices sued three Chinese manufacturers for $950 million, and meanwhile fracked shale went from 1.7% of American natural gas in 2000 to 4.1% in 2005 to 34% by 2013 with gas prices down more than 70% since 2008 (ch. 13, PDF pp. 123–124). **Secret**: the sector justified itself with conventional truths about needing a cleaner world, which the author contrasts with the specific reasons for success that other people do not see (ch. 13, PDF pp. 124–125).

Two further arguments close the chapter. Social entrepreneurship is criticized on the ambiguity of the word social — good for society, or merely seen as good by society — and on the claim that progress is held back by the sameness of corporations and nonprofits rather than by any difference between them, cleantech being the result: many undifferentiated products under one overbroad goal (ch. 13, PDF p. 125). Tesla is scored against all seven questions as the counter-case: technology other carmakers license, a one-time subsidy window taken with a $465 million Department of Energy loan in January 2010, a start in high-end electric sports cars at about 3,000 Roadsters at $109,000 each before more than 20,000 sedans in 2013, a team led by an engineer-salesman, ownership of its own distribution chain rather than dealerships, durability from a head start and a coveted brand with the founder still in charge, and the secret that cleantech was more a social phenomenon than an environmental imperative (ch. 13, PDF pp. 126–128). The generalization offered is that no sector is ever so important that participating in it suffices, and that the entrepreneurs who build a new energy business face the paradoxical task of thinking small — replacing diesel on remote islands, or modular reactors for military installations (ch. 13, PDF pp. 128–129).

### 14. The Founder's Paradox (PDF pp. 130–143)

The chapter asks which personal traits matter in a founder and why a company led by a distinctive individual is both more powerful and more dangerous than one led by an interchangeable manager (ch. 14, PDF p. 131). The opening data point is PayPal's own six founders: four had built bombs in high school, five were 23 or younger, four were born outside the United States, three had escaped communist countries, and the one conventional American among them took a third of the salary a bank had offered (ch. 14, PDF p. 130). Where a normal population's traits plot as a bell curve, the author argues founders' traits follow an inverse normal distribution, since opposite traits coexist in them — cash poor and millionaire on paper, sullen and charismatic, insider and outsider, famous and infamous (ch. 14, PDF pp. 131–133). He explicitly leaves the cause open between nature, nurture, strategic exaggeration by the founder, and exaggeration by everyone else, and says all four can reinforce each other in a cycle (ch. 14, PDF p. 133). The illustrations are Richard Branson, of whom he says it is hard to tell whether the media made him or he made the media; Sean Parker, arrested as a minor, then Napster with 10 million users in its first year and a shutdown order twenty months in, then Facebook's founding president and a 2005 departure that enhanced his notoriety; and Lady Gaga as a personal brand whose self-manufacture is itself evidence of an unusual person (ch. 14, PDF pp. 134–136).

The anthropological detour makes founders and scapegoats the same shape: Oedipus and Romulus as insider/outsiders, and the scapegoat as simultaneously the weakest member, powerless to stop his victimization, and the most powerful, since he can end the conflict — with sacrificial victims worshipped before execution, which the author offers as the root of monarchy (ch. 14, PDF pp. 136–137). Modern celebrity is read the same way through Elvis, Michael Jackson, Britney Spears, and the 27 Club (ch. 14, PDF pp. 137–139). Two technology cases follow. Howard Hughes built Houston's first radio transmitter at 11 and its first motorcycle a year later, made nine commercially successful films by 30, set airspeed and flight records, and after his third and worst crash in 1946 withdrew for thirty years (ch. 14, PDF p. 139). Bill Gates held about 90% of the operating-system market in 2000, was ordered broken up in June 2000, had already stepped down as chief executive six months earlier after spending most of his time on legal threats, won the breakup on appeal and settled in 2001, but by then Microsoft had lost the full engagement of its founder (ch. 14, PDF p. 140). Against that, Jobs's return: pushed out in 1985 after clashing with the professional chief executive brought in for adult supervision, hired back as interim chief executive in 1997 when credentialed predecessors had nearly bankrupted the company and Michael Dell said he would shut it down and return the money, then the iPod in 2001, the iPhone in 2007, and the iPad in 2010, with Apple the most valuable company in the world by 2012 (ch. 14, PDF p. 142).

The chapter's two lessons are stated as a pair and then qualified by a third. His lesson for business is that it needs founders and should be more tolerant of ones who seem strange, because unusual individuals lead companies past mere incrementalism; his lesson for founders is that adulation can turn into demonization at any moment (ch. 14, PDF p. 143). The qualification is aimed at the founder's own self-estimate: a founder matters because he can bring out the best work from everybody at the company, not because his is the only work with value, and the chapter explicitly refuses the Randian prime mover, calling her villains real and her heroes fake and denying that there is any secession from society (ch. 14, PDF p. 143). Both errors are named: a founder who believes his own myth loses his mind, and a business that loses all sense of myth mistakes disenchantment for wisdom (ch. 14, PDF p. 143).

### Conclusion: Stagnation or Singularity? (PDF pp. 144–147)

Four patterns for the future are taken from Nick Bostrom: recurrent collapse and recovery, a plateau of convergence on the life of today's richest countries, a collapse nobody survives, and an accelerating takeoff (Conclusion, PDF pp. 144–145). Recurrent collapse is called unlikely because the knowledge underlying civilization is too widespread, leaving annihilation the more probable form of catastrophe (Conclusion, PDF p. 146). The plateau is the scenario most people expect, and the author's argument against its durability is that competition for scarce resources without new technology turns stagnation into conflict, and global conflict into extinction (Conclusion, PDF p. 146). On the fourth, he distinguishes his own position from Kurzweil's: tracing exponential trends does not make the future happen on its own, and what the Singularity would look like matters less than the present choice between nothing and something (Conclusion, PDF pp. 146–147). The closing instruction is to find singular ways to make new things, with thinking for yourself as the essential first step (Conclusion, PDF p. 147).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the authors limit the claim, admit a counter-case, or say a rule does not apply.

- **No formula.** The paradox of teaching entrepreneurship is that a formula cannot exist, since every innovation is new and unique, so no authority can prescribe how to be innovative; the book is called an exercise in thinking rather than a manual or a record of knowledge (Preface, PDF p. 6; ch. 1, PDF p. 11).
- **Which monopolies are meant.** The word is narrowed twice: the book excludes the firm that eliminates rivals deviously and the one holding a state license, and it grants that in a static world a monopolist is a rent collector whose bad reputation is deserved (ch. 3, PDF pp. 21, 27). The author also grants it is possible to question whether a legally enforceable monopoly should be awarded for something like a mobile software design (ch. 3, PDF p. 27).
- **Inverting the dogmas is not the answer.** Having listed four opposites to the post-crash lessons, the chapter says the opposite ideas are not automatically true and that you cannot escape the madness of crowds by dogmatically rejecting them (ch. 2, PDF p. 19).
- **Sometimes you fight.** The rule against competing has an explicit exception: sometimes you do have to fight, and then there is no middle ground — throw no punches, or strike hard and end it quickly; if you cannot beat a rival it may be better to merge, which is what the author did with X.com (ch. 4, PDF p. 34).
- **The four characteristics are not a checklist.** The chapter says so directly, and adds that there is no shortcut to monopoly (ch. 5, PDF p. 38). Branding is the weakest of the four on its own: the surface techniques are imitable, Yahoo! is the counter-case, and no technology company can be built on branding alone (ch. 5, PDF pp. 40–41).
- **Small is not nonexistent.** The instruction to start with a very small market is bounded by the author's own PalmPilot failure, where the users were scattered, had little in common, and used their devices episodically, so nobody needed the product (ch. 5, PDF p. 42).
- **Disruption has a real meaning.** The attack is on the buzzword, not on the original term of art for a low-end product improving until it overtakes incumbents, which the chapter says is roughly what personal computers did to mainframes (ch. 5, PDF p. 43).
- **Scaling has hidden obstacles.** eBay is the author's own limiting case: auctions work for distinctive items such as coins and stamps and less well for commodities, so eBay is still a valuable monopoly but a smaller one than people expected in 2004 (ch. 5, PDF p. 43).
- **The luck debate cannot be settled.** Companies are not experiments, every company starts once, and statistics does not work at a sample size of one (ch. 6, PDF pp. 46–47). He also grants that already successful people have an easier time of new things through networks, wealth, and experience (ch. 6, PDF p. 46).
- **Three of the four futures work.** Definite optimism, definite pessimism, and indefinite pessimism are each said to work on their own terms; only indefinite optimism is called inherently unsustainable (ch. 6, PDF p. 59).
- **Even disciplined investors hold a portfolio.** Nobody can know in advance which companies will succeed, so the best firms still hold several; the rule is that every one of them must have the potential to succeed at vast scale (ch. 7, PDF p. 67).
- **Not everyone should found a company.** The power law implies that people who understand it will hesitate more than others before founding, because joining the very best growing company can pay better, and the author says too many people are starting their own companies (ch. 7, PDF p. 70).
- **Some things are mysteries, not secrets.** The chapter's own boundary is that difficult things are achievable and impossible ones are not, and string theory is offered as a candidate for the second (ch. 8, PDF pp. 72–73).
- **The full-time rule is breakable.** It usually makes sense to hire outside lawyers and accountants (ch. 9, PDF p. 86).
- **Board size has a stated carve-out.** Three is ideal and five a maximum unless the company is publicly held, where regulation effectively mandates larger boards averaging nine (ch. 9, PDF p. 85).
- **Equity has no fair formula.** Equal shares seem arbitrary and unequal ones seem unfair, there is no ownership formula that avoids resentment, later employees can matter more than earlier ones who hold more, and equity cannot create perfect incentives — it is offered only as the best available alignment (ch. 9, PDF pp. 87–88). The author also records that most people do not want equity at all, and gives a PayPal consultant who refused options for $5,000 a day in cash (ch. 9, PDF p. 88). On cash he concedes a gradation rather than a rule: a bonus is slightly better than a salary because it is contingent (ch. 9, PDF p. 87).
- **The sales model has to match the price.** Complex sales is the only way to sell some of the most valuable products, but the same approach would have made Box a forgotten failure, and the chapter names an admitted dead zone — the distribution doldrums around a $1,000 product, where advertising is too broad or inefficient and personal sales too expensive (ch. 11, PDF pp. 99–101).
- **Advertising is not ruled out.** It works for startups when acquisition cost and lifetime value make every other channel uneconomical, Warby Parker being the case; what the chapter warns against is competing with big companies' campaigns, which is the author's own PayPal failure against Priceline's success (ch. 11, PDF pp. 102–103).
- **The best product does not always win.** Path dependence is accepted as true, with the caveat that today's operating systems and keyboard layouts were not imposed by mere chance (ch. 11, PDF p. 98).
- **Five or six of seven might work.** The seven questions are stated with that allowance, and the point made about cleantech is that companies were started with zero good answers (ch. 13, PDF p. 117). Entering a slow-moving market is likewise allowed, but only with a definite and realistic plan to take it over (ch. 13, PDF p. 119).
- **Cleantech's premise was right.** The chapter says Tesla proves nothing was inherently wrong with cleantech, that the world really will need new sources of energy, and that fracking may not be a durable energy solution either (ch. 13, PDF pp. 124, 128).
- **Machine learning and big data do work.** The criticism is of the bias toward substitution, and the chapter grants that Netflix's and Amazon's recommendations and Google Translate's eighty languages are real results of the method (ch. 12, PDF p. 112).
- **Strong artificial intelligence is deferred, not denied.** It is called a worry for the twenty-second century, and the Luddite and futurist positions are named as mutually exclusive but not exhaustive (ch. 12, PDF pp. 113–114).
- **Do not overestimate the founder.** The argument for tolerating unusual founders is immediately bounded: founders matter because a great one brings out the best work from everybody, the Randian prime mover is rejected with the author calling her heroes fake, and the danger of believing your own myth is named alongside the opposite danger of disenchantment (ch. 14, PDF p. 143).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, company, or tool the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure.

- **Google at about 68% of search as of May 2014, against Microsoft's 19% and Yahoo!'s 10%, and the argument that monopolists conceal their position (ch. 3, PDF pp. 22, 25).** Both halves moved the author's way. Google's worldwide search share is reported at about 90% for 2025, and on August 5, 2024 a US District Court held that the company holds an illegal monopoly in search under section 2 of the Sherman Act, having paid phone makers and browser developers for default placement; Google said it would appeal (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Search). The concealment the chapter describes was adjudicated rather than merely observed.
- **US airlines making 37 cents per passenger trip in 2012 at an average one-way fare of $178 (ch. 3, PDF p. 20).** Much better now, still thin. IATA's 2025 forecast is $36.0 billion of industry net profit, which its director general characterized as $7.20 per passenger per segment (iata.org press release, June 2, 2025). The comparison is not like for like — IATA's figure is global and per segment, the book's is US and per trip — but the order of magnitude has changed.
- **Twitter valued at $24 billion on its 2013 listing while losing money, against the New York Times Company's $133 million of 2012 earnings (ch. 5, PDF p. 35).** The premium did not hold as a monopoly story. Elon Musk completed the acquisition in October 2022 for $44 billion, renamed the platform X in July 2023, and in March 2025 X Corp. was acquired by xAI in a deal valuing X at $33 billion of equity and $45 billion including debt; by February 2026 SpaceX had acquired xAI (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twitter).
- **LinkedIn's $24.5 billion market capitalization in early 2014 on under $1 billion of revenue and $21.6 million of 2012 net income (ch. 5, PDF p. 36).** The future cash flows arrived. Microsoft bought LinkedIn for $26.2 billion, completed December 8, 2016; revenue was about $19.8 billion for fiscal 2026 and registered users 1.3 billion as of March 2026 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LinkedIn).
- **More than 97% of recruiters using LinkedIn (ch. 12, PDF p. 112).** Unchecked; no first-party source for the share was reached.
- **Palantir on track to book sales of $1 billion in 2014 (ch. 12, PDF p. 111).** The 2014 number is unconfirmed — later summaries record analysts expecting nearly $1 billion in *contracts* that year rather than revenue, and no 2014 revenue figure was found. The direction held well past the claim: Palantir direct-listed on the NYSE on September 30, 2020, reported $4.48 billion of revenue for 2025, and its market capitalization exceeded $400 billion in 2025 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palantir_Technologies).
- **Hewlett-Packard worth about $23 billion in late 2012, as the cautionary tale of a company that stopped believing in secrets (ch. 8, PDF p. 78).** The subject no longer exists as one company: on November 1, 2015 it split, the original entity renaming itself HP Inc. and spinning off Hewlett Packard Enterprise (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hewlett-Packard).
- **Yahoo! under Marissa Mayer as the case of beginning with brand rather than substance (ch. 5, PDF p. 41).** It ended the way the chapter implied. Verizon announced the acquisition of Yahoo's core internet business on July 25, 2016 for $4.83 billion, cut the price by $350 million on February 21, 2017 after the data-breach disclosures, and closed on June 13, 2017, at which point Mayer resigned; Apollo Global Management bought 90% from Verizon in September 2021 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahoo!).
- **Zynga and Groupon as fast growth masking long-term problems (ch. 5, PDF p. 37).** Both bore it out. Take-Two announced the acquisition of Zynga on January 10, 2022 in a cash-and-stock deal valued at $12.7 billion and closed it on May 23, 2022 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zynga). Groupon reported $515 million of revenue for 2023 with an operating loss of $18 million and 2,213 employees, against the biggest internet listing since Google in 2004 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupon).
- **Square's white card reader of October 2010 and the shape war that followed (ch. 4, PDF p. 32).** The originator outlasted the imitation. Square renamed itself Block, Inc. on December 10, 2021, reported $24.12 billion of revenue for 2024, and joined the S&P 500 on July 23, 2025 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block,_Inc.).
- **Tesla scoring seven for seven, with about 3,000 Roadsters at $109,000 and more than 20,000 sedans in 2013, and owning its own distribution chain (ch. 13, PDF pp. 126–128).** Both hold and have grown. Tesla produced 1.66 million vehicles on $94.83 billion of revenue in 2025, and still sells through its own website and nearly 400 company-owned stores and galleries in more than 35 countries rather than franchised dealers (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla,_Inc.).
- **Better Place raising and spending more than $800 million, selling about 1,000 cars, and its assets going for $12 million in 2013 (ch. 13, PDF pp. 122–123).** The funding and the volume hold — roughly $850 million raised, fewer than 1,000 Fluence Z.E. cars in Israel plus about 400 in Denmark, bankruptcy filed on May 26, 2013 — but the asset price does not: a $12 million offer fell through and the remaining assets sold for about $450,000 to Gnrgy in November 2013 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Better_Place_(company)).
- **Photovoltaic efficiency improving slowly and linearly, with neither crystalline silicon nor thin film exceeding 25% in the field (ch. 13, PDF pp. 118–119).** Still broadly right for what a customer buys and understated for the laboratory. The best commercial modules reached about 24.5% as of 2025, while a silicon heterojunction cell hit 26.63% in 2017 and a perovskite-silicon tandem 32.5% in 2022 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_cell_efficiency).
- **Fracked shale rising from 1.7% of American natural gas in 2000 to 34% by 2013 (ch. 13, PDF p. 124).** It kept going. The EIA reports that about 78%, or 37.87 trillion cubic feet, of total US dry natural gas production came from shale formations in 2023 (eia.gov/tools/faqs, id 907).
- **Eroom's law, new drugs approved per billion dollars of R&D halving every nine years since 1950 (ch. 6, PDF p. 57).** The term and the original finding hold — Jack Scannell coined it in 2012 — but no source was reached that reports whether the trend continued, flattened, or reversed afterward, so the current state is unchecked (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eroom%27s_law).
- **Replacement by computers as a worry for the twenty-second century, and strong artificial intelligence as remote (ch. 12, PDF p. 113).** The date the author set has been pressed hard by events he did not have: GPT-2 in 2019, GPT-3 in 2020, ChatGPT's consumer release in late 2022, GPT-4 in 2023, and reasoning models from 2024 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model). Whether these substitute for or complement knowledge workers is unchecked: the source consulted reports capabilities without displacement or productivity measurements.
- **Self-driving cars already on California roads in 2014 (ch. 12, PDF p. 106).** Far past that now. Waymo runs commercial robotaxi services in ten US metropolitan areas with about 3,871 vehicles and 500,000 paid rides a week as of June 2026, having started in Phoenix on October 8, 2020 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waymo).
- **Warby Parker as the advertising case at about $100 a pair, selling online instead of through retail distributors (ch. 11, PDF p. 102).** The channel the example illustrates is no longer the company's main one. It direct-listed on the NYSE on September 29, 2021, and reported $771 million of 2024 revenue and 2.28 million customers, with about two-thirds of revenue coming from roughly 276 physical stores (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warby_Parker).
- **Box as the personal-sales case, and Aaron Levie as the low-pay case (ch. 9, PDF p. 87; ch. 11, PDF pp. 100–101).** Both still standing. Box went public on January 23, 2015 and reported $1.09 billion of revenue for the year to January 31, 2025, with Levie still chief executive (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box,_Inc.).
- **ZocDoc charging doctors a few hundred dollars a month and serving more than 5 million people a month (ch. 11, PDF p. 101).** The pricing model changed and the usage grew: Zocdoc moved from subscription to a per-booking referral fee in some markets in January 2019, and more than six million patients a month were booking by 2017; its last public valuation is $1.8 billion in August 2015 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zocdoc). The chapter discloses that ZocDoc is a Founders Fund portfolio company.
- **Venture-backed companies creating 11% of private-sector jobs and revenues equal to 21% of GDP (ch. 7, PDF p. 69).** The figure traces to the National Venture Capital Association and is repeated in later summaries without a year attached; no independent later measurement was reached, so it is unchecked (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venture_capital).
- **Napster amassing 10 million users in its first year and being ordered shut twenty months after opening (ch. 5, PDF p. 44; ch. 14, PDF p. 135).** The dates do not line up cleanly against the later record: Napster launched in June 1999, a preliminary injunction came in July 2000 at thirteen months, verified use peaked at about 26.4 million users in February 2001, and the network shut down in July 2001 at twenty-five months (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napster). The brand has changed hands repeatedly since, most recently to Infinite Reality for $207 million in March 2025.
- **More than 1.5 billion people with instant access to the world's knowledge on pocket-sized devices (ch. 12, PDF p. 106).** Grown: about 75% of the world's population were smartphone users by 2020 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartphone). An absolute current count was not found in the source consulted.
- **Apple at $500 billion in January 2013 against $467 billion for Google and Microsoft combined, offered as the price the two rivals paid for fighting each other (ch. 4, PDF pp. 31–32).** All three are far larger now, and the ordering has changed twice over: as of June 30, 2026 the largest companies by market capitalization are Nvidia at about $4.85 trillion, Alphabet at about $4.31 trillion, Apple at about $4.25 trillion, Microsoft at about $2.77 trillion, and Amazon at about $2.56 trillion (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_public_corporations_by_market_capitalization). The snapshot the chapter used to date the cost of the rivalry did not hold as a ranking.
- **Not checked:** Founders Fund's 2005 fund returns and the rule drawn from them (ch. 7, PDF p. 66); the Andreessen Horowitz Instagram figures of $250,000 into $78 million (ch. 7, PDF p. 66); PayPal's fraud losses above $10 million a month and the $29.3 million quarterly loss (ch. 12, PDF p. 110); the $20 acquisition cost and 7% daily growth (ch. 11, PDF p. 103); Zuckerberg's words at the July 2006 board meeting (ch. 6, PDF p. 61); and the Founders Fund rule about passing on founders who wore suits (ch. 13, PDF p. 121). Each is the author's own account of a private company or a private meeting, with no outside source to check it against.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **The central claim is defined so that nothing can refute it.** Monopoly is given as the condition of every successful business, and the word is defined as being so good at what you do that no firm offers a close substitute (ch. 3, PDF pp. 21, 28). Any successful business therefore satisfies the definition by having succeeded, and the chapter offers no observation that would count against the thesis. The two-lies argument (ch. 3, PDF pp. 22–23) has the same shape from the other side: a firm that denies being a monopoly is concealing one, and a firm that claims uniqueness is exaggerating, so both denial and assertion confirm the reading.
- **One term covering two different things.** In the same chapters, monopoly means a measured market structure — Google at 68% of search — and also means any differentiated business, since a yoga studio is said to be hard to make a monopoly because it cannot serve millions (ch. 3, PDF p. 22; ch. 5, PDF p. 40). A word that stretches from an antitrust finding to "not a commodity" does less work than the argument needs.
- **The evidence is almost entirely the author's own.** PayPal supplies the bubble chapter, the viral-growth numbers, the one-thing management rule, the fraud-detection case, and the founder-traits opening; Palantir supplies the complementarity case; Founders Fund supplies the power law; and ZocDoc is disclosed as a portfolio company at the point of use (ch. 2, PDF pp. 15–16; ch. 7, PDF p. 66; ch. 10, PDF p. 94; ch. 11, PDF p. 101; ch. 12, PDF p. 110; ch. 14, PDF p. 130). The counter-examples that would test the rules — the author's own investments that answered the seven questions and failed — do not appear.
- **A retrospective scorecard presented as a predictive test.** The seven questions are introduced after the cleantech bubble burst and are then used to score companies whose outcomes were already known, with Tesla scoring seven for seven (ch. 13, PDF pp. 116–117, 126–128). No instance is given of the questions being applied to a company before its outcome, and no cleantech company is shown answering several of them well and failing anyway, which is what would distinguish the checklist from a description of what survived.
- **Correlation offered as cause.** Apple's overtaking of Microsoft and Google is attributed to the two rivals fighting each other, on a single comparison of market capitalizations in January 2013 (ch. 4, PDF pp. 31–32); section 7 notes that both companies are far larger now than then. The claim that HP declined because it stopped believing in secrets rests on a sequence of dates rather than on any comparison with firms that kept inventing (ch. 8, PDF pp. 77–78).
- **Figures without a source.** The airline margin of 37 cents per passenger trip (ch. 3, PDF p. 20), the US advertising industry at $150 billion and 600,000 people and the sales industry at $450 billion and 3.2 million (ch. 11, PDF p. 97), the 11% of private-sector jobs and 21% of GDP from venture-backed companies (ch. 7, PDF p. 69), the claim that more than 97% of recruiters use LinkedIn (ch. 12, PDF p. 112), and the market shares for search (ch. 3, PDF p. 22) are all stated without attribution in the text.
- **A claim the author's own argument undercuts.** The chapter on luck concedes that companies are not experiments and that statistics does not work at a sample size of one, then treats the existence of serial entrepreneurs as reason to reject the luck explanation (ch. 6, PDF pp. 46–47) — which is an inference from a sample the same paragraph says cannot support one, and does not address selection: the people who succeed once are the ones able to raise money and hire for a second attempt, a point the chapter grants about networks, wealth, and experience and then does not carry through.
- **Assertions with no evidence at all.** The book states that remote work should be avoided, that anyone without options or a salary is fundamentally misaligned, and that consultants and part-time employees therefore do not work (ch. 9, PDF p. 86). No study, no counted case, and no counter-case is offered, and the rule is broken in the same paragraph for lawyers and accountants. The cap of $150,000 on an early-stage chief executive's salary is presented as the clearest pattern the author has seen across hundreds of startups (ch. 9, PDF p. 86), with the pattern itself never shown.
- **Anthropology without a citation.** The scapegoat argument in chapter 14 — founders and scapegoats sharing a shape, sacrificial victims worshipped before execution, and monarchy descending from that — is carried entirely by myth and assertion, names no source for the mechanism it borrows, and moves from Aztec practice to a general claim about the roots of kingship in a paragraph (ch. 14, PDF pp. 136–137).
- **Internal tension about sameness.** The book's opening instruction is to think for yourself and refuse the crowd (ch. 2, PDF p. 19), while chapter 10 wants everyone at the company to be different in the same way and endorses making early staff as personally similar as possible, with shared reading and shared obsession (ch. 10, PDF p. 93). The same chapter places the best startups near the cult end of its own spectrum (ch. 10, PDF p. 95), which is a hard position to hold alongside a thesis that independent thought is the scarce input.
- **Internal tension about brand.** Chapter 5 says the surface techniques do not work without substance and that no technology company can be built on branding alone (ch. 5, PDF p. 41), while chapter 13 credits Tesla's win partly to a coveted brand and to the secret that cleantech was a social phenomenon more than an environmental imperative — that is, to how the car made its driver look (ch. 13, PDF pp. 127–128).
- **Secrets have no test.** The chapter distinguishes secrets from mysteries by whether a thing is hard or impossible, but gives no way to tell a secret from a false belief before the outcome is known; the operative standard offered is that belief in secrets is an effective truth (ch. 8, PDF p. 78). Nutrition is offered as a field likely to yield secrets on the grounds that it is under-institutionalized and that the food pyramid reflected lobbying (ch. 8, PDF p. 80), with no source for either claim.
- **Numbers that have moved.** The Better Place asset figure of $12 million, Palantir's projected $1 billion of 2014 sales, the fracking share of 34%, and the Twitter-versus-New York Times comparison were true, close to true, or forecast in 2013–2014 and are not now; section 7 has the later figures. The strong-artificial-intelligence timeline in chapter 12 is the one under most pressure: the chapter places replacement by computers in the twenty-second century without argument for the date, and the language models released since 2019 fall inside the window it treated as empty, though section 7 records that whether they substitute for knowledge workers is unchecked.
