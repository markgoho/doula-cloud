## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the authors' frame, is that a recurring-revenue software company grows predictably by learning what its best customers experienced, mapping that experience, measuring it, and fixing the worst gap in it, rather than by adding marketing tactics; and that the traditional marketing funnel is the wrong picture for such a company because it stops at the purchase, whereas a business paid month after month has to keep proving its value (ch. 1, PDF pp. 21–22). The named process is the Customer-Led Growth Framework, in three stages: learn from customers, map and measure their experience, and find the biggest levers for growth (ch. 1, PDF pp. 19–20). The evidence offered is the authors' own careers, Laudi at Unbounce and Suellentrop as the second employee at Calendly, each of which the book says grew sharply in two years on a seed round and without paid acquisition (ch. 1, PDF pp. 17, 21), plus a run of client engagements. The information the reader needs is said to be in one place, inside the best customers' heads, and the seven things to learn from them are listed as a sequence running from life before the product to how the customer has grown since (ch. 1, PDF p. 18).

The authors say what the claim is not. It is not a toolbox of tactics guaranteed to drive growth, since the reader is told they already know enough tactics; the offer is a blueprint for choosing among tools instead (ch. 1, PDF p. 24). It is not advice imported from another company, because what worked elsewhere with another audience is said not to transfer automatically (ch. 1, PDF p. 18). It is not an argument that the reader's ideas were bad, only that they were guesses (ch. 1, PDF p. 18). It is not solely a marketing book, in that the authors say marketing problems bring the reader in and the whole business benefits (ch. 1, PDF p. 24). And it is not a book to be read alone: chapter 2 makes a cross-functional team with named roles the precondition for everything after it (ch. 2, PDF pp. 27–28).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Foreword (Bob Moesta, PDF pp. 11–13)

Moesta, whom the foreword identifies as a co-architect of the Jobs-to-be-Done framework, tells one story as the argument. Running sales and marketing for a Michigan home builder, he took notes in a focus group where buyers named Energy Star compliance as their top want, ten times over; the company spent six months and changed windows, insulation, furnace, air conditioner, and foundations to offer it, and nobody bought it (Foreword, PDF p. 12). Watching a sale close, he saw that once buyers learned the upgrade cost about what a finished basement cost, they chose the basement every time, and homeowners he interviewed afterward said the same (Foreword, PDF p. 12). His reading is that what people say they want and what they do are different things, and that the experts had it wrong (Foreword, PDF pp. 12–13). He calls this book the sister volume to his own *Demand Side Sales 101* (Foreword, PDF p. 13).

### Chapter 1: Why You Should Read This Book (PDF pp. 16–25)

Laudi opens with her own state while leading marketing at a growing SaaS company: long hours, a list of tactics drawn from what other companies were doing and from trending channels, and marketing experiments she compares to petri dishes (ch. 1, PDF pp. 16–17). The diagnosis given for this is one sentence, that the reader is guessing (ch. 1, PDF p. 18). The remedy is the seven-item list of what to learn from customers, and the claim that with it a reader knows what to say, which channels to use, and which parts of the product to show first (ch. 1, PDF pp. 18–19). The three stages of the framework are named here and mapped to the book's parts II, III, and IV (ch. 1, PDF p. 19; Figure 1.1, PDF p. 20).

The naming story is the argument for the title: the authors bought forgetthefunnel.com at a conference in Boston, and the reason given is that a marketing funnel accounts only for a customer searching and buying, while a recurring-revenue business needs the customer to keep paying (ch. 1, PDF pp. 21–22; Figure 1.2, PDF p. 22). Treating marketing as something that ends at signup is called a serious mistake (ch. 1, PDF p. 22). The two origin stories carry the book's headline numbers: Unbounce grew revenue by nearly 900% in two years on a seed round with no paid acquisition, and Calendly went from pre-revenue to $4 million ARR in two years on the same terms (ch. 1, PDF pp. 17, 21). Forget the Funnel began in 2017 as a video workshop series, not a business, and the combined framework is said to have helped thousands of marketers, founders, and consultants (ch. 1, PDF pp. 22–23). Every chapter closes with a Your Turn recap and points at a free companion workbook the authors host (ch. 1, PDF pp. 24–25).

### Chapter 2: Building Your Customer-Led Team (PDF pp. 26–34)

The case is Tara Robertson at Sprout Social, hired to build a revenue-share program for agency customers because several other SaaS companies were running them. Surveys and interviews found agencies were not motivated by revenue share and wanted community, offer help, and introductions instead; she took the finding to leadership, ran a beta with 100 agency customers as founding members, and the resulting Agency Partner Program is credited with dropping churn and adding $2.1 million of ARR in six months (ch. 2, PDF pp. 26–27). The authors say the lift is not the point of the story, and that it would not have happened had Robertson worked alone (ch. 2, PDF p. 27).

Three reasons are given for a cross-functional team: it uses knowledge the departments already hold, it makes each department invested because contribution builds ownership, and it reduces duplicate research (ch. 2, PDF p. 28). Two objections are anticipated and answered. To the worry that research slows a team down, the answer is that a team running ineffective tactics is already wasting time (ch. 2, PDF pp. 28–29). To skepticism about cross-functional teams, the authors concede the skepticism is valid and cite a *Harvard Business Review* piece reporting that 75% of cross-functional teams fail on at least three of five criteria, then argue the failure comes from a lack of systematic approach, unclear ownership, and vague goals (ch. 2, PDF p. 29, footnote 1 on PDF p. 34). The roles are Primary Stakeholder, the founder or CEO with the decision-making power; Champion, a leader of a customer-facing department who runs the work; contributors or secondary stakeholders; and an optional facilitator, a role the authors say they often fill themselves (ch. 2, PDF p. 30). These are mapped onto RACI, with the Champion responsible and the Primary Stakeholder accountable, and the authors add that nothing falls apart if a role is unfilled (ch. 2, PDF pp. 30–31). Role worksheets giving each role's description, time investment, and actions run to PDF p. 32. Three worked team compositions are given by company size: Tiller Money at 6 employees, Unbounce at 25, and Sprout Social at 200, where leaders were reached through workshops because there were too many to involve directly (ch. 2, PDF p. 33).

### Chapter 3: Learning from Customers (PDF pp. 37–45)

The case is Autobooks, an all-in-one finance platform for small business owners distributed through banks and credit unions. Interviews run by Chris Spiek with the most engaged customers found that one feature set, online invoicing and payment processing, was what actually brought owners in, while the messaging had been presenting the whole platform at once, which the authors compare to throwing a toolbox at someone who needs a wrench (ch. 3, PDF pp. 37–38). Suellentrop led a messaging rewrite down to that single feature, with further features introduced one at a time afterward; thirty days later the rate of small business owners signing up after first learning about Autobooks rose from 14% to 23%, a 64% increase, and the number of customers accepting card payments on a sustained basis rose 300%, which matters because payment processing is how the company earns (ch. 3, PDF pp. 38–39).

Two objections are answered: that the team already did research when building the product, and that the team is its own customer (ch. 3, PDF p. 39). The counter-argument is that building a product and learning from use are different, that customers change as problems are solved and markets mature, and that old research often answered the wrong questions, being about demographics or about which features are used rather than why (ch. 3, PDF p. 39). The best customers to learn from are defined by five marks: they have personally struggled with the problem, pay without hesitation, have an ongoing rather than one-off need, have reached what the authors call value realization, and started paying recently enough to remember life before, ideally three to six months ago and up to a year for some products (ch. 3, PDF pp. 40–41). The Henry Ford faster-horse line is named as a misquote and used to make the point that customer talk is only a problem when taken at face value instead of read for the psychology under it (ch. 3, PDF p. 41).

The method is open-ended questions, on the ground that only they yield the customer's own words, where multiple-choice and true/false force the researcher's phrasing on the answer (ch. 3, PDF pp. 41–42). A two-column table pairs leading or closed questions with open-ended replacements (ch. 3, PDF p. 42). Ten interview questions are then printed in full, running from daily use back to the moment the old way stopped working, then forward through the search, the choice, the signup, and what the customer can now do (ch. 3, PDF pp. 42–43). The format rule is a threshold: with 500 or more ideal customers, start with a survey, assuming a 10–15% response rate; with fewer than 500, or with resources for only one method, choose interviews, which the authors say give energy, verbal cues, and emotion that a survey cannot (ch. 3, PDF pp. 43–44). The target sample table asks for 10–15 interviewees, or 25–50 high-quality survey responses (ch. 3, PDF p. 44).

### Chapter 4: Learning from Future Customers (PDF pp. 46–53)

The case is Paytouch, a restaurant management platform the authors began working with in February 2020 on a plan to simplify onboarding. COVID-19 shutdowns ended the market for the full platform, and earlier research had already shown that juggling delivery services was a top restaurant pain (ch. 4, PDF pp. 46–47). The team repackaged the online menu and ordering features as a smaller separate product, Dash, aimed at restaurants that had been offline; since Paytouch had no offline restaurants as customers, the idea could not be validated by customer research, so the authors studied forums, communities, and review sites instead, and built positioning, messaging, and a launch plan from that (ch. 4, PDF p. 47). Hundreds signed up, 980 restaurants became Dash customers, and multi-location Dash customers later converted to the full product (ch. 4, PDF p. 47).

Audience research is defined as learning from potential customers experiencing the problem out in the world, and is said to answer what influences them, who they trust, where they go for solutions, and what they are trying instead (ch. 4, PDF p. 48). Its limit is stated plainly: a happy customer is known to be a fit and an audience member is not, so audience findings are hypotheses to prove or disprove rather than a path (ch. 4, PDF p. 48). The difference is framed as whose voice is captured, customer research starting from a narrow band of quality and audience research from a wider band of quantity, with some junk responses treated as normal (ch. 4, PDF p. 50). Five collection methods are listed, including target market interviews, a survey to email subscribers, mining reviews of competing solutions, audience intelligence tools, and social listening (ch. 4, PDF p. 49). For a company that already has a site and traffic, the authors say a website survey is the quickest route, on the argument that money has already been spent sending people there and those people are in problem-solving mode (ch. 4, PDF p. 49).

Five website survey questions are printed with a stated purpose for each: a multiple-choice question about which description fits the visitor, to read the level of awareness; what tool they use today; what they dislike about it; what matters most in a new solution, which the authors call especially important because it decides what the site should lead with; and what is holding them back from signing up or booking a demo, which surfaces anxieties and blockers (ch. 4, PDF pp. 50–51). The worry that a survey will hurt conversion rates is acknowledged and sent to the workbook (ch. 4, PDF p. 51). The second case is Tiller Money, where the same five questions produced the visitors' pain points, their own words, and the order to arrange messages in; the homepage was rewritten using copy taken from the responses, and page value in Google Analytics rose 237% (ch. 4, PDF pp. 51–52).

### Chapter 5: Identifying Your Customers' Jobs-to-be-Done (PDF pp. 54–70)

The case is a company called Alistair, a marketing tool at $2 million ARR and 5,000 customers whose growth had flatlined despite continued marketing spend; after a few months of customer work, trial signups rose 89% and trial-to-paid conversion 40%, which the authors attribute to understanding the customers' Jobs rather than to a new channel (ch. 5, PDF p. 54). Jobs-to-be-Done is defined here in three parts, a struggle that motivated customers to seek a desired outcome, and rendered as a job statement in the form When I, Help me, So I can (ch. 5, PDF pp. 54, 56; Figure 5.1, PDF p. 56). Alistair's 5,000 customers were narrowed to its 500 healthiest and two Jobs emerged, increasing marketing presence and saving time through automation, which the authors nickname Newbies and Pros; the messaging had been selling to both, and some of the biggest investments were aimed at the Newbies (ch. 5, PDF p. 55).

The method is four steps. Step 1 sorts research answers into struggle, motivation, and desired outcome, each summarized as a theme, with the questions that surface each kind listed and worked examples tabled from Alistair's responses; two or three themes inside one quote are called normal (ch. 5, PDF pp. 57–61). Step 2 charts the struggle themes by frequency to find the most common (ch. 5, PDF p. 63; Figure 5.3). Step 3 filters to the customers who voiced the top struggle and reads their most common motivations and outcomes, producing two or three job statements (ch. 5, PDF pp. 64, 66). Step 4 picks one Job, and the authors name trying to solve every Job at once as one of the biggest mistakes they see, on the ground that job statements represent different needs and optimizing for all of them returns the team to chaos (ch. 5, PDF p. 66). Seven criteria are offered for the choice: urgency, with the observation that a painkiller sells more easily than a vitamin; willingness to pay; less hand-holding or sales burden; high retention potential, meaning a recurring rather than one-off need; expansion and upsell potential; whether those customers congregate somewhere targetable, where the authors say demographic data may usefully be layered in; and wildcards unique to the company, such as vision, an unfair advantage, or whether the Job suits a self-serve or a sales-led motion (ch. 5, PDF pp. 67–68). Alistair's own scoring is given as a criteria table, where the two Jobs tie on urgency, expansion, and congregating and the Pros take the other three, willingness to pay, minimal support burden, and retention (ch. 5, PDF p. 68). The chapter's closing claim is that the chosen Job is built on customer data and is not guesswork (ch. 5, PDF p. 69).

### Chapter 6: Deconstructing the Customer Experience (PDF pp. 73–94)

The origin story is Laudi at Airbnb's office with Lenny Rachitsky, seeing a row of illustrations taped to a wall that represented the ideal customer journey, each sheet a milestone with the customer's touchpoints, how they felt, and what value they got before moving on (ch. 6, PDF p. 73). The contrast drawn is with buyer journey maps and pirate metrics, which the authors name as AARRR and say center on business value rather than customer value (ch. 6, PDF p. 73). Laudi and two colleagues mapped their own customers' milestones and gave each a metric representing the customer getting value; two years after the map was operationalized company-wide, revenue was up 864% and the company was ranked fourteenth in the Deloitte Top 50 fastest-growing companies (ch. 6, PDF p. 74).

The argument against the generic frameworks is made in two parts: they force every customer into the same stages of awareness, consideration, and purchase, which flattens the real steps and loses the part after first purchase that a recurring-revenue business lives on; and they push teams to measure transactional moments such as signup or paid, which puts the business at the center of the story (ch. 6, PDF pp. 74–75). The illustration is a trial user who enters a card and never finds the feature they came for, whose card entry the authors call worthless (ch. 6, PDF p. 74).

The map itself has three phases, Struggle, Evaluation, and Growth, and three rows, what the customer is thinking, doing, and feeling in each (ch. 6, PDF pp. 75–76). Thinking is treated as the window into doing and feeling, and the three together are used to find where each milestone begins and ends (ch. 6, PDF p. 76). The mechanics are a whiteboard, one heading per phase, and sticky notes moved around under them (ch. 6, PDF pp. 76–77). The worked example throughout is SparkToro, an audience research tool co-founded by Rand Fishkin, whose customer Job is printed in full (ch. 6, PDF p. 77). For each phase the book prints the survey question asked, the verbatim answers, the doing and feeling the authors read out of them, and then the milestone grouping (ch. 6, PDF pp. 78–82, 86–90). The reading technique is named directly: read between the lines, and the worked instance is an answer about a client asking unanswerable questions, from which the authors infer client management, a wish to keep clients happy, and stress (ch. 6, PDF p. 79).

Milestones are defined as the leaps of faith a customer takes, and the method for finding their boundaries is to group similar feelings, because feelings shift as a customer moves from one to the next (ch. 6, PDF p. 85). Two or three per phase is called common, with no one right number (ch. 6, PDF p. 85). The named milestones are Problem and Interest in the Struggle phase; First Value, also called activation or the aha moment, and Value Realization in the Evaluation phase; and Continued Value and Value Growth in the Growth phase (ch. 6, PDF pp. 85, 87–89). The Evaluation phase is said to vary more in milestone count than the Struggle phase, with enterprise buyers bringing stakeholder layers, security requirements, and onboarding load, and small-business buyers caring more about self-evaluating, reaching value quickly, and not having to talk to sales (ch. 6, PDF p. 87). The authors quote Teresa Torres on maps relieving working memory and surfacing unintended consequences, and describe the common team reaction as noticing what has not been done and then the opportunities that follow (ch. 6, PDF p. 93).

### Chapter 7: Identifying Customer-Led KPIs (PDF pp. 95–107)

The opening case is a startup where marketing and outbound sales both owned qualified leads, and the sales manager reported marketing's leads as outbound, which created tension between the departments and inflated revenue projections; the authors say the root problem is not the manager but unclear, poorly set KPIs (ch. 7, PDF p. 95). Goldratt is quoted on measurement determining behavior, glossed as people doing whatever is in their control to minimize the risk of punishment (ch. 7, PDF p. 95). A second example is a support team measured on ticket volume or speed, where reps are incentivized toward quick boilerplate replies and a closed ticket does not mean a solved problem (ch. 7, PDF p. 96).

The argument against active-user counts is made through FeedOtter, a newsletter automation tool whose value is not having to log in; measuring engagement by daily logins would push the team to nudge customers back into a product they bought to avoid, and the value-aligned measure offered instead is newsletters consistently automated and error-free (ch. 7, PDF pp. 96–97). Lagging indicators are defined as results, usually dollar amounts such as ARR, CAC, ARPU, and lifetime value, critical to watch but not actionable because they say what happened and not why; leading indicators are actions customers take, and a two-column table sorts common metrics between them (ch. 7, PDF pp. 97–98). The stated relationship is that improving the right leading indicators improves the lagging ones (ch. 7, PDF p. 98).

Each milestone then gets one KPI. SparkToro's are built up in order: new unique website visits at the Problem milestone, chosen over total traffic because the intent is to measure awareness among new people; first search at Interest; then, at Evaluation, a mapping of what customers said was valuable to the features that created that value, tabled side by side (ch. 7, PDF pp. 98–101). Value Realization is defined as the point where everything clicks and is called the best indicator that a customer will pay, and SparkToro's threshold is ten or more searches, two or more lists, and one or more exports; First Value, a glimpse of the value to come, is set lower at five searches and one list (ch. 7, PDF pp. 101–102). Continued Value applies the Value Realization threshold on a monthly basis, and Value Growth adds an upgrade, a review, or a referral on top (ch. 7, PDF pp. 103–105). Seven possible Value Growth behaviors are listed, from upgrading a tier to teaching others (ch. 7, PDF p. 104). The chapter closes by telling the reader to build dashboards for the customer KPIs and to start benchmarking current performance, since knowing how well the gaps are bridged today is what allows prioritizing (ch. 7, PDF pp. 105–106).

### Chapter 8: Bridging Customers' Success Gaps (PDF pp. 110–116)

SparkToro's KPI table is annotated to show the drop: many people reached Problem and Interest, far fewer reached First Value (ch. 8, PDF p. 110). The authors say research had already established what those customers valued, what outcome they wanted, and which features delivered it, and that the failure was new users not finding those features (ch. 8, PDF pp. 110–111). Two changes were made, an in-app checklist pointing at the key features and an onboarding email series written from customer responses; two months later free-to-paid conversions had doubled (ch. 8, PDF p. 111). A success gap is defined as the disconnect between what ideal customers need and what the current customer experience provides (ch. 8, PDF p. 111).

The process is four steps. Step 1 is to walk the map as the ideal customer, literally doing what they do at each milestone and looking at the team's own campaigns, homepage, and pricing, with the Autobooks failure named again as the thing to look for (ch. 8, PDF pp. 111–112). Step 2 chooses one gap, by asking which is doing the most harm and which, if bridged, would raise the impact of the other opportunities; the tie-breakers offered are financial payoff, fit with existing team and budget, short-term actionability, and whether the team is excited because the path is clear, and Gary Keller's *The ONE Thing* is quoted on the one thing that makes everything else easier or unnecessary (ch. 8, PDF pp. 112–113, footnote 2 on PDF p. 116). Step 3 puts every idea on the table so long as it bridges the chosen gap, shelves ideas for other gaps, and narrows to a handful of projects deliverable in weeks or months; a table of common projects by milestone runs from ad campaigns and thought leadership at Problem through onboarding and training webinars at First Value to win-back emails, continuous education, and referral solicitation in the Growth phase (ch. 8, PDF pp. 113–114). Step 4 is execution, with recurring CX health-checks scheduled (ch. 8, PDF p. 114).

Three further client results close the chapter: CareerCake, where revised nurture copy cut the average time to book a sales call from a month to three days, reported as a 90% increase in velocity; Life Lapse, where a rewritten homepage raised visitor-to-user conversion from 9.42% to 18.19% and sales 18.9% in 50 days; and HiringBranch, which after months with no inbound leads got four qualified inbound leads from multimillion-dollar prospects within 12 days of a new site going live (ch. 8, PDF pp. 114–115).

### Chapter 9: Integrating and Iterating Customer-Led Practices (PDF pp. 117–122)

The chapter's warning is that the biggest remaining mistake would be to shelve the work (ch. 9, PDF p. 117). Presentation is treated as load-bearing: a messy map is less likely to build company-wide trust, so the authors suggest a designer or their template (ch. 9, PDF p. 117). A six-point agenda is given for sharing the map company-wide, covering the tie back to revenue, credit to contributors, the data and methods behind the decisions, the milestones and their KPIs, how the map will be used over three horizons, and an explicit invitation to questions and challenges, which the authors call a good sign (ch. 9, PDF p. 118). The claim made for the map is that it democratizes the customer's experience and serves as a source of truth for the whole organization (ch. 9, PDF p. 119).

Six ways to use it daily are listed: guiding annual and quarterly planning, where each department's plan states which Job, milestone, and KPI its work serves; adding a line to project briefs saying the same; redefining team and individual targets against the new KPIs; building accessible dashboards for top-level and nested metrics; tying research done by different teams at different times back to the map, with marketing, product, and customer success research each mapped to a milestone; and using the map in employee onboarding (ch. 9, PDF pp. 119–120). The claimed effect at Unbounce is that meetings got simpler because the team no longer had to level-set on goals, and the authors link that cohesion to the 864% figure (ch. 9, PDF p. 120). Two ways to iterate are named, returning to a Job that was identified and tabled, or restarting the whole framework for an underserved segment or a new market (ch. 9, PDF p. 121). A First Round Review article on Guru is quoted on proving customer outcomes in a quantified, unambiguous, company-wide way, and the authors take that as the framework's ultimate goal (ch. 9, PDF p. 121, footnote 3 on PDF p. 122).

### Chapter 10: What Got You Here Won't Get You There (PDF pp. 123–125)

The closing chapter restates the opening. The funnel view is said to flatten the customer's real experience and to muddy which opportunities to pursue, and the framework's demand is to see customers as whole people in a relationship (ch. 10, PDF p. 123). One addition is made to the picture: although the journey is easiest to visualize as a line, the authors suggest thinking of the relationship as a circle, because a recurring-revenue business has nearly limitless potential to keep providing and expanding value (ch. 10, PDF p. 125; Figure 10.1, PDF p. 125). The Your Turn item is to put the book into the hands of the decision makers who will champion the work (ch. 10, PDF p. 125).

### Acknowledgments and About the Authors (PDF pp. 126–129)

The acknowledgments credit Bob Moesta with believing in the project, Gail Fay and the team at Scribe with the manuscript, and a long list of early readers, and they name the people referenced in the book, including Tara Robertson, Lenny Rachitsky, Teresa Torres, Gary Keller, Chris Spiek, Rand Fishkin, and Amanda Natividad (Acknowledgments, PDF pp. 126–127). The author note describes Forget the Funnel as a consultancy helping SaaS teams reach and retain high lifetime value customers, dates Laudi's marketing career to 2000 and her advisory work to 2010, and Suellentrop's SaaS work to 2014 (About the Authors, PDF p. 129).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the authors limit their own claim, admit a counter-case, or say a rule does not apply.

- **The funnel is not simply wrong.** Chapter 10 concedes that the funnel view gives a reader a starting point, and locates the harm in what comes after, when it muddies which opportunities to pursue (ch. 10, PDF p. 123). The naming argument in chapter 1 is narrower than the title: the objection is to treating marketing as something that stops at signup in a recurring-revenue business (ch. 1, PDF p. 22).
- **The reader's tactics are not the problem.** The authors say explicitly that the reader's ideas are not bad or wrong and that the company does need to invest in marketing; the fault named is guessing (ch. 1, PDF p. 18).
- **The team is adaptable.** Having defined the four roles, the authors say nothing falls apart if a role has no one in it, and to go with the people available (ch. 2, PDF p. 31); the three role worksheets that follow cover the Champion, the Primary Stakeholder, and the contributors, and the facilitator gets none (ch. 2, PDF pp. 31–32). The facilitator role is optional throughout (ch. 2, PDF p. 30). Sprout Social at 200 employees could not involve its leaders directly and ran workshops instead (ch. 2, PDF p. 33).
- **Cross-functional teams do fail.** The skepticism is called valid and the 75% figure is printed rather than argued away; the limiting condition offered is that the failures happen where a team lacks a systematic approach, clear ownership, and specific goals (ch. 2, PDF p. 29).
- **Old research is not worthless, it is aimed elsewhere.** The objection answered is not that prior research was bad but that demographic and product-usage data answer different questions than this process asks (ch. 3, PDF p. 39).
- **Customer talk is unreliable when taken literally.** The faster-horse misquote is granted its force: the authors agree customers are not product experts, and confine the method to reading the psychology under the answer (ch. 3, PDF p. 41).
- **Research method depends on pool size, and interviews are the fallback.** Surveys are for 500 or more ideal customers and are said not to give nuance, humanize, or describe; interviews are named the choice below 500 and also the choice for a team with resources for only one method (ch. 3, PDF pp. 43–44).
- **Audience research is weaker evidence than customer research, and the authors say so.** Not everyone in an audience is an ideal customer, so the output is hypotheses to prove or disprove rather than a path to take, and junk responses are called normal (ch. 4, PDF pp. 48, 50). Website survey respondents carry the same caveat: nobody can know who is on the site (ch. 4, PDF p. 49).
- **Audience research is the fallback, not the method.** It is offered for a new product, a sudden pivot, or a company that does not yet have enough of the pry-it-from-my-hands customers chapter 3 defines (ch. 4, PDF p. 48). Forums and review sites were the only path for Dash, and the authors call those options labor intensive and in need of practice (ch. 4, PDF p. 49).
- **One Job at a time, with the others explicitly deferred rather than abandoned.** After a first Job shows wins, attention shifts to the others, and chapter 9 makes returning to a tabled Job one of the two named ways to iterate (ch. 5, PDF pp. 66–67; ch. 9, PDF p. 121).
- **The choice of Job is not forced by the criteria.** The seven factors are offered in case the choice is not already obvious, and the last is a wildcard covering vision, an unfair advantage, or the shape of the team, which can push the choice against the other six (ch. 5, PDF pp. 67–68).
- **Demographics come back in one place.** Having ruled demographic data out as an answer to what is in customers' heads, the authors say it may usefully be layered into the congregate-and-target criterion (ch. 3, PDF p. 39; ch. 5, PDF p. 67).
- **Milestone counts have no right answer.** Two or three per phase is called common, and the number is said to depend on the product and how the team operates; the Evaluation phase is said to vary more than the others, by customer complexity, with enterprise and small-business buyers given opposite profiles (ch. 6, PDF pp. 85, 87).
- **Phases overlap, and that is expected.** One research question can feed more than one phase, which the authors call totally okay and something a reader gets better at spotting (ch. 6, PDF p. 80).
- **Lagging indicators are not dismissed.** ARR, CAC, ARPU, and lifetime value are called critical to keep an eye on; the limit claimed is that they are not actionable (ch. 7, PDF p. 97).
- **The active-user critique is scoped.** The authors say the metric fails to track value for many products, and the counter-case they build it on is a product whose value is not logging in (ch. 7, PDF pp. 96–97).
- **Value Realization is rarely immediate.** The authors say it is rare, if ever, that a customer goes from first landing in a product to certainty, which is why the lower First Value milestone exists (ch. 7, PDF pp. 101–102).
- **The first map will not be good.** Laudi's own first map is described as imperfect but working, and chapter 9 assumes the initial version will look messy without a designer (ch. 6, PDF p. 74; ch. 9, PDF p. 117).
- **Accountability is outside the framework.** After saying the framework gives a team its approach and specificity, the authors hand keeping the team accountable back to the reader (ch. 2, PDF pp. 29–30). Chapter 2 likewise says the Primary Stakeholder has to protect the process and that nothing happens without them (ch. 2, PDF p. 34).
- **Much of the how is not in the book.** Survey best practices, how to run a survey without hurting conversion, interview scripts, the database template for parsing themes, guides to crafting job statements, the audience research tool list, and the CX map template are all sent to the companion workbook rather than printed, and the authors say parsing data and writing job statements take practice (ch. 3, PDF pp. 43, 44; ch. 4, PDF pp. 49, 51; ch. 5, PDF p. 66; ch. 9, PDF p. 117).

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Every case is the authors' own, reported before-and-after with no control.** Sprout Social, Autobooks, Paytouch, Alistair, Unbounce, SparkToro, Tiller Money, CareerCake, Life Lapse, and HiringBranch are all clients or employers of one of the two authors, and each result is a single change followed by a single number. Nothing in the book names what else was happening at the same time. The clearest instance is Paytouch, where Dash's 980 restaurants are credited to audience research during the months of 2020 when restaurants had no alternative to online ordering, a confound the chapter itself describes and then does not weigh (ch. 4, PDF p. 47). Chapter 9 goes further and attributes the 864% figure to team cohesion at scale (ch. 9, PDF p. 120), where chapter 6 had attributed it to the map (ch. 6, PDF p. 74).
- **The chapter 8 table is a funnel with the drop-off written in.** The annotated KPI table reads lots of people getting here, and here, but way fewer getting here (ch. 8, PDF p. 110), and the diagnostic in step 2 is to look for a high volume of customers reaching one milestone and few reaching the next (ch. 8, PDF p. 112). That is stage-to-stage conversion, the thing the title tells the reader to forget. The book's actual objection, stated in chapter 10, is narrower than the title: the funnel flattens the customer's real steps and stops at the purchase (ch. 10, PDF p. 123).
- **The book's Jobs-to-be-Done is Moesta's, not Ulwick's, and the difference is never named.** The definition given is a struggle that motivated a customer to seek a desired outcome, expressed as When I, Help me, So I can, and derived from retrospective interviews with recent buyers (ch. 5, PDF pp. 54, 56). Moesta writes the foreword and the framework is presented as Jobs-to-be-Done without qualification (Foreword, PDF p. 11). A reader who meets the name elsewhere will meet a different method under it.
- **Figures stated as rules with no source.** The 500-customer threshold between surveys and interviews, the 10–15% expected survey response rate, and the sample targets of 10–15 interviewees or 25–50 high-quality survey responses (ch. 3, PDF pp. 43–44) carry no citation and no reasoning. So do the three-to-six-month recency window for a best customer (ch. 3, PDF p. 41) and the two-or-three milestones per phase (ch. 6, PDF p. 85). The one figure that is sourced, the 75% cross-functional-team failure rate, is used to raise an objection the authors then set aside on the ground that those teams lacked a systematic approach, which the study is not cited as having measured (ch. 2, PDF p. 29).
- **The method's hardest step is the least specified.** Turning verbatim answers into themes, and themes into a job statement, is the point where the analysis is actually made, and the book's instruction for it is to read between the lines, with the worked instance inferring stress and frustration from one sentence about a client's questions (ch. 6, PDF p. 79). The book says of both parsing and job statements that they take practice, and sends the reader to the workbook for the guides (ch. 5, PDF p. 66); audience research gets the same caution (ch. 4, PDF p. 49). Every inference in the SparkToro example is the authors', and no check is offered for whether a different reader would extract the same themes from the same responses.
- **The audience-research caveat is stated once and then dropped.** Chapter 4 is explicit that audience findings are hypotheses to prove or disprove rather than a path to take (ch. 4, PDF p. 48). The Tiller Money case in the same chapter has a homepage rewritten straight from website-survey responses and reports the result as a win, with no step in between where the hypothesis was tested (ch. 4, PDF pp. 51–52).
- **Every chapter routes to the product the authors sell.** The survey best practices, the interview scripts, the parsing database, the job-statement guides, the audience-tool list, the CX map template, and the kickoff materials are all outside the book (ch. 2, PDF p. 33; ch. 3, PDF pp. 43–44; ch. 4, PDF p. 49; ch. 5, PDF p. 66; ch. 9, PDF p. 117). The workbook is free, and the chapter 10 call to action is to hand the book to decision makers (ch. 10, PDF p. 125), so what the reader can act on without contact is a diagnosis and a vocabulary rather than a procedure.
- **The organizational assumptions are large and unexamined.** The framework as written needs a Primary Stakeholder, a Champion, contributors from several departments, an optional facilitator, a company-wide all-hands to share the map, department-level quarterly plans, dashboards, and an employee onboarding program (ch. 2, PDF pp. 30–31; ch. 9, PDF pp. 118–120). The smallest worked team is six employees (ch. 2, PDF p. 33). The authors say to adapt and go with who you have (ch. 2, PDF p. 31), and that is the whole of the guidance for anything smaller.
- **Two claims have already aged.** The SparkToro KPIs are written in searches, lists, and exports, and the product now sells reports (ch. 7, PDF pp. 101–105; section 7 above). The Autobooks messaging the book presents as the fix has been replaced by the all-in-one framing it was the fix for (ch. 3, PDF pp. 38–39; section 7 above). Both are the kind of example the book's own argument predicts will move, since it says customers change as problems are solved and markets mature (ch. 3, PDF p. 39).
