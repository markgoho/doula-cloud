## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that the greatest risk in a startup lies not in building the product but in finding customers and a market, so the greatest cause of failure is that startups "lack customers" and a proven financial model rather than a product (Chapter 1, p. 34). Every company uses some form of a Product Development model, which the author dates to early twentieth-century manufacturing, and it fits a new product entering a known market with known customers; startups then borrow it as the roadmap for finding customers, timing a launch and setting a revenue plan, which it was never built to do (Chapter 1, pp. 25–26). In its place the author proposes a second process that runs in parallel with Product Development and has four steps: Customer Discovery, Customer Validation, Customer Creation, and Company Building (Chapter 2, p. 74). Each step is drawn as a circle with recursive arrows, because "finding the right customers" is unpredictable and the model assumes several iterations of each step before it is right (Chapter 2, pp. 75–76). The second organizing idea is Market Type: a startup is entering an existing market, creating a new market, resegmenting an existing market as a low-cost entrant, or resegmenting it as a niche entrant, and the author's claim is that Market Type "changes everything" a company does, including customer adoption rate, positioning, market size, launch, cash needs, and the shape of the sales growth curve (Chapter 2, pp. 87, 90). The evidence offered is the author's own eight startups over twenty-one years, his board and consulting work for two venture firms (Preface, pp. 13–15; Introduction, p. 18), and a set of named company cases: Webvan, Furniture.com and Living.com against Design Within Reach, FastOffice, InLook, PhotosToYou, Palm against Handspring, and a disguised company called BetaSheet (Chapter 1, p. 23; Chapter 2, p. 70; Chapter 3, p. 105; Chapter 4, p. 235; Chapter 5, p. 339; Chapter 6, p. 433).

The author says what the claim is not. Customer Development is not a replacement for Product Development but "a companion to it" (Chapter 2, p. 75). He states deliberately that he is not calling it a sales process or a marketing process, because early on the company is neither selling nor marketing (Chapter 2, p. 65). The purpose of Customer Discovery is not to collect feature lists from prospects and not to run focus groups; the first product specification comes from the founders' vision, and the Customer Development team's job is to find out whether a market exists for that vision (Chapter 3, pp. 79–80, 111). It is not a chasm-crossing book: the author argues the Technology Life Cycle Adoption Curve is real but that an early-stage startup's problems "occur much earlier" than any chasm, and that chasm activities are a sign of success one should be lucky to face (Chapter 1, p. 62). Alpha and beta tests belong to engineering and are not Customer Development activities at all, because they validate the product technically rather than the market (Chapter 4, pp. 299–301). Business development is not a new word for sales (Chapter 6, p. 505). The Customer Creation chapter explicitly declines to cover ordinary marketing-communications tactics such as adwords, advertising and public relations, offering strategy in their place (Chapter 5, p. 369). Appendix B's worksheets are an enterprise-software example and the author says a reader's own process "will differ" by Market Type and company (Appendix B, p. 601). Finally, the author says this 2013 edition is substantively the 2003 text with typos fixed, and that the update to it is *The Startup Owner's Manual*, written with Bob Dorf in 2012, while this book "remains the uber text" (Introduction, pp. 20–21).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Preface: The Hero's Journey (pp. 11–16)

The preface borrows Joseph Campbell's archetypal journey and argues that startups, like myths, have one outline however different the details, so the path of a startup is "well worn" and well understood, and the only secret is that nobody has written it down (Preface, pp. 12–13). The author dates his own recognition of the pattern to consulting for the two venture firms that had put $12 million into his last failed startup, plus two board seats, which gave him what he calls an out-of-body view of other people's startups (Preface, pp. 13–14). The observation that drives the book is that venture capitalists develop pattern recognition for a recurring set of operating problems, which suggested to him the problems were structural rather than endemic, and that the way startups are organized and managed is itself the cause (Preface, pp. 14–15). His conclusion after his eighth startup, E.piphany, is that successful startups all invent a parallel process of customer learning and discovery, knowingly or not (Preface, p. 16).

### Introduction (pp. 18–21)

The introduction is new to the 2013 edition and is the author's account of what happened after 2003. He writes that investors and educators taught founders to use the process of an established company — write a plan, raise money, execute to the plan in a straight line — and that his experience said they were wrong (Introduction, p. 18). The framing he now puts on it is that tools existed for executing a business model but none existed for searching for one, and searching is what startups do (Introduction, p. 18). He credits adoption to necessity rather than to investors or educators: web, mobile and cloud teams already using agile development needed faster customer feedback (Introduction, p. 19). He names the lineage directly — Eric Ries, a student in his first Berkeley class, became the first practitioner at IMVU and coupled customer development to agile engineering, and Alexander Osterwalder's business model canvas supplied a front end that organizes a startup's hypotheses (Introduction, pp. 19–20). He says these ideas became the Lean Startup movement, that it is taught in most major universities, and that it is the standard for commercializing scientific research in the United States (Introduction, p. 20).

### Chapter 1: The Path to Disaster: The Product Development Model (pp. 23–67)

The chapter runs the Product Development model stage by stage — concept and seed, product development, alpha/beta test, product launch and first customer ship — in general and then through Webvan, which raised over $800 million, launched in June 1999, filed to go public sixty days later, and was bankrupt about twenty-four months after the offering (Chapter 1, pp. 23–33). It then lists ten flaws. First, the model ignores where the customers are (p. 34). Second, focusing on the first customer ship date produces what the author calls a fire, ready, aim strategy, because that date only means Product Development thinks it is finished (pp. 35–36). Third, the model emphasizes execution where a startup needs learning and discovery, which happens as recursive circles rather than a linear flow (pp. 38–40). Fourth, sales and marketing before first customer ship have no meaningful milestones, and the author proposes a different set of questions in their place: whether customers' problems are understood, what they will pay, whether visionary customers exist, and whether the sales roadmap is understood well enough to sell consistently (pp. 41–43). Fifth and sixth, using a Product Development schedule to time sales and marketing readiness means neither strategy is tested until after first customer ship (pp. 44–48). Seventh, all this drives premature scaling; Webvan spent $18 million on software and $40 million on its first automated warehouse before shipping anything (pp. 49–50). Eighth is the death spiral, told as a ritual in which the VP of Sales goes first, the VP of Marketing second, and the founding CEO third, which the author says happens in more than half of startups with first-time CEOs (pp. 50–55). Ninth, not all startups are alike, and the four Market Types have different adoption rates and different cash needs — a new market may be unprofitable for five or more years while an existing market may generate cash in twelve to eighteen months (pp. 55–57). Tenth, the model produces three unrealistic expectations about what it can guide, about schedule, and about uniform adoption (pp. 57–58). The chapter closes by examining the Technology Life Cycle Adoption Curve of Everett Rogers and Geoffrey Moore, granting that it identifies real customer types but arguing it leads early-stage founders to four bad conclusions — dreaming of the mainstream too soon, treating technology enthusiasts as early customers when "they very rarely buy", believing adoption is a smooth curve rather than a step function, and emphasizing execution (pp. 59–63). Its counter-example is Tesco, which by 2002 was handling 85,000 online grocery orders a week and more than $559 million in sales, built off 929 existing stores rather than a war chest (pp. 66–67).

### Chapter 2: The Path to Epiphany: The Customer Development Model (pp. 69–102)

The chapter opens with Design Within Reach, whose founder Rob Forbes built a catalog business on the observation that designer furniture took four months to ship, kept tuning the catalog against each catalog's sales results, was turned down by venture capitalists who wanted an e-commerce company, and reached $180 million as a public company while Furniture.com failed (Chapter 2, pp. 69–73). The four steps are then defined. Customer Discovery finds out who the customers are and whether the problem is important to them, working from the founders' product as specified (pp. 79–80). Customer Validation builds a repeatable sales roadmap and proves it with orders, and the author calls it the checkpoint: only if repeatable customers, a repeatable sales process and a profitable business model are all found does the company proceed (pp. 82–83). Customer Creation creates end-user demand and drives it into the channel, and is placed after Validation deliberately so that heavy marketing spending comes after the first customers (p. 83). Company Building turns the Customer Development team into formal departments (p. 85). The counter-example throughout is Furniture.com, which spent $7 million on its website before knowing demand, spent $34 million on marketing against $10.9 million of revenue, and had 209 employees at $10 million of sales; Living.com agreed to pay Amazon $145 million over four years for home-page placement (pp. 80, 84, 85). The chapter's second half defines the four Market Types and argues Market Type changes everything, using Palm in 1996 (a new market, where no customer had heard of a personal digital assistant) against Handspring in 1999 (an existing market, $170 million of revenue in twelve months) with essentially identical products and team (pp. 87–90). An existing market means higher performance against known competitors who define the basis of competition; a new market means a large customer base that could not do this before, where product features are at first irrelevant and the problem is convincing customers the vision is not a hallucination; low-cost resegmentation asks whether customers at the low end will buy "good enough" performance for substantially less; niche resegmentation asks whether part of a market will buy a product built for their specific needs, even at a higher price, and the example is In-n-Out Burger against a McDonald's menu of more than fifty-five items by 2001 (pp. 90–95). The author notes over half of startups attempt resegmentation and calls it the trickiest choice (p. 95). The chapter ends on synchronization: in a big company the product spec is market-driven and in a startup the marketing is product-driven, so the two teams hold formal synchronization meetings at each step and Customer Development does not advance without agreement (pp. 98–101).

### Chapter 3: Customer Discovery (pp. 105–231)

The longest chapter opens with FastOffice, whose $1,395 home-office device had no compelling need behind it at that price, went through two failed repositionings and lost its founder as CEO before the company found its real asset in a voice-over-data communications chip (Chapter 3, pp. 105–108, 121–122). Customer Discovery is defined as turning the founders' hypotheses about business model, market and customers into facts, on the premise that a startup on day one is "a faith-based enterprise" and the facts live outside the building (p. 110). The philosophy section lists what a marketer is not going to do — understand all customers, collect every wanted feature, hand engineering a requirements document, run focus groups — and says the product is developed "for the few, not the many" (pp. 111–113). It introduces the book's coined term, earlyvangelists: visionary customers who will buy an unfinished product, identified by five characteristics on a scale of pain — the customer has a problem, knows it, is actively searching with a timetable, has cobbled together an interim solution, and has or can get budget — with the author's contention that earlyvangelists are found only at points four and five (pp. 113–118). Development starts from the founders' vision, feature requests are by exception rather than by rule, and the first release is meant to be good enough only for the first paying customers (pp. 118–121). The process then runs through five phases. Phase 0 is board and team buy-in on the process, on Market Type, on funding for two to three passes through Discovery and Validation, on Product Development spending at least fifteen percent of its time outside the building, and on a mission statement and three to five core values (pp. 128–131). Phase 1 writes six one- or two-page hypothesis briefs: product (features, benefits, intellectual property, dependency analysis, delivery schedule, total cost of ownership), customer and problem (customer types, problems, a day in the life, organization and influence maps, ROI justification, and the minimum feature set), channel and pricing, demand creation, Market Type, and competition (pp. 131–179). The customer-type taxonomy is end users, influencers, recommenders, the economic buyer, the decision-maker, and saboteurs, and the author insists the same hierarchy applies to consumer sales with more familiar titles (pp. 140–145). The problem brief grades each customer type on a recognition scale of latent need, active need, or a vision of the solution (pp. 148–149). The pricing brief asks what customers pay today for something like it, considers lifetime value rather than a single sale, and offers the author's two questions: whether they would deploy it if it were free, and then whether they would pay a deliberately absurd price, which he says gets customers to name their own number more than half the time (pp. 161–163). The Market Type brief treats the choice as a late-binding decision that can be deferred to Customer Creation but still needs a working hypothesis, and supplies question sets and two diagrams, a competitive diagram and a market map (pp. 166–176). Phase 2 goes outside the building: a list of fifty potential customers and a separate innovators list, referrals rather than cold email, ten calls a day until three visits a day are booked, with fifty follow-up calls yielding five to ten visits, and a three-column problem presentation — problems, today's solutions, your solution — used to elicit rather than persuade, with the instruction not to convince customers they have the problem described (pp. 180–192). The example is RoboVac, whose most excited prospects turned out to be single men who barely knew where their vacuum was rather than people who vacuum regularly (pp. 190–191). Phase 2 also covers in-depth customer understanding and market knowledge — adjacent companies, analysts, press, two conferences at minimum — with the author's own story of studying petroleum geology until Chevron's research center offered him a job (pp. 193–200). Phase 3 tests the product concept: a first company reality check in which the team asks whether it has product/market fit and applies the rule that no features are added until the search of market space is exhausted, a solution-oriented product presentation of five key features and eighteen months of roadmap, more customer visits at the titles that will actually decide, a second reality check that sorts customer reactions into four categories, and the first informal advisory board approaches (pp. 201–223). The four categories run from unequivocal love to no perceived need, and the author calls category two, more features, the most dangerous, since knowing which features do not matter is as important as knowing which do (pp. 217–219). He distinguishes fast to market from first to market, and names "technology packaging" — selling the same core technology as modules or a subscription rather than one monolithic package — as the real problem often mislabeled positioning (pp. 219–221). Phase 4 verifies the problem, the product, and the business model, and produces a problem statement, an expanded product requirement document, an updated sales and revenue plan, and a business and product plan, before deciding to iterate or exit (pp. 223–230).

### Chapter 4: Customer Validation (pp. 235–335)

The chapter opens with InLook, whose CEO discovered that an eleven-person sales and marketing organization had produced a forecast in which none of the top accounts would deploy the product even if it were free, and who then fired the VP of Sales and seven staff and went into the field himself with six months of runway (Chapter 4, pp. 235–243). Customer Validation's goal is a proven sales roadmap, not revenue, and the author states plainly that the company cares less about revenue at this point than about a repeatable process and business model (p. 245). The distinction he draws is between a sales pipeline, which every mature sales force has, and a sales roadmap, which answers who influences, recommends, decides and pays, where the budget is, how many calls a sale takes, and what the profile of the optimal visionary buyer is; a pipeline cannot be built without a roadmap first (pp. 247–248). The author has founders and the CEO in front of customers through at least the first iteration, because earlyvangelists want to hear the founders rather than a salesperson (pp. 249–251). The chapter's four phases follow. Phase 1 gets ready to sell: a value proposition reduced to one sentence, tested for emotional compulsion, economic case and credibility, and distinguished as incremental in an existing market or transformational in a new one, with InLook's example being a phrase about managing profitability aimed at chief financial officers (pp. 255–260). Then a collateral plan naming which piece is used with whom and when, covering websites, sales presentations, demos, data sheets, ROI white papers, price lists, contracts and a billing system, with the guidance to spend nothing on flashy design at this stage and the author's own E.piphany failure of pitching a value proposition that told a chief information officer his organization was unnecessary (pp. 260–267). Then a distribution channel plan in three parts — a channel food chain with a written description of each tier, channel discounts and financials, and channel management — worked through a book-publishing example in which a $20 book nets the publisher $7, and warning against recording a sale into the channel as revenue and against stuffing the channel (pp. 268–276). Then a preliminary sales roadmap in four parts: organization and influence maps, a customer access map, a sales strategy, and an implementation plan listing everything that must still happen between agreement and payment. The worked case is E.piphany's discovery that four constituencies had to be sold in a fixed order, operational executives first and corporate IT staff last, because information technology could veto a deal (pp. 276–287). Then hiring a sales closer, explicitly not a VP of Sales, and aligning executives on the engineering schedule, a slip factor computed from past milestones, a good-enough first release, fact-checked sales collateral, and ten percent of Product Development's time spent in the field on sales, installation and support (pp. 287–294). Then formalizing technical, customer, industry and business advisory boards, with rules of thumb about how many of each to keep (pp. 294–298). Phase 2 sells to visionary customers: expect ninety-five percent to say no and one in three to one in five of the rest to close, sell three to five customers before shipping, sell the standard product rather than custom extensions, ask for near list price while staying flexible on terms, and keep win/loss statistics, because understanding why a customer said no matters more here than why one said yes (pp. 302–311). The chapter insists a giveaway proves nothing, that alpha and beta testing belong to engineering, and that the word customer should be reserved for people who pay (pp. 299–301). It also distinguishes four customer groups — early evaluators, earlyvangelists, scalable customers, and mainstream customers — and warns against confusing corporate evaluation labs with earlyvangelists (pp. 252, 303–305). Channel partners are validated by an actual order, with the caution that a channel partner is not a customer and creates no demand (pp. 312–313). Phase 3 develops positioning for product and company, by Market Type, then presents it to industry analysts and influencers, with instructions to do the homework first and to treat the meeting as influence rather than a sale (pp. 314–324). Phase 4 verifies the product solution, the sales roadmap, the channel plan and the business model, and the author names the most important exit criterion as whether the sales closer believes other salespeople can sell the product as specified, repeatably; he also notes that no objection to price at all may mean the price is too low (pp. 325–333).

### Chapter 5: Customer Creation (pp. 339–429)

The chapter opens with PhotosToYou, a digital-photo printing service whose new executives believed branding was the difference between winning and losing, ran a national advertising campaign into a market whose size was bounded by digital camera ownership in 1999, burned its venture money in two years, and was refinanced at one-tenth of its previous valuation with the entire executive staff replaced (Chapter 5, pp. 339–348). The author's advice at the time, which the board and marketers rejected, was that in a new market the first year's goal is market adoption rather than market share, and that demand creation should be low-cost education rather than customer acquisition (pp. 346–347). The chapter then names its four building blocks — year-one objectives, positioning, launch, and demand creation — and its central claim that there are three of each, matched to Market Type, rather than one (pp. 360–362). Timing is explicit: no serious marketing spending until a proven and repeatable sales roadmap exists, which is why Customer Creation is a discrete step (p. 363). Phase 1 picks the Market Type with a customer questionnaire and sets first-year objectives. Its analytical tool is the New Lanchester Strategy, from which the author draws share thresholds — 74 percent is an effective monopoly, a combined 74 percent with the leader within 1.7 times the second is a duopoly, 41 percent with 1.7 times the next is a market leader, 26 percent makes a market unstable, and below 26 percent the market is easiest to enter — plus cost-of-entry ratios of three times a monopolist's sales and marketing budget or 1.7 times a competitor's in a crowded market (pp. 352–357). He adds his own rule that a startup creating a new market will not reach profitable size for three to seven years, derived from looking at hundreds of high-tech startups (p. 359). Year-one objectives then differ: market share in an existing market, computed from a serviceable available market and a backwards-worked funnel; market adoption in a new market, where the success criterion is that potential customers have gone from zero to meaningful; and both at once when resegmenting (pp. 380–385). Phase 2 positions company and product, hiring a public relations agency for strategy rather than press tactics, running internal and external audits with the founding team making the first five calls itself, and matching positioning to Market Type: difference and credibility in an existing market, a vision of what could be in a new market, and the value of a chosen segment when resegmenting, illustrated by Handspring, Palm, Jet Blue and Wal-Mart (pp. 385–398). Phase 3 launches, and the author names three launch types: onslaught for an existing market, early adopter for a new market, and niche for a resegmented one, with onslaught described as maximum exposure at a single point in time and the early adopter launch as a long education campaign aimed at tipping-point diffusion (pp. 399–404). The launch audience must be a small group carrying the most influence rather than everyone on the influence map, and the messengers are of three kinds — experts, evangelists and connectors — drawn from Malcolm Gladwell's *The Tipping Point* (pp. 404–412). Messages must be memorable and sticky, and the author's examples are a pesticide named Malathion sprayed over Santa Clara County in 1981 and a hypothetical literal description of a hamburger, plus the observation that a new market usually gets its own three-letter acronym (pp. 412–415). Message context matters as much as the message, since the same words raised $50 million during the bubble and would not get a call returned afterward (pp. 415–417). Media are treated as paid messengers, with the note that unpaid messengers read as more credible and that paid media are usually overkill at a business-to-business launch (pp. 417–418). Success is measured by re-running the pre-launch audits after launch and by a press audit checking whether the company's own framing was picked up; the author's own trade-show measurement found more than 78 percent of booth visitors who had not heard of the company could later describe it correctly (pp. 418–420, 426–427). Phase 4 creates demand, with the rule that demand-creation goals must be the same as the sales goals, and a worked example in which $3 million at a $500 average selling price means 6,000 units, worked backwards into qualified and raw leads and a cost per lead and per customer (pp. 421–426). The chapter also devotes a section to the fallacy of first-mover advantage, citing the 1988 Montgomery and Lieberman paper, the authors' 1998 retrospective backing off it, and Golder and Tellis's 1993 study of 500 brands in 50 categories, in which almost half of market pioneers failed and early market leaders — entering an average of thirteen years later — failed at 8 percent (pp. 377–379).

### Chapter 6: Company Building (pp. 433–546)

The chapter is told through BetaSheet, a drug-discovery startup whose founding CEO sold the first three large accounts himself, could not delegate, rewrote his own company's data sheets to push the next product, and was removed after the executive staff threatened to resign; the professional CEO who replaced him rebuilt the team from large-company executives, grew revenue for eighteen months, then lost the innovative staff, and the company closed on its fifth anniversary (Chapter 6, pp. 433–442, 451–453). The author's argument is that both the founder and the investors were wrong: long-term success needs founder continuity past the point conventional wisdom replaces them, and Ford, Microsoft, Nike, Polaroid, Oracle, Amazon and Apple are offered as the counter-examples (pp. 443–444). Company Building requires three actions — build a mainstream customer base beyond earlyvangelists, build organization and management for scale, and create fast-response departments — and the chapter's four phases follow them (pp. 445–446). Phase 1 reaches mainstream customers, and the framing is that Moore's chasm is real but its width and timing depend on Market Type. In a new market the gap is widest, producing the hockey-stick curve and two risks: a sales force content with a low level of repeatable earlyvangelist business, and pragmatists who never adopt at all; a third risk is a fast-follower who piggybacks on the market education (pp. 463–466). In an existing market the chasm is small or absent because visionaries and pragmatists are the same customers, and the author's distinction there is that positioning is the fast and cheap differentiator while branding is not, with Starbucks used to illustrate both (pp. 472–474). In a resegmented market the position is in between, and branding and positioning are the appropriate tools, with Subzero, Miele, Bosch, Starbucks, Dell, Perrier and Calistoga as the examples; the risk is being seduced by a low volume of sales skimmed from a competitive market, and the cost of resegmenting is usually underestimated (pp. 476–482). Each Market Type is then attached to a set of scaling questions about capital requirements, burn rate, market education and hiring plan (pp. 469–470, 475–476, 481). Phase 2 has the board review the CEO and executive staff against a table of characteristics by company stage, and the author frames the choice as a third path between startup chaos and corporate rigidity: a mission-centric organization (pp. 450–455, 483–490). The mission statement he wants here is explicitly internal, action-oriented, and names revenue and profit; the worked example is CafePress, with per-store revenue, margin, revenue and customer targets written into it (pp. 491–497). Phase 3 turns the Customer Development team into functional departments, writing a departmental mission statement before any hiring, because departments rationalize their own existence once they exist; departmental roles then differ by Market Type, with Sales, Marketing and Business Development each getting a different objective in an existing, new and resegmented market (pp. 498–512). Business Development's real function throughout is to assemble the "whole product", a concept the author attributes to Bill Davidow, because mainstream customers will not assemble piece parts from startups (pp. 505–506). Phase 4 builds fast-response departments on two principles, decentralized decision-making and John Boyd's OODA loop, with a checklist of questions under Observe, Orient, Decide and Act (pp. 512–516). Mission-centric management has five parts: mission intention, which he says matters more than the task because it survives a change in circumstances; employee initiative; mutual trust and communication, in which bad news must travel faster than good; good-enough decision-making, quoting Patton on a good plan violently executed now; and mission synchronization through peer-wise cross-departmental meetings rather than top-down orders and bottom-up status (pp. 519–530). The information section asks for three views — first-hand knowledge from executives in the field at least quarterly, a synthesized bird's-eye view from a market intelligence function in each department, and a role-played view through customers' and competitors' eyes — and makes hoarding information or suppressing bad news a firing offense (pp. 531–536). The leadership section uses Sun Microsystems, one of nine licensees of Andy Bechtolsheim's Stanford design and the only survivor, and Southwest Airlines, whose twenty-five-minute turnarounds and 24 percent lower seat cost the author attributes to a leadership culture rather than better planes or routes (pp. 516–518, 536–541).

### Bibliography (pp. 549–581)

The bibliography is thirty-three pages, organized by theme with a paragraph of the author's own commentary before each list. The opening section, "Entrepreneurial Management Stack", names the four books he says a founder needs beside this one: Osterwalder's *Business Model Generation*, Ries's *The Lean Startup*, Kent Beck's *Extreme Programming Explained*, and, for medical devices, *Biodesign*, with *The Startup Owner's Manual* and its workbook listed alongside (Bibliography, pp. 549–551). He credits Christensen's *Innovator's Dilemma* and *Innovator's Solution* with helping him refine the four Market Types, Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* with starting his search for the patterns that precede the chasm, and Gladwell's *The Tipping Point* with the new-market communications strategy (pp. 551–552). Later sections cover strategy, innovation in large companies, war as strategy (where the New Lanchester Strategy is called offbeat but hard to ignore), marketing communications, sales, startup law and finance, Silicon Valley history, manufacturing, presentation and product design, human resources, business history, and the entrepreneurial university (pp. 552–581). His comment on entrepreneurship textbooks is that startups in Silicon Valley ignore them after school and that not one of them teaches the business model, customer development and agile engineering stack (p. 569).

### Appendix A: The Customer Development Team (pp. 583–599)

The appendix is the organizational argument the chapters assume. Through Customer Discovery and Validation there are no Sales, Marketing or Business Development organizations and, more importantly to the author, no executives carrying those titles, because people default to what they did before and a title tells them what that is (Appendix A, pp. 584, 594–595). He works through each function. A world-class VP of Sales brings hiring, scaling, forecasting and budgeting skills that he calls toxic in the first two steps, and reconstructs a phone call in which the sales pipeline is good but the corporate presentation changes twice a week (pp. 585–587). Marketing's demand creation and requirements documents are futile while end users, markets and channels are unknown, and he singles out the bubble-era VP of Branding title (pp. 588–590). Business development he treats hardest: in the first two stages there is no use for the function, "None. Zero.", because until validation is finished you do not know who your customers, channel or partners will be (pp. 590–591). Engineering keeps its product work but sheds the technical publications, quality assurance and phone-book specifications of a large department, and its strategy is to execute the founders' technical vision, ship fast to market rather than first, and use earlyvangelist feedback to specify the next release (pp. 593–594). In their place he puts a Customer Development Team with two roles, Customer Execution and Customer Vision, alongside a Product Execution and Vision team, led by a founder or someone with an equal vote, with up to fifty percent of the product visionary's time spent in front of customers; the test for any function, title or hire is whether it moves the company closer to knowing the customer's problem, the product's match to it, the sales roadmap, and orders that validate it (pp. 583–584, 596–598).

### Appendix B: Customer Development Checklist (pp. 601–749)

The appendix is a 149-page worksheet set for an enterprise software company, and the author says a reader's own will differ by Market Type and company (Appendix B, p. 601). Each worksheet carries the same header fields — goal of the phase, author, approval, presenter, time and effort — then a question list, then exit criteria. The Customer Discovery worksheets run from 0-a, board and management buy-in, whose exit criterion is team and board agreement on the process, Market Type and exit criteria for each step, through 0-b, the team and its three to five core values, and then the hypothesis worksheets 1-a to 1-f for product, customer, channel and pricing, demand creation, Market Type and competition, each of which converts the chapter's prose into numbered questions (pp. 604–633). Worksheet 1-a asks, among other things, what happens to the company if a dependency does not arrive and what metric would measure that change; worksheet 1-b ends with the smallest, least complicated problem the customer will pay to have solved (pp. 610–611, 616). The Customer Validation worksheets follow the same pattern for the value proposition, sales materials, the preliminary channel plan and sales roadmap, the sales closer, executive realignment, the advisory board, and then contacting visionaries, validating the sales roadmap and the channel (pp. 679–749). Time and effort are stated in days throughout, typically two to five days of authoring plus a half-day meeting per worksheet (pp. 608, 612, 617).

### Acknowledgments and author note (pp. 750–758)

The acknowledgments name three mentors — Ben Wegbreit, Gordon Bell and Allen Michaels — and three marketing influences, Bill Davidow, Regis McKenna and Geoffrey Moore, and record that Will Harvey and Eric Ries at IMVU were the first company to implement the methodology and made the book required reading for new hires; Ries also copyedited it (Acknowledgments, pp. 750–752). The author note records eight startups in twenty-one years including Zilog, MIPS Computers, Convergent Technologies, Ardent, SuperMac, ESL, Rocket Science Games and E.piphany, summarized by the author as two significant craters, one dot-com home run and several base hits, and it records the later teaching: the Lean LaunchPad at Stanford, Berkeley and Columbia, the National Science Foundation's Innovation Corps with more than 400 teams by early 2013, and a free Udacity version with more than 100,000 sign-ups (pp. 754–757).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **The Product Development model.** He calls it "a good fit" for launching a new product into an established, well-defined market where the basis of competition is understood and the customers are known, and says it "at times succeeds" for the first of the four Market Types where the market resembles past experience (Chapter 1, pp. 25, 56).
- **The adoption curve he argues against.** The Technology Life Cycle Adoption Curve "provides true insight", because there really are different customer types across a product's life; his objection is to using it as an early-stage roadmap (Chapter 1, p. 62).
- **Market Type is not fixed.** It is a late-binding decision that can be deferred to Customer Creation as long as a working hypothesis exists, and he writes that Market Type "is not immutable" and that almost any product fitting an existing market could be positioned as a niche in a resegmented one (Chapter 3, p. 166; Chapter 5, p. 351). Creating a new market may be repositioned as resegmenting an existing one, and he lists that as a question to answer in the new-market brief (Chapter 3, p. 175; Chapter 5, p. 358).
- **Speed by Market Type.** A company entering an existing market with a better version of the same product may finish the process "in a matter of weeks or months", while a company creating a new market may take a year or two or longer (Chapter 2, p. 96).
- **When a feature request is not one.** The rule is no new features until the market search is exhausted, but if enough customers ask for the same custom features, "they are not custom at all", and he says this is the time to pivot and write them into the spec (Chapter 3, p. 203; Chapter 4, p. 310). Promising unique extensions is a trap, yet he allows it is "sometimes essential" to make such promises to get an order or two (Chapter 4, p. 309).
- **Price.** The instruction is to ask near list price and be tough on discounts, while being flexible on terms — no payment until delivery, or until the product works as specified (Chapter 4, pp. 310–311).
- **"Good enough" has a floor.** He calls it "a fine line" between a good-enough minimum feature set and an unusable product customers call junk, and adds the separate caution not to distribute an incomplete release so widely that its limited feature set becomes etched in customers' minds as the finished product (Chapter 4, pp. 292, 294).
- **Earlyvangelists as references.** They are the ultimate reference accounts, with the parenthetical limit "Until you reach the Chasm", where pragmatists want references from other pragmatists (Chapter 4, pp. 252, 463).
- **Consumer products without a problem.** Clothing, fashion and entertainment do not address a problem or a need, and he notes United States consumers spend over forty percent of their income on discretionary purchases; for these, the hypothesis is about emotional wants and a justification the buyer gives themselves (Chapter 3, p. 145).
- **Competitive analysis in a new market.** Doing one is "like dividing by zero" because there are no direct competitors, so the market map stands in for the competitive brief (Chapter 3, pp. 176–177).
- **The sales closer.** A company may go around the Customer Validation loop once without one, then hire when it can see where the missing sales skill is stunting progress (Chapter 4, p. 288).
- **The New Lanchester numbers.** The author writes that he does not swear he can derive the formulas or prove the theorems, only that the results come out looking like the real world (Chapter 5, p. 353).
- **Cost of entry against a diversified competitor.** The ratio applies to the division or product line that directly opposes the product rather than the whole company, which he says makes an apparently hopeless entry calculable (Chapter 5, pp. 400–401).
- **Speed in decisions.** He excepts long-term strategic, engineering and product planning, where time is not the limiting factor and decisions should not be rushed (Chapter 6, p. 528).
- **Replacing founders.** He grants that the case for discarding founders is "often true", particularly in an existing market where the gap to the mainstream is nonexistent and execution is paramount, and that a founding CEO who chases new markets instead of reaping an existing one is an unwitting candidate for unemployment; he then says the jury is out on whether more startups fail from removing founders or from keeping them too long (Chapter 6, pp. 487–488).
- **Alpha and beta testers, and channel partners.** Testers are not customers but can be influential recommenders in the sales process (Chapter 4, p. 301). A channel order is the goal of the channel phase, yet signing a channel partner is "emphatically not the same" as a customer buying (Chapter 4, p. 313).
- **A new market may not exist.** The market itself can turn out to be a chimera, and his examples of markets that never materialized are home dry-cleaning products, low-fat snack substitutes, smart cards, the artificial intelligence market of the early 1980s and pen computing in the early 1990s (Chapter 6, pp. 470–471).
- **His own method is not the most rigorous.** In the bibliography he says Eric Von Hippel's four-step lead-user process is "more rigorous and disciplined" than the approach suggested in this book (Bibliography, p. 556). Appendix B carries the same limit for its worksheets: the reader's process "will differ" by Market Type and company (Appendix B, p. 601).

## 7. What has changed since publication

The book's second edition is dated 2013 and its text is the 2003 text, so most of its examples were already a decade old when it was reissued. Each figure, company or tool it leans on is checked below against a later source, in September 2026. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure.

- **Webvan: over $800 million raised, bankrupt about twenty-four months after the IPO (Chapter 1, pp. 24, 33, 55).** Confirmed in direction, slightly different in composition: Wikipedia's account gives over $396 million from venture investors plus $375 million from the November 1999 offering, about $771 million in total, with the bankruptcy filing in mid-2001 ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webvan)). The book's $800 million is a round-up of the same money, and the chronology holds.
- **Tesco raced past Webvan to become the largest online grocer, with 85,000 orders a week and more than $559 million in sales by 2002, and bought 35% of Safeway's online grocery service in June 2001 (Chapter 1, pp. 66–67).** The first half held and the second half ended: Tesco.com launched in April 2000 and its online sales reached £1.2 billion by the year ending February 2007, but Tesco sold its GroceryWorks stake back to Safeway in 2006, so the United States re-entry the book presents as the happy ending was unwound ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesco.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesco.com)). The book's June 2001 date for the investment is unchecked as to its month: the source read gives only the year, and no first-party filing was read.
- **Design Within Reach reached $180 million as a public company (Chapter 2, p. 73).** The company survived past the book's account and is no longer independent: the Design Within Reach article now redirects to MillerKnoll, the merged Herman Miller and Knoll, where it is listed as a subsidiary brand ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_Within_Reach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_Within_Reach)). The $180 million peak figure itself is unchecked, as is the date and price of the acquisition; the redirected entry carries no revenue history and no first-party filing was read.
- **Handspring took 30% of the Palm-compatible market in fifteen months and $170 million of revenue in twelve, against Palm as the market creator (Chapter 2, pp. 88–89; Chapter 5, pp. 393–394).** The companies and the market are gone: Handspring went public in June 2000, moved to the Treo smartphone from early 2002, and merged into Palm's hardware division in 2003 ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handspring_(company)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handspring_(company))). The personal digital assistant, which the book uses as its running example of a created market, was absorbed into the smartphone; the revenue and share figures themselves are unchecked, since no source read gives them.
- **E.piphany, the author's eighth startup and the source of several worked examples (Preface, p. 15; Chapter 4, pp. 211–212, 262, 279–282).** Still verifiable as a company and finished as one: incorporated 1996 by Steve Blank and Ben Wegbreit, public on NASDAQ in September 1999, about $71.5 million of revenue by 2004, bought by SSA Global on 29 September 2005 and folded into Infor in 2006 ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E.piphany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E.piphany)). The book's claim that an average selling price of $250,000 and a three-year customer lifetime near $1 million emerged from the pricing conversations (Chapter 4, p. 212) is unchecked; it is the author's recollection and no filing was read against it.
- **In-n-Out Burger as a niche resegmentation, "now with 200 company-owned stores", against a McDonald's menu of more than fifty-five items by 2001 (Chapter 2, pp. 94–95).** The store count has roughly doubled: Wikipedia gives 424 locations, with the same three-burger core menu the book describes ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-N-Out_Burger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-N-Out_Burger)). The McDonald's menu count for 2001 is unchecked; no source read carries a historical menu-item count.
- **Sun Microsystems as the survivor of nine licensees of Bechtolsheim's Stanford design, "a $15 billion company" twenty years later (Chapter 6, pp. 516–518).** The scale is in the right range and the ending is not in the book: Sun posted $13.8 billion of revenue in 2007–2008, and Oracle announced its acquisition for $7.4 billion in April 2009, closing in January 2010 ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Microsystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Microsystems)). The company the chapter offers as proof that speed and mission-centric management win outlived the book's writing by about seven years as an independent firm.
- **Southwest Airlines as the most profitable airline in the United States thirty years after 1973, with twenty-five-minute turnarounds and a seat cost 24% below its nearest competitor (Chapter 6, pp. 536–537).** The streak is documented and it ended: Southwest was profitable for 47 consecutive fiscal years from 1973 through 2019 and posted a $3.074 billion net loss in 2020 ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Airlines)). The twenty-five-minute turnaround and the 24% cost gap are unchecked; the source read describes the strategy without quantifying either.
- **CafePress as the worked example of a lived mission statement, with an average store at $45 a month, 40% margins, $30 million of revenue and 25,000 new customers a month (Chapter 6, pp. 493–494).** The company is still trading and has changed hands twice: it went public in March 2012 at $19 a share, was bought by Snapfish for about $25 million in 2018, and by PlanetArt in September 2020 ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CafePress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CafePress)). The four internal figures the mission statement names are unchecked; they are private operating numbers and no source read reports them.
- **First-mover advantage: Montgomery and Lieberman's 1988 paper, their 1998 retrospective backing off it, and Golder and Tellis's 1993 finding that almost half of market pioneers failed while early market leaders failed at 8% (Chapter 5, pp. 377–379).** The citations are correct as printed in the book's own footnotes (Chapter 5, p. 429), and the direction of the later literature supports the author's caution without settling it: surveys of the field record that "different studies have produced varying results" on whether first-mover advantages exist at all, and warn that the same data sets have been reused across studies ([en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-mover_advantage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-mover_advantage)). The 47% and 8% failure rates themselves are unchecked: the Lieberman and Montgomery retrospective is behind a publisher paywall that returned 403, and the Golder and Tellis article was not reached.
- **The New Lanchester Strategy thresholds — 74% for a monopoly, 41% for a leader, 26% for instability, and entry costs of three times or 1.7 times a competitor's sales and marketing budget (Chapter 5, pp. 352–357).** Unchecked, and the author himself does not vouch for the derivation: he writes that he does not swear he can derive the formulas or prove the theorems (Chapter 5, p. 353). No peer-reviewed source for these ratios was reached; the works the bibliography names are a Japanese marketing tradition rather than published empirical studies (Bibliography, pp. 558–559).
- **The messenger theory — experts, evangelists and connectors, and the law of the few from *The Tipping Point* (Chapter 5, pp. 402–403, 408–411).** Unchecked against the later literature. The relevant later work, network research questioning whether a small set of influentials is necessary for a large cascade, could not be checked in this session: the web search budget for the session was exhausted before that query ran, and no direct URL for the paper was available to fetch.
- **The author's own later trajectory: the Lean Startup movement, the business model canvas as a front end, the NSF Innovation Corps with more than 400 teams by early 2013, and a Udacity course with more than 100,000 sign-ups (Introduction, pp. 19–20; author note, pp. 755–756).** The I-Corps figure has grown by roughly sixfold: NSF reports more than 2,500 teams since the program began in 2012, of which nearly 1,400 launched startups that raised $3.16 billion, delivered as a seven-week program in cohorts of twenty to thirty teams, with three new Hubs named in October 2024 ([nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/i-corps](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/i-corps)). The Udacity sign-up figure is unchecked; the course page was not reached.
- **The tooling the book assumes.** Its Customer Development activities are paper briefs, slide presentations, trade shows, printed data sheets, Wall Street analyst reports and phone calls, with Appendix B measuring each worksheet in days of authoring plus a half-day meeting (Appendix B, pp. 608, 612, 617). The book was written before the cheap instrumentation the repo now uses for the same questions — a cookieless analytics product at $6 a month and a hosted newsletter platform, both recorded in [ADR-0016](../../adr/0016-teaser-analytics-are-cookieless-and-the-channel-rides-on-the-form.md) and [ADR-0014](../../adr/0014-the-waitlist-lives-outside-our-stack.md) — and it names none of that class of tool.
- **The disguised and unnamed companies: FastOffice and Steve Powell (Chapter 3, pp. 105–108, 121–122), InLook, Snapshot and Chip Stevens (Chapter 4, pp. 235–243), PhotosToYou and its founders (Chapter 5, pp. 339–348), RoboVac (Chapter 3, pp. 190–191), and BetaSheet (Chapter 6, p. 433).** Unchecked, and BetaSheet is unverifiable by construction: the author states the names there were changed (Chapter 6, p. 442). None of these is a company a later source can be matched to with confidence, so none of the outcomes the book reports for them can be confirmed or refuted.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **The evidence is a set of cases chosen after the outcome was known.** Every company in the book is introduced by its ending: Webvan and Furniture.com failed and are shown not following the model, Design Within Reach and Tesco succeeded and are shown following it. The author's own bridge for the successes is that they understood the process "explicitly or implicitly" (Chapter 2, p. 67), which no observation can contradict, because any surviving company can be read as an implicit adopter. There is no counted sample, no comparison group, and not one company in the book that followed Customer Development and still failed. The claim that all successful startups recapitulate the process (Preface, p. 16) is therefore asserted rather than shown.
- **Market Type does the explanatory work and is also a choice.** The book's second thesis is that Market Type changes everything (Chapter 2, p. 90), while the same book says the decision is late-binding (Chapter 3, p. 166), that Market Type "is not immutable", and that almost any product fitting an existing market could be positioned into a resegmented one (Chapter 5, p. 351). A variable the founder selects cannot both determine the outcome and be freely chosen, and the combination makes a post-hoc diagnosis available for any result. PhotosToYou is the case where this shows: its failure is attributed to reading its Market Type wrong, yet the same pages say its revenue was bounded by how many people owned digital cameras in 1999 (Chapter 5, pp. 345–346), which is a market-size problem a different launch strategy would not have fixed.
- **Numbers arrive without provenance.** Three to seven years to profitability in a new market is "derived from looking at the results" of hundreds of high-tech startups over twenty years, with no sample, definition or citation (Chapter 5, p. 359). The New Lanchester thresholds and entry-cost ratios carry the author's own disclaimer that he cannot derive or prove them (Chapter 5, p. 353). The sales conversion rules — one in twenty prospects engages, one in three to five of those closes (Chapter 4, pp. 302–303) — and the prospecting rule that fifty follow-up calls yield five to ten visits (Chapter 3, p. 186) are presented as rules of thumb with nothing behind them. These are the figures a reader would plan a budget against, and they are the least supported claims in the book.
- **It is an enterprise software book that says it is a general one.** The influence maps, the access map, the sales closer, the four-constituency approval sequence, the ROI justification worked at $900,000 of customer cost, the channel food chain, the industry analyst firms, and every one of Appendix B's 149 pages of worksheets assume a six-figure sale into a large organization (Chapter 3, pp. 154–156; Chapter 4, pp. 268–287; Appendix B, pp. 601–749). Consumer guidance is added a sentence at a time — the same questions are said to work for consumers (Chapter 3, p. 194) — and the consumer section then concedes that some consumer products address no problem or need at all (Chapter 3, p. 145), which removes the problem-first method's foundation for that entire class of product. A small self-serve purchase with no approval chain is the case the book addresses least.
- **It presumes a funded company with a board and an executive staff.** Phase 0 is agreement between investors and founders (Chapter 3, p. 128) and requires funding for two to three passes through Discovery and Validation (p. 129). Later phases assume a VP of Product Development to synchronize with, an executive staff to align, four advisory boards to staff, and a board to review the CEO (Chapter 4, pp. 289, 294–298; Chapter 6, p. 484). The smallest configuration the book describes is a founder outside the building while five engineers write code (Appendix A, p. 597). A company with no board, no investors and no engineers to synchronize with is never described, so a reader in that position has to guess which of the phases still apply and which have no counterpart.
- **The last chapter abandons the book's own standard.** Chapters 1 to 5 argue that opinions inside the building are worthless and facts must be gathered outside it. Chapter 6's mission-centric management, employee initiative, mutual trust, good-enough decision-making, mission synchronization and leadership culture rest on two company vignettes and on United States Marine Corps warfighting doctrine, John Boyd's OODA loop and a line from Patton (Chapter 6, pp. 512–541), under a Sun Tzu epigraph (Chapter 6, p. 433). Nothing in that chapter is tested the way the earlier chapters demand, and the Sun and Southwest attributions — that Sun beat eight other licensees because it executed faster, and that Southwest's profitability came from culture rather than planes or routes (pp. 518, 536) — are exactly the kind of after-the-fact causal reading the first chapter criticizes in others.
- **The 2013 edition is the 2003 text.** The author states the edition is substantively unchanged with typos corrected (Introduction, p. 20), so every price band, channel assumption, analyst firm and company example is at least two decades old, and the new Introduction acknowledges a decade of practice it does not fold into the argument. The author's own update is a different book, *The Startup Owner's Manual*, which this one points at rather than absorbing (Introduction, pp. 20–21).
- **The rule about features has no stated threshold.** The method says to find a market for the product as specified rather than to change the product (Chapter 2, p. 98), and that no features are added until the search of market space is exhausted (Chapter 3, p. 203). It then permits a pivot when enough customers ask for the same thing (Chapter 4, p. 310) and an exit from Validation back into Discovery to reconfigure or repackage the product (Chapter 4, p. 334). What counts as exhausting the market space, and how many customers make a request no longer custom, are never defined, so the discipline the book is most emphatic about is entirely a matter of the judgment of whoever applies it.
- **The central term is only observable in retrospect.** An earlyvangelist is defined by five characteristics ending in a homegrown interim solution and an acquirable budget (Chapter 3, pp. 116–118), and the author's contention is that only points four and five count. None of the five can be observed before a conversation, and the conversation the book prescribes is itself the thing that is supposed to be targeted at earlyvangelists. The prospecting instruction accordingly falls back on any individual who will give some time and loosely fits the customer hypothesis (Chapter 3, p. 182), which is not the definition the chapter argues for.
