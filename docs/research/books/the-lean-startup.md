## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that entrepreneurship is a kind of management, that a startup's unit of progress is neither revenue nor shipped features but what he calls validated learning, and that this learning is produced by running the vision through a Build-Measure-Learn feedback loop as fast as possible (Introduction, PDF pp. 17, 22-23). A startup is defined without reference to size, industry, or sector: a human institution creating a new product or service under conditions of extreme uncertainty (Ch. 2 Define, PDF p. 38). The five principles the book says inform all three of its parts are that entrepreneurs are everywhere, that entrepreneurship is management, that learning is validated by experiment, that Build-Measure-Learn is the fundamental activity, and that progress is tracked by a discipline he names innovation accounting (Introduction, PDF pp. 22-23). The claimed payoff is that startup success can be engineered by following a process, which means it can be learned and taught (Introduction, PDF p. 17).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a collection of tactics: the IMVU techniques students most want to copy, a low-quality early prototype, charging from day one, tiny revenue targets, are called useful but not the moral, with "too many exceptions" (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF p. 62). It is not the "just do it" school, which the book treats as the mirror failure of over-planning (Introduction, PDF p. 24; Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF p. 64). It is not opposed to quality or to high-quality products, only to quality decided before the customer is known (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 110). It is not a rigid formula for the pivot decision, because the human element cannot be removed from entrepreneurship (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 145). And it is not a blueprint: the closing warning is that anyone adopting it as a defined set of steps will not succeed, because telling a systemic problem from a special one requires theory (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF p. 259).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction (PDF pp. 16-25)

Two founding stories carry the argument. The first company failed with what the author calls a great product and the right idea, because nobody in it knew the process that turns a product insight into a company (Introduction, PDF p. 16). The second is IMVU, where the founders deliberately did everything the received wisdom forbade: they shipped a buggy minimum viable product, charged for it, changed it dozens of times a day, talked to customers constantly, and then declined to do what those customers asked, treating their input as one signal among several (Introduction, PDF p. 18). The author's stated reading of the entrepreneurial press is that its success stories are selection bias and after-the-fact rationalization, and that most startups fail (Introduction, PDF p. 17). The method's parentage is named: lean manufacturing, design thinking, Steve Blank's Customer Development, and agile development (Introduction, PDF p. 19). Evidence offered for IMVU's outcome is 60 million avatars, more than $50 million of annual revenue in 2011, more than a hundred employees, and a catalog of more than 6 million customer-created items growing by more than 7,000 a day (Introduction, PDF p. 21).

### Part One VISION, Ch. 1 Start (PDF pp. 26-35)

The chapter's claim is that general management, successful as it has been, is ill suited to the uncertainty a startup faces, and that the alternative most founders reach for, avoiding process altogether, produces chaos rather than success (Ch. 1 Start, PDF p. 27). The book's stated macro premise, sourced in the endnotes to a FiveThirtyEight post, is that United States manufacturing output rose 15 percent in a decade while jobs were lost, leaving more productive capacity than firms know how to use (Ch. 1 Start, PDF pp. 27-28; Endnotes, PDF p. 280). Two metaphors do the structural work. A startup has an engine of growth, tuned by every release, feature and campaign, and a steering wheel, the Build-Measure-Learn loop, and the author's charge against conventional business plans is that they are written as though launching a rocket rather than driving a car, so a small error in assumptions ends in catastrophe (Ch. 1 Start, PDF pp. 32-33). The name for that outcome is "achieving failure": executing a flawed plan faithfully and well, which the book illustrates with an unnamed company that forecast millions of customers, built the infrastructure and the support organization for them, and could not adapt when they did not arrive (Ch. 1 Start, PDF p. 33). Vision is placed above strategy and strategy above product: the product changes constantly, the strategy changes in a pivot, the vision rarely changes at all (Ch. 1 Start, PDF p. 34).

### Ch. 2 Define (PDF pp. 36-45)

The definition of a startup is broadened deliberately to include the manager inside a large company, whom the book calls an entrepreneur on the same terms as a garage founder (Ch. 2 Define, PDF p. 37). The three parts of the definition are a human institution, a new product or service under the broadest reading of product, and a context of extreme uncertainty; a business that clones an existing one exactly is excluded, because its success depends only on execution and can be modeled accurately, which is offered as the reason such businesses can be financed by bank loans (Ch. 2 Define, PDF pp. 38-39). The worked example is Intuit's SnapTax, built by a team of five with an executive sponsor who gave them an "island of freedom", which shipped a product that filed only simple returns and only in California and then took more than 350,000 downloads in three weeks at national launch in 2011 (Ch. 2 Define, PDF pp. 39-41). The chapter's other evidence is Scott Cook's own account: TurboTax moved from one annual initiative to more than five hundred changes tested in a two-and-a-half-month season, up to seventy tests a week, with a Thursday-to-Tuesday cycle, and Cook's argument is that a company running one test breeds politicians while one running five hundred breeds entrepreneurs (Ch. 2 Define, PDF p. 43). Intuit's own accountability measure is reported as two numbers, the count of customers using products that did not exist three years ago and the share of revenue from offerings that did not exist three years ago, against a historical average of 5.5 years for a new product to reach $50 million (Ch. 2 Define, PDF pp. 44-45). The chapter closes on Cook's claim that building the experimentation system is senior management's job, not the team's (Ch. 2 Define, PDF p. 45).

### Ch. 3 Learn (PDF pp. 46-63)

The chapter opens by conceding that learning is the oldest excuse for a failure of execution and that nobody can spend it or return it to investors, which is offered as the reason the term needs rehabilitating as validated learning: empirical demonstration that a team has discovered true things about the business's prospects (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF pp. 46-47). The evidence is IMVU's first six months. The strategy was an instant-messaging add-on that would interoperate with the incumbent networks so that customers need not move their friends, reasoned from network effects, Metcalfe's law, and a market where the top three networks held more than 80 percent of usage (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF p. 48). The product shipped after 180 days and nobody downloaded it; revenue targets were set at $300 a month, then $350, then $400, and met by asking friends and family (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF pp. 50-51). The customer conversations that followed are reported at length: teenagers did not know what an add-on was, refused to invite a friend to something they had not judged cool, ran eight IM clients rather than the one or two the office assumed, and wanted to meet strangers rather than talk to existing friends (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF pp. 52-54). The author's own conclusion is that thousands of lines of his interoperability code were waste, and the counterfactual he poses is whether the same lesson could have been bought with one network, or with none, by offering the download before building anything (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF pp. 55-57). A worked positive example follows: replacing the phrase "avatar chat" with "3D instant messaging" across the site, split between two halves of new traffic, raised both signup and long-term paying conversion, while paying customer service agents to act as tour guides for new customers changed nothing (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF p. 59). The section named "The Audacity of Zero" argues that zero revenue invites imagination while small revenue invites doubt, which creates an incentive to delay collecting any data at all, and that the cure is validated learning rather than "success theater" such as a bought advertisement or a public-relations push (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF pp. 60-61).

### Ch. 4 Experiment (PDF pp. 64-78)

The claim is that every product, feature and campaign is an experiment, beginning with a hypothesis that predicts something and tested empirically, and that the lesson of the scientific method for startups is that an approach which cannot fail teaches nothing (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF p. 64). Four cases carry it. Zappos: Nick Swinmurn photographed the inventory of local shoe stores, posted the pictures, and bought the shoes at full price when somebody ordered, which the book credits with testing demand by observed behavior rather than by survey, and with exposing the company to payment, returns and support at the same time (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF pp. 65-66). Hewlett-Packard: Caroline Barlerin's employee-volunteering program is analyzed rather than reported, and the analysis is explicitly hypothetical, with the author naming what an experiment might look like, a dozen long-serving employees given a concierge experience and then measured on whether they volunteer again and recruit a colleague, against a plan that would take months to write (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF pp. 66-70). Kodak Gallery: Mark Cook's four questions, whether consumers recognize the problem, whether they would buy a solution, whether they would buy it from us, and whether we can build it, with the observation that product development usually skips to the fourth; the gilded wedding cards that tested well and could neither be photographed attractively nor produced easily; and the event-album prototype so thin the team did not want to show it, which nonetheless confirmed that customers wanted albums and, through the KISSinsights survey tool, surfaced the ordering feature they would not launch without (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF pp. 71-73). Village Laundry Service: a washing machine on the back of a pickup truck in Bangalore for under $8,000, which found that customers would hand over laundry but suspected a truck that might drive away, that they wanted ironing, and that they would pay double for four-hour turnaround, and which ended as a fourteen-location business reporting 116,000 kg serviced in 2010 against 30,600 kg in 2009, and roughly 60 percent repeat business (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF pp. 73-75). The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau section is the author's own proposal rather than a report: a Twilio hotline with voice prompts, advertised to a few city blocks, standing in for a $500 million agency's call center, with the agency's actual choice reported as segmenting by product rather than by geography, credit cards first (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF pp. 75-78). The chapter's general rule is that the two assumptions to break a vision into are the value hypothesis and the growth hypothesis, and that an experiment is itself a first product (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF pp. 68, 70).

### Part Two STEER (PDF pp. 79-82)

The loop is stated: Build-Measure-Learn, whose activities happen in that order but whose planning runs backwards, from what must be learned, through what must be measured, to what must be built (Part Two, PDF p. 82). The part names its three instruments in advance: the minimum viable product as the version that completes one turn of the loop with the least effort and development time, innovation accounting as the quantitative method for deciding whether engine tuning is working, and learning milestones as the alternative to product milestones, offered as useful to founders and to the investors holding them to account (Part Two, PDF pp. 81-82). The stated goal of everything in the part is to minimize total time through the loop (Part Two, PDF p. 81).

### Ch. 5 Leap (PDF pp. 83-93)

Facebook is used to show what a leap of faith looks like when it has been validated: more than half of registered users returning daily, which the book reads as the value hypothesis, and near three-quarters of Harvard undergraduates in the first month with no marketing spend, which it reads as the growth hypothesis, against contemporary criticism that the company had "no business model" (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF pp. 83-84). The chapter's method is to restate an argument by analogy in the present tense so that the hidden assumption becomes visible, worked through a plan claiming progressive rendering would win a market the way progressive image loading won the web (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF pp. 85-86). Randy Komisar's analogs and antilogs are cited, with the Walkman as the iPod's analog for whether people would listen in public through earphones, and Napster as the antilog for whether they would pay (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF pp. 86-87). Against the right-place-right-time explanation, the book cites a count drawn from Charles Duryea of 501 United States automobile companies formed between 1900 and 1908, of which 60 percent folded within a couple of years (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF p. 87; Endnotes, PDF p. 283). The Toyota term genchi gembutsu, translated as going to see for yourself, is illustrated with Yuji Yokoya driving more than 53,000 miles across North America before redesigning the 2004 Sienna, whose sales the book reports as 60 percent above 2003 (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF pp. 89-90). Scott Cook's two phone books, Palo Alto and Winnetka, are offered as the same thing at startup scale: calls at random asking whether paying bills by hand is frustrating, before any product existed (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF p. 91). The chapter's output is a customer archetype, which it insists is a hypothesis rather than a fact and which the emerging Lean User Experience practice treats as provisional until validated (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF p. 92), and its closing warning names the two failure modes as the just-do-it school and analysis paralysis (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF p. 93).

### Ch. 6 Test (PDF pp. 94-113)

The minimum viable product is defined as the fastest way through the loop with the least effort, not the smallest imaginable product, and its goal is to start learning rather than to end it (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 95). Groupon is the opening case: a skinned WordPress blog, T-shirts sold by asking for color and size by email, coupons generated in FileMaker and mailed as PDFs in batches of 500, which Andrew Mason describes as cobbled together (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 94-95). The chapter's reason for tolerating a poor first product is the early adopter, who accepts and even prefers "an 80 percent solution" and is suspicious of something too polished, with the first iPhone's missing copy and paste as the example, so polish beyond what early adopters demand is waste (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 96). Four MVP techniques are named and worked. The video MVP: Drew Houston's three-minute Dropbox demonstration, salted with in-jokes for one community, which he reports took the beta waiting list from 5,000 to 75,000 overnight (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 99-100). The concierge MVP: Food on the Table began with one customer, one grocery store, no recipes and no software, with the chief executive and the vice-president of product visiting her weekly with a printed packet and collecting a check for $9.95, and automating only at the point where they were too busy to take another customer (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 100-103). The Wizard of Oz test: Aardvark's sixth prototype, each of the first five built in two to four weeks and shown to a hundred or two hundred friends with results the founders call unambiguously negative, then nine months of eight people answering queries by hand behind what customers took to be software, through a seed and a series A round (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 104-107). And the smoke test, named here as the simplest end of the range, little more than an advertisement (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 97), and described in the next chapter as an old direct-marketing technique offering a preorder for something not yet built, which measures only whether customers are interested in trying a product (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 117-118). The chapter's quality principle is stated as a conditional, that not knowing who the customer is means not knowing what quality is, and it is illustrated by IMVU shipping avatars that teleported instantly instead of walking, a change the team felt embarrassed by and customers named among the three things they liked best (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 108-109). Four objections are answered: patent windows, which the author says open on public release in some jurisdictions and warrant legal counsel; competitors stealing the idea, against which the book offers the exercise of trying to give an idea away to the relevant product manager at an established company; brand damage, answered by launching under a different name and by the obscurity of a startup; and team morale, answered by committing in advance to iterate rather than to a go-or-kill decision (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 110-112).

### Ch. 7 Measure (PDF pp. 114-144)

Innovation accounting is defined as a three-step sequence: use an MVP to establish a real baseline, tune the engine from that baseline toward the model in the business plan, and then decide to pivot or persevere, with the riskiest assumption tested first (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 117-119). The worked failure is IMVU's own. Five dollars a day on Google AdWords bought a hundred clicks, which the book calls insignificant as marketing and priceless as learning, because it produced a fresh, independent cohort every day (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF p. 120). Seven months of daily releases moved the share of new customers using the product at least five times from under 5 percent to almost 20 percent, and left the share paying stuck at about 1 percent, which the author reports as the reading that sent him back to customer conversations (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 121-123). Cohort analysis is presented as the alternative to cumulative totals, each cohort an independent report card that cannot be blamed on legacy customers or market conditions (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 121-123). The same board meeting's other graph, total registered users and total paying customers, is the chapter's illustration of a vanity metric: the same data, in a shape that shows a hockey stick (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 127-128). Grockit supplies the positive case. Farbood Nivi's MVP was himself teaching test preparation over WebEx with no custom software, at $10,000 to $15,000 a month; the company then ran disciplined agile sprints, shipped constantly, and could not tell whether its numbers moved because of its work or because of press coverage, an ambiguity the book attributes to gross metrics rather than to a lack of discipline (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 129-133). Two changes are credited with fixing it: cohort-based metrics, and launching each feature as a split test, a technique the book traces to direct mail and to catalog A/B tests (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 134-135). A kanban rule follows, in which a user story is not done until it is validated, validation usually being a split test showing changed behavior, with four buckets of fixed capacity and an unvalidated feature removed from the product (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 136-137). The reported result is that engineers start writing stories that can be validated, and that teams begin measuring productivity by validated learning rather than by features delivered (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 137-138). The chapter's single most-quoted experiment is Grockit's test of lazy registration, an industry best practice requiring three classes of user and significant ongoing work: a cohort forced to register up front behaved identically on registration, activation and retention, which the book reads both as waste removed and as evidence that customers were deciding on the marketing rather than on the product (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 138-139). The chapter closes on the three A's. Actionable means the report demonstrates cause and effect, against which the website hit fails because nobody can say what it is (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 140-142). Accessible means concrete units and people rather than data points, which is the argument for cohort reports and for Grockit mailing every employee the day's experiment results in plain English (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 141-143). Auditable means the data can be checked by hand against real customers and drawn from the master data rather than an intermediate system, because the loser of an argument will challenge the data, and the book reports that a team whose conclusion is overturned by a data fault loses confidence and discipline (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF pp. 143-144).

### Ch. 8 Pivot (or Persevere) (PDF pp. 145-175)

A pivot is defined narrowly as a structured course correction that tests a new fundamental hypothesis about product, strategy and engine of growth, and the book is explicit that the method offers no clinical formula for the decision (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 145). Votizen is the extended case, reported with its numbers. David Binetti's first MVP cost about $1,200 and three months and returned 5 percent registration and 17 percent activation, too little data to read retention or referral; two months and $5,000 of split testing moved registration to 17 percent and activation above 90 percent, and exposed 5 percent retention and 4 percent referral; three further months of optimization reached 8 percent and 6 percent, at a total of eight months and $20,000 (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 147-149). A zoom-in pivot to @2gov, four months and $30,000, produced 42 percent registration, 83 percent activation, 21 percent retention and 54 percent referral, with under 1 percent willing to pay; a customer segment pivot to business-to-business produced letters of intent, hiring against them, and no closed sales; a platform pivot to self-serve reached 51, 92, 28 and 64 percent with 11 percent paying 20 cents a message (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 152-156). The pattern the book draws from it is the acceleration of MVPs, eight months to four to three to one, attributed to learning rather than to accumulated code, much of which was discarded or became a legacy burden between pivots (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 157). The chapter's definition of runway is the number of pivots left rather than months of cash, with the corollary that cutting costs can shorten the loop and so shorten the runway it was meant to extend (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 158-159). Three reasons are given for pivoting too late: vanity metrics that prevent a team from believing change is needed, an unclear hypothesis that makes complete failure impossible to experience, and fear, whose specific shape is named as the fear that a vision will be judged wrong before it has had a fair chance (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 159-160). Path is the illustration of facing that fear, an MVP launched by well-known founders into hostile blog coverage, with a fifty-connection limit taken from Robin Dunbar's research that the technology press found objectionable and customers did not (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 160-161). The book recommends a scheduled pivot-or-persevere meeting, more than a few weeks apart and less than a few months, attended by product and business leadership together, with the product team bringing optimization results over time and the business side bringing customer conversations (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 162). Wealthfront's pivot out of the kaChing fantasy-trading game is the worked meeting: 450,000 gamers, seven amateurs qualified to manage money, fourteen paying customers against a model predicting hundreds, and two lines of qualitative inquiry, professional money managers who welcomed transparency and consumers confused by a site mixing virtual and real portfolios (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 163-165). What survived the pivot was the manager-evaluation technology, and the book reports $180 million on the platform and more than forty professional managers at the time of writing (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 166). IMVU's own failure to pivot is the counter-case: growing revenue and rising vanity metrics masked an exhausted early-adopter market, activation and monetization fell as marketing reached mainstream customers, and the fix required a cross-functional team rebuilding the product in a sandbox against the old design until it won, with revenue reported at more than $25 million by 2009 (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 167-170). The chapter ends with a catalog of ten pivots: zoom-in, zoom-out, customer segment, customer need, platform, business architecture, value capture, engine of growth, channel, and technology (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 170-173).

### Part Three ACCELERATE, Ch. 9 Batch (PDF pp. 176-198)

The envelope-stuffing race from *Lean Thinking* opens the chapter: one envelope at a time beats folding all the letters first, because the intuition ignores the cost of sorting and moving half-finished piles, and because a letter that does not fit the envelope is discovered at the end of a large batch and at once in a small one (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 179-180). Toyota's history is the evidence: a market too small for American economies of scale, no capital for large machines, and Shigeo Shingo's SMED work reducing changeovers from hours to under ten minutes by restructuring the work rather than by asking anyone to hurry (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF p. 181). The andon cord follows, stopping the line as soon as a defect appears, which the book credits with Toyota's quality and cost position (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF p. 182). IMVU's translation of this is continuous deployment: engineers and designers working together on one feature, released to a small number of people as soon as it is testable, about fifty changes a day on average, protected by automated tests and by what the team called an immune system that also watched business metrics, because a checkout button turned white on a white background passes every functional test and ends the business (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 183-185). The immune system's four actions are reverting the change, notifying the team, blocking further changes, and holding the block until the root cause is fixed (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF p. 185). Three forces are named as bringing the same speed to physical products: hardware becoming software, production lines that can change over without excess inventory of the old version, and 3D printing and rapid prototyping against injection molding's large-batch economics (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 185-186). SGW Designworks is the physical-goods case, a collapsible military x-ray frame taken from 3D model to first prototypes in three days, through two more design cycles in five days, to a first production run of forty units in fifteen days, with eight products designed and delivered in twelve months (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 187-189). School of One is the education case, daily per-student playlists that let a teacher change a sequence and see its effect on the students currently at that point (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 189-190). The large-batch death spiral is defined as the tendency of batches to grow because moving a batch forward creates interruptions and rework, with no physical ceiling in knowledge work, until one release becomes a bet on the company and managers are incentivized to add to it rather than ship it (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 190-192). Hospital pharmacy and phlebotomy examples are offered as the same arithmetic outside software, where smaller batches lower total system cost even at the price of another phlebotomist (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 192-193). Pull is explained through a Toyota bumper moving up the supply chain, and then corrected for startups: the signal that pulls work is not a customer's stated want but the next experiment the team's hypothesis requires (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 193-195). Alphabet Energy is the clean-technology case, building thermoelectrics on silicon wafers so the existing semiconductor infrastructure could be used, six weeks from concept to a physical device, three months to disprove the power-plant hypothesis, and about $1 million raised against SunPower's factories and BrightSource's $291 million (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 195-197).

### Ch. 10 Grow (PDF pp. 199-216)

Sustainable growth is defined by one rule, that new customers come from the actions of past customers, which excludes one-time advertisements and publicity stunts (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 200). Four sources are listed, word of mouth, a side effect of product usage, funded advertising paid for out of revenue rather than investment, and repeat purchase, and these are folded into three engines (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 200-201). The sticky engine turns on retention: the rule given is that the product grows when new customer acquisition exceeds churn, and the rate of compounding is the difference between them, illustrated by a company acquiring at 39 percent a period against 61 percent retention for a compounding rate of 0.02 percent, and by the dot-com-era PointCast, whose acquisition was strong and whose churn matched it (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 203-204). The viral engine turns on the viral coefficient, the number of new customers each signup brings, where below 1.0 the loop fizzles and above 1.0 it compounds; Hotmail's appended line offering a free account is the case, reported as 1 million customers in six months, 2 million five weeks later, and a sale to Microsoft at 12 million subscribers for $400 million, with Tupperware parties as the offline equivalent (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 205-206). The book's observation about viral products is that they often cannot charge, because price is friction in the loop, so the value hypothesis has to be tested by a nonmonetary exchange, and a company selling advertising is described as serving two customer groups in two currencies (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 207). The paid engine turns on the margin between customer lifetime value and cost per acquisition, and the chapter's claim about it is that competition bids up the cost of any acquisition source until firms pay away most of their marginal profit, so lasting growth on this engine requires a differentiated ability to monetize a particular set of customers; IMVU's own example is collecting payment from customers without credit cards, by phone billing and by cash in the mail (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 209-211). The author recommends focusing on one engine at a time, while noting more than one can operate (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 211). The chapter closes by reworking Marc Andreessen's product/market fit into engine terms, with the argument that direction matters more than level, illustrated by a company compounding at 5 percent after six months of rising numbers against one flat at about 10 percent (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 211-214), and by the observation that every engine eventually exhausts its set of customers, which can take years and then presents as a sudden crisis (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF pp. 214-215).

### Ch. 11 Adapt (PDF pp. 217-243)

The chapter's problem is how much process to add as a company grows, and its objection to splitting the difference is that the practice rewards whoever states the most extreme position, which escalates (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF p. 218). The proposed alternative is an adaptive organization that adjusts its own process, with IMVU's new-hire training program offered as something that evolved rather than being decided: every new engineer assigned a mentor, with the mentor's performance linked to the mentee's (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF p. 219). The speed argument is stated as a paradox borrowed from Toyota, stopping production so that production never has to stop, and as the claim that quality cannot be traded for time because the resulting defects slow the team later; the book notes that IMVU's early low quality was harmless when nobody would use the product and became a brake once mainstream customers arrived and once each new feature could break an existing one (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 220-221). The Five Whys is presented as the mechanism, with Taiichi Ohno's blown-fuse example quoted from *Toyota Production System* and its conclusion that a technical fault resolves into a human one (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 222-223). The author's own worked chain runs from a disabled feature through a failed server, a misused subsystem and an untrained engineer to a manager who thinks his team is "too busy" for training (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 223-224). Proportional investment is the rule attached to it: a small symptom buys a small fix at each of the five levels, an hour of an eight-week plan rather than the plan, and a recurring symptom buys more, which the book describes as an automatic speed regulator (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 224-225). The named failure mode is "the Five Blames", and the tactics offered against it are putting everyone connected to the problem in the room, because whoever is absent becomes the target, and a mantra from the most senior person present that a mistake so easy to make is the system's fault (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 226-227). A simplified two-rule version is offered where trust is lacking, tolerate every mistake once and never allow the same one twice, with the admission that it works at first and does not hold up over the long term (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF p. 228). Practical advice follows: executive sponsorship is required, start with a narrow and specific class of symptom under an unambiguous triggering rule, and appoint a Five Whys master senior enough to make assignments stick and junior enough to attend (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 229-231). IGN is the case: a first attempt that took an hour, covered a list of long-standing complaints, lacked the people who knew the systems, and failed; and a later success under Tony Ford on a project missing its deadlines, whose published chain runs from blog posts that could not be edited, through a 500 error, an incompatible gem, an untested gem added in production, and gems installed ahead of a deployment, to a bonus question about production changes on a Friday night, with a named owner and an investment at each level (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 231-235). QuickBooks closes the chapter as a three-year account: year one shipped an online-banking rewrite that passed every technical test, took customers four to five times longer to reconcile transactions, dropped the Net Promoter Score twenty points and took nine months to fix; year two halved the cycle on paper and slid back, which Greg Wright attributes to organizational "muscle memory"; year three replaced the process outright, with teams of no more than five, twenty to twenty-five branches instead of five, six-week end-to-end features, customers involved from each feature's inception, and a virtualization layer that let a second version read real customer data without being able to change it (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 236-242).

### Ch. 12 Innovate (PDF pp. 244-260)

Three structural attributes are named as prerequisites for an internal startup team: scarce but secure resources, because a startup is more sensitive to a mid-course cut than a division is; independent development authority, cross-functional and able to ship without an excess of approvals; and a personal stake in the outcome, which the book says need not be financial, since credit and named ownership work in nonprofits and government, and which it illustrates with Toyota's shusa, the chief engineer with final authority over a vehicle (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 244-246). The chapter then inverts the usual framing. Its extended case is a pricing meeting at a consulting client where nobody could agree what the experiment's data meant, the people who designed it were no longer in the same division as those who ran it, and each department argued from the interpretation that suited it, so the decision was made on the most plausible-sounding argument (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 247-248). The fear underneath is described as rational: the enterprise segment carried the revenue, the consumer segment needed a lower price, and sabotage is called a rational response from a manager whose territory is threatened (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 249-250). Hiding the innovation team is therefore rejected, with the original IBM PC's skunkworks named as a cautionary tale rather than a model, because managers who learn that something of that size was concealed become more political, not less (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 250-251). The proposed alternative is an innovation sandbox with seven rules: any team may run a true split test touching only the sandboxed part of the product or certain segments; one team sees an experiment through end to end; no experiment runs longer than a stated time; none touches more than a stated share of customers; every experiment is judged on one standard report of five to ten actionable metrics; every team in the sandbox uses the same metrics; and the team monitors reactions while the experiment runs and aborts it if something catastrophic happens (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 251-252). Customers inside the sandbox are treated as real, and the sandbox is expected to grow rather than to be exited, a pricing page first, then the home page, then search (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 252, 256-257). The chapter's second half is a portfolio argument: four kinds of work, from research and development through growth, optimization and legacy operation, each wanting a different temperament, and the common practice of letting the inventor follow the product through every phase is named as one reason established companies cannot find creative managers for the next thing (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 254-256). The author's proposal is that entrepreneur be an actual job title inside large organizations, held accountable through innovation accounting (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF p. 256). The chapter ends on the author's own discomfort at becoming the status quo at IMVU, on the anchoring incentive that rewards a radical proposal with a favorable compromise, and on the observation that switching a team's definition of productivity from functional efficiency to validated learning makes things feel worse before better, because the new system's problems are tangible and the old system's were not (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 257-260).

### Ch. 13 Epilogue: Waste Not (PDF pp. 261-272)

The epilogue is built on the centenary of Frederick Winslow Taylor's *The Principles of Scientific Management*, crediting Taylor with inventing management itself and arguing that his revolution succeeded too well, with the techniques remembered and the scientific habit of mind forgotten (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF p. 261). Taylor's own line about the man having been first and the system having to be first is quoted, and so is his less-quoted qualification that the first object of a good system is developing first-class people (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF pp. 261, 265). The author's diagnosis of the present is that productive capacity now exceeds the ability to know what to build, so the question has moved from whether something can be built to whether it should be (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF p. 262). Peter Drucker's line about doing efficiently what should not be done at all is cited, and the author concedes he has no solid estimate of how wasteful modern knowledge work is, only anecdote gathered from consulting and travel (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF p. 263). Two dangers are named for the movement itself: reductionism, the fate the epilogue assigns to scientific management, and pseudoscience, defined here as highlighting a graph of gross metrics to assert cause and effect, or invoking learning to excuse a failure without turning it into validated learning in the next cycle (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF pp. 266-267). The proposed remedy is research the book does not itself conduct: startup testing labs that compare development methodologies against calibrated problems drawn from programming competitions, raising uncertainty while still measuring outcome quality, and varying cycle time by platform and channel (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF pp. 269-270). The chapter's last proposal is a Long-Term Stock Exchange, on which companies would report innovation accounting alongside profits, tie executive compensation to long-term performance, and trade at higher transaction costs to discourage day trading (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF pp. 270-271).

### Ch. 14 Join the Movement, Endnotes, Disclosures (PDF pp. 273-293)

The closing chapter lists the movement's resources: theleanstartup.com, the author's Startup Lessons Learned blog, Lean Startup meetups described as over a hundred groups with the largest in San Francisco, Boston, New York, Chicago and Los Angeles, the volunteer-maintained Lean Startup Wiki, the Lean Startup Circle mailing list founded by Rich Collins, and the Startup Lessons Learned conference (Ch. 14, PDF pp. 273-274). Required reading is Steve Blank's *The Four Steps to the Epiphany*, with Cooper and Vlaskovits's customer development guide beside it, followed by a blogroll and a reading list running from Christensen and Geoffrey Moore through Reinertsen, Liker, Womack and Jones, Taylor, Kent Beck, Ohno, Deming, Sloan, Drucker, and Mullins and Komisar's *Getting to Plan B* (Ch. 14, PDF pp. 274-279). The Disclosures page is short and load-bearing: the author states a relationship or equity interest in Aardvark, Dropbox, Food on the Table, Grockit, IMVU, Intuit, Votizen and Wealthfront, and further interests through 500 Startups, Kleiner Perkins Caufield and Byers, Floodgate, Greylock Partners and Seraph Group (Disclosures, PDF pp. 291-293).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope of the definition.** A business that is an exact clone of an existing one, down to model, pricing, target customer and product, is excluded from the definition of a startup, because its success depends only on execution and can be modeled accurately (Ch. 2 Define, PDF p. 39). The book also excludes most businesses, large and small alike, from the context it describes (Ch. 2 Define, PDF p. 39).
- **The tactics are not the moral.** The IMVU techniques most readers want to copy, a low-quality prototype, charging from day one, tiny revenue targets, are called useful but not the lesson, with "too many exceptions", and the author states plainly that not every kind of customer will accept a low-quality prototype (Ch. 3 Learn, PDF p. 62).
- **Quality.** The method is described as not opposed to high-quality products, only to deciding quality before the customer is known, and the caveat is explicit that defects which slow the Build-Measure-Learn loop interfere with learning and are dangerous to tolerate in any production process (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 110). The same tension is restated later as a paradox: shortcuts taken today in quality, design or infrastructure slow the company tomorrow, and IMVU's own low quality became a brake once mainstream customers arrived (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 220-221).
- **How small an MVP should be.** The book says the decision cannot be made formulaically and requires judgment, offering only the observation that most people overestimate how many features are needed (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 97). It also states that an MVP is not necessarily the smallest product imaginable (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 95).
- **Patents.** Where a new scientific breakthrough is the competitive advantage, the book says the risks of an early release need more careful balancing, and it tells entrepreneurs in every case to seek legal counsel (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 110-111).
- **Brand.** An MVP that would damage a brand is to be launched under a different name, and the book notes that long-term reputation is mostly at risk where a company has already generated hype (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 111-112).
- **Visionaries' fear is granted as wisdom.** The fear that customers will reject a flawed MVP that is too small or too limited is called a false negative worth taking seriously, and the author writes that there is wisdom in it (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 112).
- **The pivot decision.** There is no rigid clinical formula, the human element of vision, intuition and judgment cannot be removed, and the author says removing it would not be desirable (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 145). The purpose of learning milestones is stated as putting relevant data in the room rather than making the decision easy (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 150).
- **A pivot does not discard everything.** The Wealthfront case is used to make the point that it is not necessary to throw out what came before, and that the manager-evaluation technology became the kernel of the new business (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 166).
- **Pivoting into a famous company's strategy.** The book warns against putting too much stock in such analogies, because it is extremely difficult to know whether the essential features or only the superficial ones have been copied, and whether what worked in that industry or that era transfers (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 173-174).
- **Meeting cadence.** The pivot-or-persevere meeting has no fixed interval; less than a few weeks is called too often and more than a few months too infrequent, and each startup is told to find its own pace (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 162).
- **Small batches in product design.** The book concedes that not every type of product, as it exists today, allows design change in small batches, and that significant work may be needed first, which it assigns to senior management rather than to the innovation team (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF p. 189). The continuous-deployment example carries its own limit: the lesson is named as reducing batch size, not as shipping fifty times a day (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF p. 186).
- **Pull is not customer wants.** The book names as a misunderstanding the reading that customers pull product development, because customers often do not know what they want; what pulls work is the hypothesis, expressed as an experiment (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF p. 195).
- **Engines of growth.** More than one engine can technically operate at once, and a product can have both fast viral growth and low churn; the recommendation to focus on one is offered on the grounds of operational complexity, not of impossibility (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 211).
- **Viral products and price.** Charging is described as friction the viral loop cannot afford, so the value hypothesis for such a product has to be tested by a nonmonetary exchange, and the author says it would have been foolish for Facebook or Hotmail to charge early (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 207).
- **Value rather than profit.** The book uses the language of value creation deliberately rather than profit, to include not-for-profit ventures, public-sector startups and internal change agents, and notes that some wildly profitable organizations are value-destroying (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF p. 88). The same allowance is made for incentives: a personal stake need not be financial (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF p. 246).
- **Gross numbers in the early days.** IMVU's hockey stick is reported as running only to about $8,000 a month, and the author says investors asked whether the units were thousands; the claim is that the numbers were significant as predictions, not as revenue (Ch. 6 Test, PDF pp. 95-96).
- **Five Whys preconditions.** The full method requires an environment of mutual trust and empowerment, and where that is missing the book offers a simplified two-rule version while stating that the simplification does not work effectively over the long term (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 228-229). Executive sponsorship is called necessary, individual contributors are cautioned against starting without their manager's buy-in, and the advice is to begin with a narrow, low-stakes symptom rather than the area wasting the most time (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 229-230).
- **Structure is a prerequisite, not a guarantee.** The three attributes an internal startup team needs are said to make success possible rather than likely, while getting the structure wrong is said to lead to almost certain failure (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF p. 244).
- **The framework is not a blueprint.** Those who adopt the method as a defined set of steps or tactics are said not to succeed, because distinguishing a special cause from a systemic one requires theory, and the closing instruction is to adapt rather than copy (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF pp. 259-260).
- **The transition costs productivity.** Switching a team's measure of productivity from functional excellence to validated learning is said to cause problems and to feel worse before it feels better, and the author reports raising this from day one in his consulting practice to stop it derailing the effort later (Ch. 12 Innovate, PDF p. 260).
- **The movement's own risk.** The epilogue warns against a new pseudoscience forming around pivots and MVPs, names doctrine and rigid ideology as things the movement must avoid, and says what is known so far is the tip of the iceberg (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF pp. 267-268, 271).
- **The waste estimate is anecdotal.** The author states that it is hard to come by a solid estimate of how wasteful modern work is and that there is no shortage of anecdotes, which is what he offers (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF p. 263).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each company, figure or tool the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the claim.

- **Aardvark, the Wizard of Oz MVP, acquired by Google for a reported $50 million (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 107).** The acquisition holds and the product does not: Google bought Aardvark on February 11, 2010 for $50 million and announced its discontinuation in September 2011, the same year the book was published (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aardvark_(search_engine)).
- **Grockit, the actionable-metrics case, with close to a million students helped and more than twenty employees (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF p. 140).** Gone. Kaplan bought the social learning platform in 2013 and the company went all in on Learnist; stand-alone operation ceased in September 2016 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grockit).
- **Votizen and @2gov, the pivot case, reported as doing well two years in with $1.5 million raised from Peter Thiel (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 157-158).** Absorbed and then absorbed again: Votizen was acquired by Causes on January 10, 2013, and Causes by Brigade Media in June 2014 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Votizen). The Startup Visa Act (S.565) named on the same page was never enacted, and no later status for David Binetti was found, so both are unchecked.
- **Path, the courage-to-launch case, with a fifty-connection limit from Dunbar's research and $8.5 million raised after turning down $100 million from Google (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 160-161).** The funding and the Google offer hold; the constraint and the company do not. Path raised the limit to 150, then removed it, was acquired by Kakao on May 28, 2015, was fined $800,000 by the Federal Trade Commission in 2013 for collecting data from minors, and shut its service on October 18, 2018 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_(social_network)). The research behind the number is also contested: Lindenfors, Wartel and Lind, "'Dunbar's number' deconstructed", *Biology Letters*, 2021, reran the analysis on updated datasets and concluded that a cognitive limit on human group size cannot be derived that way.
- **Wealthfront, reported at more than $180 million on the platform and more than forty professional managers (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 166).** Far larger now and on a different model: roughly $95 billion across 1.4 million clients as of May 2026, after a $1.4 billion UBS acquisition agreed in January 2022 and mutually terminated that September, and an initial public offering in December 2025 at $14 a share (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wealthfront).
- **Dropbox, reported at the time of writing as rumored to be worth more than $1 billion (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 100).** Holds and grew: valued at $10 billion in 2014, public since March 23, 2018, and reporting $2.52 billion of revenue in 2025 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dropbox). The specific video-MVP numbers, a beta waiting list going from 5,000 to 75,000 overnight, rest on Drew Houston's own account to the author and are unchecked.
- **Groupon, on pace to be the fastest company in history to $1 billion in sales (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 95).** The claim held and the trajectory did not: Groupon reached that pace in roughly sixteen months and went public on November 4, 2011 in the largest internet offering since Google, then reported $515 million of revenue for 2023 against an operating loss of $18 million and negative total equity (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupon).
- **Zappos acquired by Amazon for a reported $1.2 billion in 2009 (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF p. 66).** Holds: announced July 22, 2009 at about $940 million in stock and cash and closing in November 2009 at a reported $1.2 billion as Amazon's share price rose (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zappos). The origin story the chapter rests on, Nick Swinmurn photographing local stores' inventory and buying the shoes at full price, appears in no first-party source that could be reached and traces to the founder's own retelling; it is unchecked.
- **Kodak Gallery, whose event-album prototype is the Ch. 4 case (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF pp. 71-73).** Gone. Shutterfly bid $23.8 million for it during Kodak's bankruptcy on March 1, 2012, and the service closed on July 2, 2012 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kodak_Gallery), so the product whose iteration the chapter describes outlived the book by less than a year.
- **Alphabet Energy, having raised about $1 million against SunPower's factories and BrightSource's $291 million (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 196-197).** The frugality did not last and the company did not: it raised more than $35 million in total, including $23.5 million led by Schlumberger in 2016, and ceased operation on August 1, 2018, sold to Synergy Fuels (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet_Energy).
- **Hotmail's viral loop, sold to Microsoft at 12 million subscribers for $400 million (Ch. 10 Grow, PDF p. 205).** The price and date hold, December 1997 at a reported $400 million; the brand does not. It became Windows Live Hotmail in 2007, reverted in 2011, and was fully replaced by Outlook in May 2013, with 400 million active users migrated (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outlook.com).
- **The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau as a $500 million startup agency building a call center (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF pp. 75-78).** The agency exists and its footing has changed sharply: its director was dismissed on February 1, 2025, work was ordered stopped that month, an administration effort to cut close to 90 percent of staff was reported in April 2025 and frozen by a federal judge, and legislation signed on July 4, 2025 cut its funding cap from 12 percent to 6.5 percent of Federal Reserve operating expenses (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_Financial_Protection_Bureau). No source was found on the complaint call center itself, so the specific mechanism the chapter discusses is unchecked.
- **School of One's daily playlists (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 189-190).** Renamed and much smaller than the book's framing implies: it became Teach to One, run by the nonprofit New Classrooms Innovation Partners founded in 2011, and the encyclopedia entry carries a stale-content warning dated February 2022 listing roughly 1,500 students across three schools (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_One). The Time magazine recognition the book cites is for 2009 innovations, and the growth figures reported there, about 1.2 years of math growth in 2012-13 and gains at 1.5 times the national average, postdate the book and are not the numbers it used.
- **The Long-Term Stock Exchange, proposed in the epilogue (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF pp. 270-271).** Built, and much narrower than the proposal: the Securities and Exchange Commission approved it in May 2019 as the fourteenth national securities exchange, trading began in September 2020, and its listings are dual listings — Twilio and Asana in 2021, ThredUp in 2023 — rather than a market of companies organized for long-term thinking. Bill Harts became chief executive in 2024, and in 2025 the exchange petitioned the Commission to allow semiannual rather than quarterly reporting for all United States public companies (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-Term_Stock_Exchange).
- **Net Promoter Score as the source of actionable customer data at Intuit and IMVU (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 237-238).** Contested before the book was written and since: Keiningham and colleagues, "A Longitudinal Examination of Net Promoter and Firm Revenue Growth", *Journal of Marketing* 71(3), 2007, questioned whether the score predicts growth at all, and Hayes, *Quality Progress* 41(6), 2008, found no evidence that the recommendation question beats other loyalty questions; a 2025 interview has Fred Reichheld himself acknowledging overuse (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_Promoter_Score).
- **Toyota as the world's largest automaker in 2008 (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF p. 181).** Holds, with an interruption: Toyota reclaimed the position from Volkswagen by 2020 and held it for a third consecutive year in 2022 (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota).
- **The movement's own resources: theleanstartup.com, the meetups described as over a hundred groups, the Lean Startup Circle and the Startup Lessons Learned conference (Introduction, PDF p. 21; Ch. 14, PDF pp. 273-274).** The site is live and still points at meetups and the Circle, but the meetup figure is smaller than the book's, 94 cities across 17 countries, and the site now leads with an online course, training, coaching and consulting through Lean Startup Co. The Startup Lessons Learned conference is not mentioned on it, so the conference's status is unchecked (theleanstartup.com).
- **Intuit's SnapTax, more than 350,000 downloads in three weeks (Ch. 2 Define, PDF p. 40).** Unchecked. The encyclopedia entry for TurboTax does not mention SnapTax at all, so neither the download figure nor the product's fate could be confirmed against a source that could be reached.
- **Not checked:** IMVU's own figures — 60 million avatars, more than $50 million of 2011 revenue, more than $25 million in 2009, about fifty product changes a day (Introduction, PDF p. 21; Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF p. 170; Ch. 9 Batch, PDF p. 184) — of which only the 2014 active-player count of about six million was available (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMVU); Food on the Table's concierge story and its $9.95 weekly check (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 102); Village Laundry Service's 116,000 kg in 2010 against 30,600 kg in 2009 (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF p. 75); SGW Designworks' three-day prototype and forty-unit run (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF pp. 187-189); QuickBooks' twenty-point Net Promoter Score drop and the year-three sales result (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 238, 242); Intuit's five hundred TurboTax tests per season and its two innovation measures (Ch. 2 Define, PDF pp. 43-45); the 2004 Sienna's 60 percent sales rise (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF p. 90); Votizen's percentage tables (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 147-156); the 15 percent decade rise in United States manufacturing output sourced to a FiveThirtyEight post (Ch. 1 Start, PDF p. 27); and the Duryea count of 501 automobile companies formed between 1900 and 1908 (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF p. 87). Each is either a private figure given to the author by a company he discloses a relationship with, or a link that could not be resolved to a source that states it.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **The evidence base is almost entirely the author's own portfolio, and the book says so only at the back.** The Disclosures page names a relationship or equity interest in Aardvark, Dropbox, Food on the Table, Grockit, IMVU, Intuit, Votizen and Wealthfront (Disclosures, PDF pp. 291-293), which is very nearly the complete list of extended cases in the book, plus further interests through 500 Startups, Kleiner Perkins Caufield and Byers, Floodgate, Greylock Partners and Seraph Group. None of these interests is disclosed at the point of use. The sharpness of this is the Introduction's own: it rejects the entrepreneurial success story as "selection bias and after-the-fact rationalization" (Introduction, PDF p. 17) while assembling its case from companies the author selected, advised, or invested in, and the counter-examples — portfolio companies that ran the method and failed — do not appear.
- **Correlation presented as cause.** Intuit's shift to five hundred experiments per tax season and its $50 million of revenue from offerings that did not exist twelve months earlier are placed side by side with no control and no other explanation considered (Ch. 2 Define, PDF pp. 43-45). QuickBooks' third year is credited with higher customer satisfaction ratings and more units sold, in a year that changed the team structure, the process, the technology and the planning cycle at once (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF p. 242). The Five Whys is credited with producing IMVU's training program, in a passage that also reports the program evolved rather than being decided (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF pp. 219, 225). The chapter on quality reports IMVU's teleporting avatars ranking among customers' top three features without stating how many customers were asked (Ch. 6 Test, PDF p. 109).
- **Figures without a source.** The premise that most startups fail, most new products are unsuccessful and most ventures fall short of their potential carries no citation (Introduction, PDF p. 17). IMVU's fifty changes a day is given as an average with no measurement behind it (Ch. 9 Batch, PDF p. 184). Facebook's early engagement is the clearest case, because the book's own endnote concedes that 2004 numbers are hard to find and then supports the claim with a 2005 statement from Chris Hughes about a later period (Ch. 5 Leap, PDF p. 83; Endnotes, PDF p. 283).
- **The theory is difficult to falsify on its own terms.** Failure after following the method is explained as pivoting too late, as an unclear hypothesis that made complete failure impossible to experience, or as vanity metrics that hid the need to change (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 159-160), and IMVU's own failure to pivot is diagnosed the same way after the fact (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 167-168). The epilogue asks for exactly the research that would test the claims — startup testing labs comparing methodologies against calibrated problems (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF pp. 269-270) — which concedes that no such comparison stands behind the book.
- **Examples that no longer exist.** Section 7 has the detail: Aardvark was shut by its acquirer the year the book appeared, Kodak Gallery closed within a year of publication, and Grockit, Votizen, Path, Food on the Table and Alphabet Energy have all since been absorbed or wound up. The cases where the method is shown working end to end are disproportionately the ones that ended.
- **The quality argument reverses inside the book and the reversal is not carried back.** The low-quality MVP is the rule in Ch. 6, and Ch. 8 reports that the very actions which worked on IMVU's early adopters were diametrically opposed to what mainstream customers needed, costing a major redesign and months of a cross-functional team's work (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 168-170). Ch. 11 restates the same finding as a general principle about quality shortcuts slowing a company later (Ch. 11 Adapt, PDF p. 221). The rule in Ch. 6 is not amended in light of either.
- **The measurement machinery presumes a volume of strangers.** The instruments the book recommends — cohort analysis, split tests, the viral coefficient, the three A's — are demonstrated on products taking a hundred fresh strangers a day for five dollars of AdWords (Ch. 7 Measure, PDF p. 120), and Votizen's percentage tables only become readable once cohorts are large enough to divide (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 147-149). Where the numbers are small the book's own remedy is qualitative — Wealthfront's pivot turned on conversations after fourteen signups (Ch. 8 Pivot, PDF pp. 164-165) — and no method for innovation accounting at that scale is given.
- **The non-software cases are thin or hypothetical.** The Hewlett-Packard volunteering section is explicitly the author's own analysis of what an experiment might look like, not a report of one that happened (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF pp. 68-70), and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau section is his suggestion to a chief technology officer, with the agency's actual choice reported in a paragraph (Ch. 4 Experiment, PDF pp. 76-78). Village Laundry Service, SGW Designworks and Alphabet Energy are each reported at one remove, through an interview or a colleague's research credited in the endnotes (Endnotes, PDF pp. 282, 289).
- **The definition is stretched wider than the claims it carries.** A startup is defined to include a government agency with a $500 million budget, a Fortune 1000 division, and a person in a garage (Ch. 2 Define, PDF pp. 37-39), while the book's motivating statistic, that most startups fail, comes from the ordinary narrow sense of the word (Introduction, PDF p. 17). The prescriptions and the failure rate are not about the same population.
- **The cure for the method's misuse is the method's own vocabulary.** The epilogue's defense against a Lean Startup pseudoscience is to insist that learning be turned into validated learning in the next cycle and that assumptions be stated and tested (Ch. 13 Epilogue, PDF pp. 267, 271), which is the same instrument being checked, and the book offers no outside test a reader could apply to tell a genuine validation from a well-told one.
