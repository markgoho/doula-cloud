# Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding, read back

## 1. Source

*Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding: How to Market Your Startup, Grow Your Company, and Think Like an Entrepreneur*, Rob Walling, revised edition, 2023 (first edition 2011). Self-published; the copyright page names no publisher and no ISBN, and points to RobWalling.com. The book is a collection of 44 essays first published on the author's blog between 2006 and 2019, each carrying a footnote with its original URL and date; the 2023 introduction says the revision updated "much of the text" and added essays published after 2011 (Introduction, pp. 6–7). Read 2026-09-10 from the `pdftotext -layout` extraction of the 252-page PDF, front to back.

**Page rule.** The book prints page numbers, and this file cites the printed page. Printed page = PDF index, offset zero, found by opening PDF page 9, which prints "9", PDF page 60, which prints "60", and PDF page 252, which prints "252". The front matter (title, copyright, contents) occupies PDF pages 1–5 and carries no printed number; printed numbering begins at 6 on the Introduction. The book has no numbered chapters. It has an Introduction and five parts (Startup Marketing, The Entrepreneurial Mindset, Running Your Company, Recruiting & Retaining Developers, Entrepreneurship) plus an Extras section, each part holding titled essays. The essays' footnote numbers are not usable as chapter numbers (footnote 36 is a content note, and the third part of the software-acquisition story carries no footnote), so a citation names the essay and the page, and the part is given at each section heading below. A citation reads ("Why Free Plans Don't Work", p. 38).

## 2. What this file is for

This file is the evidence: what the book argues, essay by essay, with its own exceptions, what has changed since the essays were written, and where it is weak. It records no Doula Cloud decision; where the book touches something the repo has already decided, section 6 names the ADR or ticket, and the decision belongs to that record.

## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that for a self-funded software founder marketing is not a task that follows the product but a "fundamental building block" of it, as necessary as saving code to disk, and so it begins on the day the founder decides to go ahead with an idea ("Why You Should Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding", p. 58). The ordering he gives is market first, marketing second, aesthetic third, and functionality "a distant fourth" ("Why 'Luck' is a Terrible Marketing Plan for Your Startup", p. 30), and the concrete form of pre-launch marketing is a landing page and an email list built months before launch, because the goal is "permission to contact" the people who are interested (p. 58). The rest of the essays, on conversion before traffic, on pricing, on free plans, on the founder's psychology, on hiring developers, and on buying products rather than building them, are the author's blog posts from 2006 to 2019 arranged under that thesis, with his own products (DotNetInvoice, HitTail, Drip) and the MicroConf community as the evidence.

The author says what the claim is not. It is not for a hobby project, which he says needs no marketing at all (p. 58). It is not a plan for a venture-funded "hot idea" startup, whose odds he puts at "1 in 10,000 if not worse" and which he sets aside as a lottery ticket ("Why 'Luck'", p. 32). It is not a claim that marketing substitutes for a market: garbage sells "to a hungry market", he says, until a competitor arrives to "eat your lunch" (p. 31). It is not advice for large or funded companies, whom he sends to the business books he otherwise tells founders to stop reading ("Why Startup Founders Should Stop Reading Business Books", pp. 111–112). And the book itself disclaims currency: it is a 2023 revision of 2011 selections from a blog, with essays the author judged to be as relevant as on the day they were written (Introduction, p. 6).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction (pp. 6–7)

The author published 383 posts between 2005 and about 2011, collected the best in 2011, and in 2023 set out to "breathe new life" into the book by updating text and adding later essays (Introduction, pp. 6–7). The essays are dated by their footnotes; the book does not say which ones were updated or how.

### Part 1: Startup Marketing (pp. 8–84)

**Losing People Through the Bottom of Your Funnel (pp. 9–12, 2009).** A recurring product has a third funnel step beyond traffic and conversion: retaining existing customers (p. 9). In the "first 1-2 months after launch" a founder has to "crank through the initial feature requests" or customers cancel (p. 11). At a 1% conversion rate, one lost customer equals a hundred visitors who never arrived (p. 11). The author's order of work: stop the leak first, then conversion, then traffic (p. 11).

**Why Focusing on Traffic Can Kill Your Startup (pp. 13–17, 2009).** Founders name traffic sources and go blank on conversion, because good marketing "looks easy", because there are fifty books on traffic for every one on conversion, and because traffic is fun and A/B tests are slow (pp. 13–15). The author's figures: un-optimized sales sites convert at "0.1% to 0.5%", optimized ones at "0.7% and 4%", and doubling conversion "is far cheaper" than doubling traffic (pp. 16). He stops traffic work on a site that is not converting until A/B testing has improved it, and has seen conversions rise "up to 10x" that way (pp. 16–17). Google Website Optimizer is the tool he recommends (p. 15; also "Losing People", p. 10).

**The Nine Levels of Traffic Quality (pp. 18–21, 2010).** Quality is closeness to the ideal customer plus an existing relationship (p. 18). A mailing list converts up to 10x, and at worst 2–3x, better than website traffic, and MicroConf sign-ups vary 200% by source (p. 19). Bidsketch's launch write-ups on startup blogs brought traffic that "stayed for less than five seconds" for two-thirds of visitors (pp. 19–20). The ranked list: mailing list, own blog, targeted referral with a write-up, direct, brand search, untargeted referral, other organic search, AdWords, banners (pp. 20–21). Google Analytics goals are how to see which sources convert (p. 19).

**The #1 Goal of Your Website (pp. 22–29, 2011).** Above an "impulse" price of about $20, most people do not buy on a first visit, and the five reasons they do not (information, trust, budget, timing, never) are moved by repeated contact (pp. 22–23). Returning visitors bought 450% more at DotNetInvoice, 770% at JustBeachTowels, 400% at AWPCP and 1585% at CrazyEgg, while "80% or more" of visitors are first-time; the author says this is a rule of thumb, "of course not" proof (pp. 24–25). So the goal is to get a first-time visitor to come back, and email beats RSS and social for that (pp. 25–26). Three steps: a landing page with the sign-up "at the forefront", a giveaway such as an 8–12 page PDF, and an automated follow-up sequence, for which he recommends MailChimp with its free tier to 1,000 subscribers (pp. 26–27). Two split tests are the evidence: Collectorz.com's four-email sequence raised sales 3.4% and profit 15.4% while cutting downloads 33.6%, and SolarAccounts's two emails raised sales 18% (pp. 28–29). Asked whether the two tests prove the point, the author answers "Nope" (p. 29).

**Why "Luck" is a Terrible Marketing Plan for Your Startup (pp. 30–34, 2009).** The single most important factor is whether a group of people is "willing to pay" (p. 30). A product with a sizable market and low competition wins with bad marketing and bad code, QuickBooks, Palm and IE5.5 being the examples (p. 31). Viral luck happens to "maybe 1 out of 10,000 startups", against "maybe a 1 in 100 chance" on the self-funded path (pp. 31, 33). Examples of the latter: Joel Spolsky, 37signals, Stephane Grenier (p. 33).

**Why Free Plans Don't Work (pp. 35–42, 2010, guest essay by Ruben Gamez).** Bidsketch launched with a free plan on the 37signals model; after the launch list was exhausted only 1% of new users chose the paid option and 0.8% of free accounts ever upgraded (pp. 35–37). Removing the free plan from the pricing page produced "an 8x increase in paid conversions" in a week and 10x over the month (pp. 38–39). Supporting cases: 37signals hid its free plan and Jason Fried said "ask for money upfront"; Pandora converted under 1%, later 1.7%; Evernote 0.5%, 2% after a year; MailChimp reported a 354% rise in abuse issues; CrazyEgg dropped its free plan in January 2009 and doubled revenue that month; LessAccounting drops a trial to free only without payment details; Pluggio at 2.5% paid was considering the same cut (pp. 39–41). Wufoo, MailChimp and FreshBooks made freemium work, but the essay's point is that "we're not them" (p. 42).

**Why Making Something Customers Want Isn't Enough (pp. 43–48, 2011).** Paul Graham's three requirements get a fourth: customers have to pay more than it costs to acquire them (p. 43). The worked AdWords case: $4 a click on "invoicing software" at 1% conversion is a $400 cost per acquisition, $800 at 0.5% (p. 44). LTV = price ÷ churn, with early churn "in the 5-10% range", so $19 a month at 8% churn is $237.50 (p. 45). Facebook clicks at $0.10–0.40 give a CPA of $40–80; SEO pays out over time; telemarketing at $15–20 an hour closing one in a hundred calls gives $75–100, with the note that he has never used one (pp. 46–47). Word of mouth is free, and HostGator's $125 referral bounty shows a company that knows its LTV (p. 47). The advice is to estimate LTV and CPA and try the marketing "before writing a single line of code" (p. 48).

**Your Market is Smaller Than You Think (pp. 49–55, 2010).** Top-down sizing suits pitch decks; a bootstrapped founder reaches "a single-digit percentage" of a market and sizes bottom-up from the AdWords Keyword Tool (pp. 49–50). Four mistakes inflate the count: not logging in, broad match instead of exact, global instead of local, and reading the tool's number as visitors, where a #1 ranking yields "40 – 60%" of it (pp. 51–55). The worked example falls from 27,100 to 3,300 (pp. 52, 55).

**Why You Should Start Marketing the Day You Start Coding (pp. 56–64, 2010).** Two objections are dismissed: nobody will steal the idea, and a theft before launch is "a favor", since "technical execution is mostly trivial" and marketing execution is what wins; and being too busy coding is like being too busy to use source control (pp. 57–58). Four reasons: idea validation from a landing page built in "2-4 hours", with 650 addresses as reassurance and several thousand visitors with six sign-ups as a stop signal (pp. 59–60); an instant beta list, with the advice to keep a beta to "5 – 20 people" and to discount or waive the price only for those who contribute (p. 60); launch day, where a list of 650 converts at "between 5 and 40%", so 32 to 260 sales, against three sales at $20 without one (pp. 60–61); and links accrued over time (p. 61). Execution: a domain, a short landing page that piques interest rather than sells, and email collection, in 2–4 hours (pp. 61–62). Three page approaches: WordPress with the LaunchPad theme, which converted "just under 50%" for his book; static HTML, with the warning not to design it yourself unless you are a designer, since a bad page converts at 5–10% and a good one at 30–50%; and Unbounce at $25 a month (pp. 62–64).

**How I Created 4 Startup Explainer Videos for $11 (pp. 65–72, 2015).** Drip had four customer types and agency videos cost about $2,000 each, so the author scripted, recorded in Audacity, cut out stock drawings, filmed them on butcher paper, and edited in iMovie, for $11 and about eight hours (pp. 66–71). Scripts run under 90 seconds; a funded startup "should probably just hire it out" (pp. 66–67). About 25% of home-page visitors watched one (p. 72).

**How to Find Your 4-Second Startup Pitch (pp. 73–75, 2010, excerpted from *Start Small, Stay Small*).** The "hook" is the home-page headline, five to seven words, distinct from a USP or elevator pitch; examples are "Save Time. Get Paid Faster." and "Simple Proposal Software Made for Designers" (pp. 73–74). Three ways to find one: what it does and for whom, a promise, or the single most remarkable feature (p. 74).

**How to Compete Against Open-Source Competition (pp. 76–79, 2009).** Open-source software is free only "if your time is worth nothing" (p. 77). Of some 300 open-source projects the author touched, 80% cost more time than commercial equivalents; the time sinks are installation, documentation, support and usability (p. 78). The response is to save users time, market hard, and build for business users, and never to fight a price war (p. 79).

**Expenses You Don't Think of When Starting a Startup (pp. 80–84, 2009).** A reply to readers who said $2,500 a month was enough to quit: filing fees (about $100 a year for a sole proprietorship, hundreds to thousands for an LLC), an accountant at $300–1,000 a year, lawyers at $250 an hour and up, E&O insurance at $1,500–2,500 a year, health insurance "around $800/month" for a family of three, disability insurance at $250–500 a month, term life, the employer half of FICA at 7.65%, and 10% to retirement; then hosting, FogBugz at $25 a user, AdWords at $100–600 a month, paid graphic design, phone, fax, printer and computer (pp. 81–84).

### Part 2: The Entrepreneurial Mindset (pp. 85–130)

**Five Reasons You Haven't Launched (pp. 86–90, 2010).** Give idea selection a month, then "close up shop" (pp. 86–87); doing everything yourself, where outsourcing five of fifteen weekly hours "makes you 33% more productive" (p. 87); distractions, including business books, "the entrepreneur's Kryptonite" (p. 88); adding features nobody asked for, with the test that a founder not working directly with at least two customers has "no idea" whether the work adds value (p. 88); and fear, "the #1 reason", which disguises itself as work (p. 89). The bonus reason is a launch nobody noticed, because a TechCrunch spike decays from 10,000 uniques to 300 and only "un-sexy" SEO, an audience and a mailing list last (pp. 89–90).

**How to Force Yourself to Ship (Even When It's Hard) (pp. 91–95, 2016).** Drip's Workflows shipped because the date was announced in public two weeks ahead, and became the "biggest growth accelerator" of the prior 18 months (pp. 91–92). Ship weekly early on to build the "shipping muscle" (p. 92); an ongoing public commitment such as a weekly podcast works, the author's own since 2010 (pp. 93–94). The caveat is deadline fatigue: commit to a hard deadline "a few times per year", not constantly (p. 95).

**It's Easy to Be Great...It's Hard to Be Consistent (pp. 96–100, 2010).** From Steve Martin's memoir: ten appearances on The Tonight Show before anyone recognizes you, so there is no "big break" and no TechCrunch link makes a business (pp. 96–97). "The Madness" of a new idea fades, and most quit at the drudgery of ongoing marketing (pp. 97–99). Remedies: spend no money on an idea in its first 48 hours, and an accountability group of three to five founders meeting every one or two weeks (pp. 99–100).

**Lesser Known Traits of Successful Founders (pp. 101–105, 2010).** From "several hundred" founders: speed from idea to decision, flexibility about the product and even the business model, confidence in one's ability to execute (with Branden's *Six Pillars of Self-Esteem* recommended), and focus long enough to deliver, again with the 48-hour rule (pp. 101–105).

**The Terror of Firsts (pp. 106–108).** Every first is frightening and the fear "wears off pretty quickly"; the fear is the sign of growth, and "99.9% of people will never overcome" it (pp. 106–108).

**Why Startup Founders Should Stop Reading Business Books (pp. 109–115, 2010).** Gladwell and Godin books reduce to "a page or two" and are too high-level to act on; case-study books such as *Business Model Generation* and *Good to Great* address markets "measured with 8 zeros or more"; and information not usable in the next one or two months is "the new television" (pp. 109–112). Read reviews instead, and read narrowly in the chosen genre; four books are named (*Web Copy that Sells*, *Always Be Testing*, *Web Analytics An Hour a Day*, *Web Design for ROI*); first-hand experience and other founders teach more (pp. 113–115).

**One of the Most Time-Consuming Startup Roadblocks (pp. 116–120, 2009).** "Drip outsourcing" of small tasks to a $6-an-hour virtual assistant and $15-an-hour contractors saves the author "60-100 hours a month" (pp. 116–117). A list of 37 tasks from idea to launch week, asterisked where easiest to outsource: graphic design, HTML/CSS, unit tests, sales-site copy, the press release; not architecture, niche selection or the email list (pp. 118–119). Start with small non-critical tasks (p. 119).

**Don't Plan to Get Rich from Your Startup (pp. 121–122, 2010).** Dharmesh Shah's line that a first startup maximizes the odds of "a modest outcome", and a niche market makes that "10x...perhaps 100x" likelier (pp. 121–122).

**How to Avoid the Three Startup Danger Points (pp. 123–126, 2010).** Founders quit at idea choice, at two months into development, and one month after launch (p. 123). For the second, a full task list of 80–120 lines should total 400–600 hours, and over 600 "you need to cut something" (p. 124). For the third, the site's first goal is to bring people back, an email list gives "your best sales day ever", AdWords and SEO take "4-6 or more" months, and a first six months of $6,000 is "more than the vast majority of launches" (pp. 125–126).

**The Software Product Myth (pp. 127–130, 2008).** The developer who ships a $25 product selling 100 copies a month ends up with $2,500, a day job, a growing support load, and legacy code, and most products wither there; the upside is on the far side of "a big dip" (pp. 128–130).

### Part 3: Running Your Company (pp. 131–152)

**From 160 Hours to 10: A Tale of Agile Business Practices (pp. 132–135, 2010).** A hosted DotNetInvoice was estimated at 160 hours until the team dropped multi-tenancy, dropped automated provisioning in favor of the author creating accounts by hand, and used PayPal subscriptions instead of a purchase page, landing at about 10 hours (pp. 132–134). The rule attached: decide in advance at what point automation becomes worth the time, or the manual process becomes permanent (p. 135). The best code, he says, is the code "you never had to write" (p. 135).

**How to Detect a Toxic Customer (pp. 136–141, 2010).** Five warning signs in a sales exchange: disrespect, a discount request with no reason, multiple contacts through multiple channels, unrealistic expectations, and questions the website already answers; two or three together are a red flag (pp. 138–140). The author turned down an 80-question prospect and the prospect's colleague bought within a week (pp. 140–141).

**Why You Should Build Your Startup to Sell (But Not to Flip) (pp. 142–145, 2010).** From Warrillow's *Built to Sell*: a scalable business is repeatable, teachable and valuable; for a startup that means a sales process that runs on the website with no manual step, a written process for everything, a vertical niche, with occupations and hobbies named as the best kind, and charging up front (pp. 143–145). The punchline he draws is simply to charge for the product at all (p. 145).

**You Can't Make Money Charging $1 Per Month (pp. 146–148, 2011).** A reader pricing a teachers' product at $1 a month is told $5–7 minimum; the arithmetic is 2,000 customers for $2,000 a month, 200,000 uniques at 1%, no budget for ads, and the observation that cheaper products carry higher churn, more support, and "more price-sensitive customers" (pp. 146–147). "Aspirational Pricing": when asked to lower the price, ask what to build to be worth it (p. 147). $50–100 a month "is such a better way to go", and if stuck at $5–7, charge annually (p. 148).

**Four Things I Learned By Asking My Customers (pp. 149–152, 2011).** Sean Ellis's PMF Survey sent to DotNetInvoice customers: 85% said they found it through Google where Google Analytics's last click credited search with 37% (p. 150); 48% would be "very disappointed" to lose it, above Ellis's 40% bar (p. 151); 26% would write their own and 22% would use nothing, so Word and Excel were competitors nobody had named (p. 151); and the primary benefits customers named, automated reminders and simplicity, were not the ones marketed (p. 152).

### Part 4: Recruiting & Retaining Developers (pp. 153–178)

**How to Recruit a Developer Entrepreneur for Your Startup (pp. 154–157, 2008).** Code, marketing and money: a founder needs "at least two of them", and an idea is not one of the three (p. 155). Court a developer as an investor of time, with research and numbers; avoid bank IT departments and Craigslist; break into the local network, as the author did in New Haven through Higher One's founder (pp. 156–157).

**How to Attract Software Developers that Fit Your Company (pp. 158–162, 2007).** Three dimensions, size, chaos level and focus, define a "developer demographic"; write the job description for the person who will stay (pp. 159–162). IndyMac and Countrywide are the examples of stable employers (p. 158).

**Personality Traits of the Best Software Developers (pp. 163–168, 2006).** Short-term pessimism with long-term confidence (the Stockdale paradox), anger at sloppy code (with NASA's shuttle software as the standard), long-term life planning, and attention to detail (pp. 164–167). Glass's *Facts and Fallacies* is cited for the best programmers being "up to 28 times" better than the worst (p. 167).

**Nine Things Developers Want More Than Money (pp. 169–178, 2006).** Herzberg's hygiene and motivation factors; nine motivators including being set up to succeed, excellent management, learning, the right problems, a voice, recognition, work that matters, no act of Congress, and few legacy constraints (pp. 170–177). Google, he guesses, would score "an 8 or a 9" (p. 178).

### Part 5: Entrepreneurship (pp. 179–245)

**The Stair Step Method of Bootstrapping (pp. 180–187, 2015).** Step 1 is a simple product on one organic channel (a WordPress plugin, a Shopify app); step 2 repeats it until the founder owns their time; step 3 is standalone recurring revenue (pp. 182–186). Dave Rodenbaugh, Phil Derksen and the author's own DotNetInvoice, job board and beach-towel store are the examples (pp. 184–185). Low-LTV products "just can't make paid advertising work", and only a higher LTV ($150+) opens PPC, content and integration marketing (pp. 185–186). The author doubts HitTail or Drip would have "taken off" as his first projects (p. 187).

**Should You Build or Buy Your Startup? (pp. 188–194, 2008).** Building a 1.0 costs the author at least $40,000 in lost consulting; hiring it out runs $16,000–27,000 in the US; buying DotNetInvoice cost "about 20%" of building it (pp. 189–192). Product build is "around 50%" of the time it takes to launch, and the single most important success factor is marketing and sales, "not the software itself" (pp. 190–191).

**The Inside Story of a Small Software Acquisition, Parts 1–3 (pp. 195–207, 2007).** Leverage is people or products (p. 197). The author bought DotNetInvoice after a month of email, then found 30 angry customers, 23 major bugs and an unsupported purchase tier, and the sales he had valued were a pre-purchase spike, so "I overpaid" (pp. 200–205). Seven months later revenue was "nearly triple" (p. 205). Advice: install and break the product, email five customers, take six to twelve months of revenue, and expect "between 12 and 30 months of revenue" as the price (pp. 205–206).

**The Inside Story of a Small Startup Acquisition, Parts 1–3 (pp. 208–228, 2012).** HitTail, bought August 2011 for less than the price of "a new car" after the seller countered at 5x and settled at 1.5x the first offer plus legal fees (pp. 208, 211, 225). Priorities: stabilization, then plugging the funnel, then spreading the word (pp. 223–224). The funnel leaks found: 60-day trials that never ended, "exactly zero emails" during a trial, and content pages with no path back to the product (p. 213). Fixes included trial expiry at 30 days and trial emails, which lifted the share of trials installing the code "from 25% to around 75%", a sign-up form cut from 15 fields to 6, and asking for a card up front (pp. 224, 228). "Rob's Theory of Time to 1.0" is 4–6 months and 300–600 hours, "time to flywheel" is 6–12 months after launch, and market risk is "the deadliest of the three" risks (pp. 217, 219). Due diligence asked for eight numbers, most unverifiable beyond Analytics screenshots (p. 222); the offer was 18–24 months of net profit against his usual 8–14 (p. 225).

**How a Seemingly Well-Planned Server Move Crashed, Burned, and Rose from the Ashes (pp. 229–233, 2012).** The migration night: a drive failed twelve hours early, replication had silently stopped 48 hours before, a 75-million-row copy ran 20x slow, and the author rewrote the suggestion algorithm as a Windows Forms app between 11 pm and 3 am (pp. 230–232).

**The Biggest Gamble of Your Career (pp. 234–238, 2014).** Drip stalled at $7,000 a month for three months with churn; building marketing automation, planned at one month and taking five, cut churn "by more than 50%" and raised revenue "almost 50%" with no marketing (pp. 235–237).

**What I Learned Buying, Growing, and Selling My Startup (pp. 239–245, 2015).** HitTail grew "10x the revenue in 15 months" and was sold through FE International after Google's "Not Provided" and Drip's growth (pp. 241–242). Lessons: stair-stepping works, money makes things easier, "Buying is Probably Better Than Building" by 12–18 months, and a team of one can do "crazy things" with Upwork, AWS and Stripe (pp. 243–245).

### Extras (pp. 246–249)

**The 3 Aspects of a Great Conference Talk (pp. 247–249, 2019).** Content, delivery and entertainment; MicroConf's best talks, after 200 speakers, pair applicable content and good delivery with "a bit (but not too much)" entertainment (p. 249).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Scope of the thesis.** A hobby project needs no marketing ("Why You Should Start Marketing", p. 58). The venture-funded "hot idea" path is set aside as a lottery ticket rather than argued against: it has excitement and fun along the way, and a founder who accepts the odds is told to "go for it" ("Why 'Luck'", p. 32).
- **Marketing does not beat a missing market.** A product with a sizable market and low competition wins "even with bad marketing"; luck "trumps everything" for the 1 in 10,000 ("Why 'Luck'", p. 31). Market risk is "the deadliest of the three" and nearly impossible to correct later ("Small Startup Acquisition Part 2", p. 219).
- **The retention rule has a trade-off.** Feature work is "at times more important" than sales, and the essay names a real trade-off between growing revenue and keeping current customers happy ("Losing People", p. 9).
- **Conversion figures are ranges, not rules.** Optimized sites convert between 0.7% and 4% depending on price point and "quality of traffic" ("Why Focusing on Traffic", p. 16). The traffic-quality list has exceptions the author says he can point to ("Nine Levels", p. 21). The returning-visitor figures are "a good rule of thumb", not proof, which "would require reams of data" ("#1 Goal", p. 24). The two email split tests do not "unequivocally prove the point" (p. 29).
- **The $20 impulse threshold.** The first-visit rule stops applying below the "impulse" price point of about $20 ("#1 Goal", p. 22).
- **Social media is not dismissed.** Blogging and social are called "fantastic avenues" for an audience, for anyone with the time, tools and talent; email is the first goal, social comes once that "cornerstone" is in place ("#1 Goal", p. 26).
- **Free plans can work.** The essay does not say a free-plan launch cannot succeed; Wufoo, MailChimp and FreshBooks did, and "certain types of apps" are likelier to succeed with freemium ("Why Free Plans Don't Work", p. 42). The 37signals quotation the essay opens with is the counter-position stated in full (p. 35).
- **Acquisition-cost figures are estimates.** The telemarketing numbers "are estimates only" and the author has never used such a service ("Why Making Something", p. 47). Only one of LTV, cost per click and conversion rate can be known before marketing begins (p. 45). AdWords at a $100 CPA is named as a case where venture funding "makes complete sense" (p. 46).
- **Bottom-up sizing is a rule of thumb.** The 40–60% of the tool's number is "only a rule of thumb" ("Your Market is Smaller", p. 55), and bottom-up shows reach, not "the total market size" (p. 50).
- **Open betas and large betas.** The author is "not a fan of open betas", but a large beta is allowed with "a good reason", provided the software is not given to everyone ("Why You Should Start Marketing", p. 60).
- **Launch conversion depends on the list.** The 5–40% range depends on how long the list was collected, how interested the prospects are, and how compelling the offer is (p. 61).
- **Landing page tools.** Unbounce is "a bit pricey for a developer" but a good choice for a non-developer (pp. 63–64). Static HTML is fine for a designer (p. 63).
- **Explainer videos.** A funded startup, a top-dollar consultant, or anyone needing one video is told to hire it out; the author's process is "not the best fit" for everyone (pp. 66, 72).
- **Open source.** Exceptions exist, "Absolutely": Firefox, WordPress and Paint.NET are named, and sometimes the open-source app is worth using ("How to Compete Against Open-Source", pp. 77–78).
- **Expenses.** The list depends on country, readers with national health care are told to count themselves lucky, and not every item will apply ("Expenses", p. 81).
- **Deadlines.** The author does not use deadlines "all that much"; they can hurt quality of work and of life, and overuse brings "deadline fatigue" ("How to Force Yourself to Ship", pp. 92, 95).
- **Speed can be a weakness.** Quick response "can be taken to an extreme", spending five minutes where five hours are needed ("Lesser Known Traits", p. 102).
- **Business books have a place.** The author is not telling founders to stop reading books; survey-course books help before a founder knows what kind of company to start, and four specific books are recommended ("Stop Reading Business Books", pp. 111, 113–114). Reading as a hobby is allowed, "in your spare time" (p. 113).
- **Outsourcing.** The author says not to hire out every aspect of the product; architecture is outsourced "only for small applications"; a first attempt is likely to go wrong ("Time-Consuming Startup Roadblocks", pp. 117–119).
- **Manual processes need an end date.** The 160-to-10 approach fails "Without a number in mind" for when to automate, leaving the founder "hostage" to half-baked manual processes ("From 160 Hours to 10", p. 135).
- **Discounts.** The toxic-customer sign is a discount request "With No Reason"; the author's company works with particular circumstances, schools and non-profits especially ("How to Detect a Toxic Customer", p. 138). A warning sign alone "is not a big deal" (p. 138), and the fifth is "far from a deal-breaker" (p. 140).
- **Low prices.** A $1 product can work if the market is "gigantic" and the founder has the expertise or funds to reach it ("You Can't Make Money Charging $1", p. 146); a founder stuck at $5–7 is told to bill annually rather than quit (p. 148). A low monthly price kept HitTail's customers long enough to make Facebook ads pay ("Stair Step", p. 186).
- **Survey findings are product-specific.** The four DotNetInvoice insights are specific to that product and "may or may not apply" elsewhere ("Four Things I Learned", p. 150), and the author's own Analytics disagreed with the survey (p. 150).
- **Developer hiring.** The developer-demographic descriptions "are generalized" and companies "may not match exactly" ("How to Attract Software Developers", p. 159). NASA's standard is not the bar the author sets ("Personality Traits", p. 165).
- **Stair-stepping is a path, not a guarantee.** The author states there is "no guarantee" the approach launches a successful product ("Stair Step", p. 187); it is not the fastest path but "a predictable one" ("What I Learned", p. 243). Steps can be skipped by those with time, money, skills and confidence, which the author says he lacked (p. 187).
- **Buying over building.** Building "is a viable option" for a founder who enjoys code or has non-billable time ("Build or Buy", pp. 189–190); buying carries the risk of "a product you didn't build" and the limit of "what's for sale" (p. 193). The DotNetInvoice purchase was overpaid (p. 205) and the HitTail payback was not going to hit twelve months (p. 227).
- **The founder's numbers describe the founder.** The 4–6 month, 300–600 hour theory of time to 1.0 and the 6–12 month flywheel are "Rob's Theory", with 9 to 24 months as the observed spread, and some companies never reaching it (p. 217).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, or company the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. The essays are dated 2006–2019 and the 2023 revision does not say which passages it updated, so each item is checked against the essay's own date. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes the figure.

- **Google Website Optimizer as the A/B testing tool ("Losing People", p. 10; "Why Focusing on Traffic", p. 15; "#1 Goal", p. 29).** Gone twice over. Website Optimizer was retired in 2012 into Analytics Content Experiments, which gave way to Google Optimize in 2017, which was shut down in September 2023 (searchenginejournal.com, "Google Optimize Discontinued"; parahgroup.com, "What Happened to Google Website Optimizer"). Optimizely and Visual Website Optimizer, also named on p. 29, are live.
- **Google Analytics goals for tracking conversion by source ("Nine Levels", p. 19; "#1 Goal", p. 24).** Renamed twice: Universal Analytics goals became GA4 conversions, and on March 21, 2024 conversions not tied to Google Ads became "key events" (analyticsmania.com, "Key Events with Google Analytics 4"). The mechanism the essay describes exists under a different name.
- **MailChimp's free tier to 1,000 subscribers ("#1 Goal", p. 27; "Why You Should Start Marketing", p. 62).** Changed. The free plan allowed 2,000 contacts for years, dropped to 500 contacts in 2023, and on February 17, 2026 dropped to 250 contacts and 500 sends a month (emailexpert.com, "Mailchimp's Free Plan Just Shrunk"; blog.groupmail.io, "Mailchimp Free Plan Changes 2026").
- **Unbounce at $25 a month, "launched a few months ago" ("Why You Should Start Marketing", pp. 63–64).** Changed. Unbounce's 2026 tiers start at a $29 Starter plan capped at 500 visitors, with the practical entry Build plan at $99 a month (landerlab.io, "Unbounce Pricing 2026"; leadpages.com, "Unbounce Pricing in 2026").
- **The LaunchPad WordPress theme (p. 62).** Now a plugin, Launchpad by Obox, still listed on WordPress.org as a coming-soon and maintenance-mode plugin (wordpress.org/support/plugin/launchpad-by-obox). Whether it is maintained in 2026 is unchecked.
- **The AdWords Keyword Tool with broad, phrase and exact match checkboxes and global versus local searches ("Your Market is Smaller", pp. 51–55).** Gone. Google replaced it with Keyword Planner in 2013, which reports exact match only, and which shows volume ranges rather than numbers to accounts without active spend (wordstream.com, "How to Get Phrase & Broad Match Traffic Data Back from Google Keyword Planner"; support.google.com thread on ranges). The four mistakes cannot be made in the same form; the underlying caution about a tool's number exceeding real visits is untested here.
- **AdWords at about $4 a click for "invoicing software" ("Why Making Something", p. 44).** Higher. 2025 benchmarks put the average Google Ads CPC at $5.26 across industries, $5.34 for B2B software and $15.36 for pure SaaS terms (wordstream.com, "Google Ads Benchmarks 2025"; kampaio.com, "B2B SaaS Google Ads Benchmarks 2026"). The specific term's price is unchecked.
- **Facebook clicks at $0.80–1 untuned and $0.10–0.20 with tricks (p. 46).** Higher. 2025 averages are $0.70 for traffic campaigns and $1.92 for lead campaigns across industries (wordstream.com, "Facebook Ads Benchmarks 2025"; localiq.com, "Facebook Advertising Benchmarks for 2025"). The $0.10–0.20 figure has no 2025 counterpart.
- **HostGator's up-to-$125 referral bounty (p. 47).** Holds: the affiliate program pays $65 to $125 per referral on a tiered scale (uppromote.com, "HostGator Affiliate Program"; hostgator.com/affiliates).
- **Early monthly churn "in the 5-10% range" (p. 45).** Consistent with later data. ChartMogul's benchmarks put a median company under $300K ARR at 6.5% monthly customer churn and 9.1% gross MRR churn, and 8.2% gross MRR churn for products under $25 ARPA (chartmogul.com/saas-metrics/revenue-churn; chartmogul.com/reports).
- **Un-optimized sites at 0.1–0.5% and optimized at 0.7–4% ("Why Focusing on Traffic", p. 16); landing pages capturing 30–50% of visitors as emails, 5–10% if badly designed, and "just under 50%" for the author's book page (pp. 62–63).** Different measures, so not directly comparable. Unbounce's 2024 report of 41,000 pages puts the median landing-page conversion at 6.6% across industries and 3.8% for SaaS, counting any page goal as a conversion, with email-driven visitors converting at 19.3% (unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/saas-conversion-rate; unbounce.com/average-conversion-rates-landing-pages). - **IndyMac and Countrywide as the stable, well-paid employers ("How to Attract Software Developers", p. 158; "Nine Things", p. 170).** Both failed in 2008, a year after the essay: IndyMac was seized by the FDIC in July 2008 and Countrywide was absorbed by Bank of America the same year. Not re-verified against a primary source here; widely recorded.
- **Higher One's founder as the author's way into New Haven ("How to Recruit", p. 157).** The company was bought by a Blackboard affiliate for $260 million in August 2016 (insidehighered.com, "Blackboard Owner Acquires Higher One").
- **SourceGear Vault at $299 a license against Subversion ("Open-Source", pp. 76, 78).** Vault is still sold, per named user (sourcegear.com/vault); the $299 figure is unchecked against current pricing.
- **Glass's "up to 28 times" between best and worst programmers ("Personality Traits", p. 167).** The figure is in Glass's book, and the underlying individual-differences studies have been challenged since: a Carnegie Mellon SEI analysis found the spread came from a few very low performances rather than exceptional high ones, with the same programmers seldom best or worst (cacm.acm.org, "Is there a 10x gap between best and average programmers"; blog.codinghorror.com, "Revisiting The Facts and Fallacies of Software Engineering").
- **NASA's shuttle software, 420,000 lines with one error ("Personality Traits", p. 165).** The quotation matches Charles Fishman's 1996 Fast Company article (fastcompany.com/28121/they-write-right-stuff). The shuttle program ended in 2011.
- **oDesk, elance and Upwork ("Roadblocks", p. 120; "What I Learned", p. 245).** Elance and oDesk merged in 2013 and became Upwork in May 2015 (techcrunch.com, "Elance-oDesk Rebrands As Upwork"). Escrow.com, used for the HitTail close (p. 226), was bought by Freelancer.com for $7.5 million in 2015 (techcrunch.com, April 2015). FE International and Quiet Light (p. 242) are live brokers.

WP Simple Pay was sold to Sandhills Development in October 2018 (sandhillsdev.com, "WP Simple Pay joins the Sandhills Development family). PHP Grid and FatCatApps are unchecked.

TechZing, the podcast the author cites for "The Madness" (p. 97), posted episode 413 in December 2025 (podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/techzing). Mixergy (p. 40) is live. ## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **A revision that did not revise.** The 2023 introduction claims the text was updated (Introduction, p. 7), yet the essays still recommend Google Website Optimizer, retired in 2012 (pp. 10, 15), the AdWords Keyword Tool's match-type checkboxes, gone since 2013 (pp. 52–53), a MailChimp free tier that no longer exists at that size (p. 27), Unbounce at $25 (p. 63), RSS as an audience channel (p. 25), and a fax machine (p. 84). IndyMac and Countrywide stand as the stable employers (p. 158) in an essay dated 2007, with no note that both failed in 2008. Section 7 has each check. A reader cannot tell which sentences the author still stands behind.
- **Editing defects.** "The Terror of Firsts" carries the footnote URL of the next essay, "Why Startup Founders Should Stop Reading Business Books" (pp. 106, 109). The Part 2 divider lists "Don't Plan to Get Rich from Your Startup" twice, at 116 and 121, while page 116 is the outsourcing essay (p. 85). The book's own table of contents numbers the essays' original-URL footnotes as if they were chapters, and the numbering breaks at footnote 36 (p. 197) and again at the unnumbered third part of the software-acquisition story (p. 204).
- **Correlation presented as cause.** Bidsketch's 8x and 10x rise in paid conversions after the free plan was deleted is measured in the weeks after a launch-list spike had already faded, with no control (pp. 36–39). CrazyEgg's doubled revenue in the month it dropped free (p. 41) is one month, and the source later described the decision as not the best for the long term (section 7). Drip's Workflows launch is called the biggest growth accelerator in 18 months on the strength of a public deadline (p. 92), and Drip 2.0's churn falling by half is attributed to the features while "not doing a lick of marketing" (p. 237), with no separation of feature, season, or the customers already lost. The HitTail trial-install jump from 25% to 75% (p. 224) followed a redesign, trial expiry, and trial emails at once.
- **Figures without a source.** "1 out of 10,000" for viral luck and "1 in 100" for the self-funded path (pp. 31, 33), "99.5%" of successful businesses finding a paying market (p. 34), "10x…perhaps 100x" better odds in a niche (p. 122), "80% or more" first-time visitors (p. 25), the 5–10% and 30–50% landing-page capture rates (p. 63), the 5–40% launch conversion (p. 61), "80%" of open-source projects costing more time (p. 78), and "60-100 hours a month" saved by outsourcing (p. 117) are stated from experience with no data behind them.
- **The LTV formula is used without its own caveat.** LTV = price ÷ churn (p. 45) is offered as "simple", and the more complex formulas are waved at; the essay then reasons about a $237.50 LTV as if it were cash, while a separate aside admits the money takes 12.5 months to arrive (p. 46). The Stair Step essay treats LTV as the fact that decides which channels are open (pp. 185–186) using the same formula.
- **Examples from the author's own portfolio and circle.** DotNetInvoice, HitTail, Drip, JustBeachTowels and the Academy are the author's; Bidsketch's founder wrote the free-plan essay and is a friend; the stair-step examples are MicroConf attendees (pp. 24, 35, 184, 208). Counter-examples, products in the same circle that failed, appear only as the anonymous "half-finished carnage" (p. 104) and the boneyard (p. 129).
- **The self-funded odds are asserted against a strawman.** The venture path is described only as the "hot idea" consumer play and the "startup lottery" (pp. 31–33); a funded vertical B2B product, the case closest to the book's own audience, is not considered.
- **Internal tension.** Business books are "the new television" (p. 112), yet the book recommends four of them (p. 114), quotes Glass, Collins, Warrillow, Branden and Steve Martin as authorities, and is itself a business book. Audience-building is "un-sexy" and what works long term (p. 90), and the author's path ran through a blog, a podcast and a conference, while the Stair Step essay warns against marketing complexity (pp. 182–187). "You Can't Make Money Charging $1 Per Month" argues for $50–100 (p. 148), while the same author's HitTail is praised for a low monthly price that kept customers long enough to make ads pay (p. 186).
- **The benchmarks describe a 2009–2011 web.** Un-optimized sites at 0.1–0.5%, $4 clicks, $0.10 Facebook clicks and a 1,000-subscriber free email tier (pp. 16, 44, 46, 27) are ten to fifteen years old, and section 7 finds every paid-click figure higher and the free tier smaller. The direction of the advice may survive the numbers; the book gives no way to tell.
- **The bottom-up sizing method is tied to a dead tool.** The four mistakes (pp. 51–55) are steps in a UI that no longer exists, and the 40–60% rule of thumb for a #1 ranking is unsourced; nothing in the essay survives as a procedure a reader can run.
- **Claims the evidence does not reach.** "Technical execution is mostly trivial" and "your code isn't worth much" (p. 57) are argued from clone scripts at $100; the same book spends 40 pages on the difficulty of building, buying and stabilizing software (pp. 188–233). The two email split tests (pp. 28–29) are presented with the author's own admission that they prove nothing, then used to promise "10% or more" (p. 29). The developer essays generalize from "a dozen colleagues" (p. 172) and one Herzberg study of 200 engineers and accountants in the 1950s (p. 170).
- **Advice written for a one-time-purchase business.** The conversion arithmetic, the $329 product, the "impulse" $20, the 30-day money-back guarantee, and the downloaded-trial split tests (pp. 22, 28, 45, 205) come from selling installable software; the subscription essays (pp. 9, 146, 185, 235) are later and thinner, and the book never reconciles the two sets of numbers.
