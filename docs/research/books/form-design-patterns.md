## 3. The claim

The book's thesis, in the author's frame, is that forms are where "every meaningful interaction" on the web happens and also "a 10,000-volt electromagnet for attracting usability problems", so the same problems recur and are best solved with reusable, well-researched patterns rather than from scratch each time (Introduction, pp. viii–ix). The author first drafted the book as fifty principles, one chapter each, and abandoned that shape because "rules can be broken — occasionally", because problems cut across principles, and because trade-offs have to be made; the book is instead organized around ten concrete forms, each a chapter, whose patterns are meant to transfer to other domains (Introduction, p. x). Its measure of good design is the seven inclusive design principles from the Paciello Group, quoted in full and referred to by number throughout: provide a comparable experience, consider situation, be consistent, give control, offer choice, prioritize content, add value (Introduction, pp. xv–xvi). Its method is progressive enhancement: start from the native control the browser gives "for free", enhance only when the basic experience is not good enough, and make sure the enhancement fails back to something that still works (Ch. 1, pp. 35–38; Ch. 3, pp. 129–130). The foreword by Heydon Pickering frames the book as "simple solutions to would-be complex problems" and singles out labels above inputs as its radical position (Foreword, PDF pp. 8–9).

The author says what the claim is not. It is not a set of rules: principles "crumble under scrutiny" without a reason behind them, and the chapters are problems, not rules (Introduction, pp. x, xiv). It is not about visual style: "the way you style your form components will, at least in part, be determined by your product or company's brand", and only label position and focus styles are held to be usability matters (Ch. 1, p. 30). It is not a JavaScript book: jQuery and constructor functions are used "so that this book can focus on forms", and a reader who prefers otherwise "will have no trouble refactoring" (Ch. 1, pp. 42–43). It is not a claim that its patterns beat research: where it departs from convention (passphrases, no-password sign-in) it says to do so "only once we've conducted thorough and diverse user research", and it closes the expense chapter with "there's no right or wrong here. It's about choosing the most appropriate pattern for your users" (Ch. 1, p. 30; Ch. 9, p. 370).

## 4. The argument, chapter by chapter

### Introduction (pp. viii–xvi)

The author came to forms from the "view-source school" and argues that forms are where users "create, update and delete things", that their low barrier to entry attracts usability problems, and that patterns save time and give users a familiar, "coherent experience" (Introduction, pp. viii–x). The fifty-principle draft was dropped for ten problem-shaped chapters (p. x); principles still matter because "without principles, it's hard to know whether what we've designed is objectively good", and the book's are the Paciello Group's seven inclusive design principles, with Frank Chimero's "The Web's Grain" as the reason to go with the web's constraints rather than fight them (pp. xiv–xvi).

### Chapter 1, A Registration Form (pp. 18–67)

Every control that accepts input gets a `<label>`, connected by `for`/`id`, because a label is read by sighted users, heard by screen reader users, and enlarges the hit area for motor-impaired users, with Laura Kalbag's four parameters (visual, auditory, motor, cognitive) as the frame (Ch. 1, pp. 20–21). Placeholders are not hints: they disappear on typing, get mistaken for values, fail contrast, are cut off, and are unsupported in some browsers and screen readers, so a hint sits above the control, either inside the label in a `<span>` or joined by `aria-describedby`; the book prefers the former because clicking it focuses the control and because Internet Explorer 11 did not support `aria-describedby`, citing "the first rule of ARIA" (pp. 22–25). Float labels save no space and lose the hint (pp. 25–27, quoting Luke Wroblewski). The "question protocol" asks why each field exists; first and last name are dropped from the registration form, and no-password sign-in (Medium's emailed link) and passphrases are weighed as alternatives to a password, each with a cost in familiarity (pp. 27–30). Labels go above inputs on Matteo Penzo's eye-tracking finding that users "capture both elements with a single eye movement", on small viewports, and on zoom (p. 31). Fields "should look like form fields": bordered boxes, labels closest to their own control (the law of proximity), a font size of at least 16 pixels, a tap target of "at least 44px" (p. 32). Default focus outlines are faint but never removed, only restyled (p. 33). The email field uses sentence case and `type="email"` for the keyboard, with a plain text box as the fallback, which introduces progressive enhancement: HTML first, then CSS and JavaScript that may fail, with feature detection before use, and "so often, the basic experience is the best and most performant one" (pp. 34–38).

The password field carries its rules as a hint, and a reveal button replaces a "Confirm password" field (pp. 38–41); the toggle is a `<button type="button">` whose label switches between Show and Hide, with a checkbox or an `aria-pressed` state offered as alternatives if research shows the changing label confuses screen reader users (pp. 40–45). The label reads "Choose password", not "Password", to avoid prompting an existing one (p. 45). Buttons are not links: a link has weak perceived affordance and a pointer cursor, a button has strong affordance and no pointer; Jeremy Keith's "material honesty" is the book's reason against a link that looks like a button (pp. 45–47). The submit button sits left-aligned directly below the last field, where the eye is already going, and its text is a verb (pp. 47–48). Validation turns off HTML5 validation with `novalidate` because browser messages differ and so that client and server errors look the same; it listens to the form's `submit` event, not the button's click, so implicit submission by Enter still works (pp. 49–51). Three parts of the page update on error: the document title is prefixed with the error count, an error summary at the top with the heading "There's a problem" (never the word "Error"), `role="group"`, `tabindex="-1"` and focus moved to it, with links that focus each field, hidden entirely when there are no errors; and an inline message above each field, inside the label, in red with an SVG icon, plus `aria-invalid` (pp. 51–58). Rules forgive trivial mistakes, trimming whitespace as Jared Spool's "one line of code" joke demands (p. 60). Live inline validation interrupts too early or too late despite "some evidence" that it helps in long forms; the checklist affirmation pattern (MailChimp's password rules ticking off) is the gentler variant; and disabled submit buttons leave users wondering what is wrong, are unfocusable, and are hard to read (pp. 61–63). Error copy is content: one study put custom error messages at a 0.5% conversion gain worth "more than £250,000" a year; one message is written to work in both the summary and inline; and the checklist is concise, consistent, specific, human, plain, active, and blameless, with "Please", "invalid", "forbidden" and "mandatory" named as words to avoid (pp. 63–66).

### Chapter 2, A Checkout Form (pp. 68–122)

The evidence for one thing per page is the author's own work: a 2008 Boots accordion checkout that users struggled with and was rebuilt one page per step, and a 2014 Just Eat rebuild of the same kind that produced "a whopping 5% increase in conversion", which "equated to 2 million orders a year" (Ch. 2, pp. 68–69). The pattern was named by Robin Whittleton of GDS in 2016 and first written up by Caroline Jarrett in 2015; it is not one element per page, and Jarrett's GOV.UK Verify test of "Create a username" and "Create a password" on separate pages found users "weren't bothered", so the rule is "start with one thing (field or question) per page, then, through research, find out if grouping fields improves the experience" (pp. 70–71). Its reasons: focus on one thing, errors caught early, fast pages, granular saving, no timeouts (the film *I, Daniel Blake* is the example), momentum, and screen space for components like a seat chooser (pp. 72–73). Questions come in a sensible order (Jarrett and Gaffney's car salesperson), and guest checkout is mandatory on Spool's "$300 Million Button" (pp. 73–75). Each field's hint says why it is asked; optional fields are marked "(optional)" in the label rather than required ones with an asterisk, on Wroblewski and on Jessica Enders' point that a mark should show what is different, and most fields are required once the question protocol has run (pp. 75–79). Address fields are sized to their content, because Baymard found that a field "too long or too short" made users doubt the label (pp. 79–82). Radio groups need `fieldset` and `legend`, but wrapping every field in one is "unnecessary and verbose"; the common option comes first and is checked by default; and GDS built custom radio controls because users did not know a label was clickable (pp. 84–87). A textarea with a limit does not use `maxlength`, which truncates silently for people watching the keyboard; a character countdown in a `role="status"` `aria-live="polite"` region announces only after typing stops, and ideally only near the limit (pp. 88–93). The payment form is reduced to what Stripe confirmed is the minimum, "card number, CVV, and expiration date", with the billing address kept for the records and the "Valid from" date dropped (pp. 94–96). `autocomplete` tokens turn on autofill, which Wroblewski's Chrome figures put at "9 billion times/month" (pp. 97–98). `type="number"` is for amounts only: not for card numbers, expiry dates, or codes, because browsers strip leading zeros and ignore slashes; `type="tel"` as a workaround is discouraged; a text box with `pattern="[0-9]*"` gets the numeric keyboard on iOS (pp. 99–103). Input is forgiven under Postel's law, the CVC gets a hint saying where it is, and a "same as delivery" checkbox hides the billing address by progressive disclosure (pp. 103–106). A "Check your answers" page reduces effort even though it adds a step, and the final button says "Place order"; every answer appears with an Edit link that returns to the review page (pp. 107–109). The confirmation page is "the start of the relationship" and the right time to ask for a password, and a second-time customer skips straight to the review page (pp. 109–112). Progress bars: the Carer's Allowance team "removed a 12-step progress bar with no effect on completion rates or times", so start without one, put "Step 3 of 4" in the `<h1>` if needed, and hide a visual bar with `aria-hidden` (pp. 113–117). An order summary sits beside the form on wide screens and below on narrow, and the browser's back button is kept working, with an in-page back link at the top left if research shows it helps (pp. 117–120).

### Chapter 3, A Flight Booking Form (pp. 123–199)

Native controls first, "familiar (by convention) and fully accessible out of the box" (Ch. 3, p. 124). Select boxes hide options and users "don't understand how they work", on Alice Bartlett's GDS research and Wroblewski's "Dropdowns Should be the UI of Last Resort"; radio buttons expose options but do not scale to hundreds; a search box risks zero results; `<datalist>` is "too buggy for use on the open web"; so a destination field is a select box enhanced into a custom autocomplete (pp. 124–130). The component is built against Steve Faulkner's punch list (works without JavaScript, focusable, operable, works with assistive tech): the select box stays in the document, visually hidden, as the value that is submitted; the text box takes `role="combobox"`, `aria-owns`, `aria-autocomplete="list"`, `aria-expanded`, `autocomplete="off"` and `autocapitalize="none"`; the menu is a `listbox` of `option`s with `aria-selected`; a live region announces "13 results available"; the control has one tab stop, with focus moved into the options on Down (roving `tabindex`, since `aria-activedescendant` does not fit a sibling menu); hiding on blur is rejected for an iOS 10 bug in favor of listening for Tab and document clicks; clicks use event delegation; the filter is case-insensitive, forgives partial matches, and accepts endonyms and common typos from a `data-alt` attribute (pp. 129–153). Dates: three select boxes still allow 31 February and a free-text box is ambiguous, so the type of date decides, per the GDS Service Manual: a date from a document matches the document; a memorable date is three text boxes for day, month and year with `pattern="[0-9]*"`, because typing six digits beats scrolling a calendar and the split solves locale problems; a date in the near future gets a picker, and the native `<input type="date">` is that picker, with good mobile support but no desktop Safari or Internet Explorer support at the time of writing (pp. 154–160). Users do not notice that native pickers look different across browsers (Nicholas Zakas' two-photo slide), so a custom picker is built only for unsupporting browsers, detected by feature test, as an inline calendar (not an overlay) with a toggle button, `role="grid"`, full dates in `aria-label`, roving tabindex and arrow keys; when JavaScript also fails the user gets a bare text box with no hint, and a placeholder is called "the lesser of two evils" there (pp. 161–178). Passenger counts are amounts, so `type="number"` fits, and a custom stepper with big Add and Remove buttons replaces the tiny spinners, with a live region carrying the value because putting live-region attributes on the input broke its semantics; plus and minus icons pass Thomas Byttebier's test because "users can type a number if they want" (pp. 179–185). Flights are radio buttons whose labels carry time and price, and a group error goes inside the legend with `aria-invalid` on the fieldset, the summary linking to the first radio (pp. 186–190). Seats are checkboxes laid out in rows; nested fieldsets are avoided by asking for the class first; "checkboxes are never round" (Daniel De Laney); Miller's seven-item limit is a myth on a page where "the information is visually present"; unavailable seats are disabled, "one of the few use cases where disabling elements is appropriate"; and when a user picks one seat too many the earlier pick is unchecked rather than the rest disabled (pp. 190–197).

### Chapter 4, A Login Form (pp. 200–219)

The login form mirrors the registration form: the label says "Email address" (or "Username or email address"), never a bare "Username" (Ch. 4, pp. 201–202). `autocapitalize="none"`, `autocorrect="off"` and `spellcheck="false"` stop iOS and Android "fixing" a username, though the email input itself stopped autocapitalizing after iOS 5 (pp. 203–204). The password field keeps the hint and the reveal button, because "hackers don't hack this way" and the rules are on the registration page anyway (pp. 204–206). Auto-tabbing between split boxes is rejected on BBC guidance and on Léonie Watson ("it takes me more time and effort to correct mistakes caused by auto-tab"), and a single clearly labeled box replaces three (pp. 206–207). "Sign in" is "perhaps more human than 'Log in'", banks say "Log in", and whichever is chosen is used consistently across URLs, links, headings and buttons, with "Log out" pairing "Log in" (p. 208). The error "The username and password don't match" is replaced by "The password doesn't correspond to your username", on Spool's account of the security argument and the observation that username availability is checkable by registering anyway (pp. 209–210). The login page inherits the site's layout (Tesco's different layout is the counter-example); login and register are separate pages with a link between them, not two forms on one page; the forgotten-password link goes before the form, because between the fields it takes focus on Tab and before the submit button it swallows Enter, and "the submit button should be the last interactive element in the form" (pp. 211–215). Social login raises privacy ("We won't post without asking", Medium), account interchange (Medium merges accounts, Kidly showed an error), and choice paralysis: Barry Schwartz's jam display (24 jams, 3% bought; 6 jams, 30%), Vanguard's 2% drop in participation per ten extra funds, and Hick's law (pp. 215–218).

### Chapter 5, An Inbox (pp. 220–260)

A list of emails is marked up as a `<ul>`, not a `<dl>` (not terms and definitions), not a `<table>` (hard to style on small viewports, and Gmail's headingless table is "materially dishonest" layout per Keith), and not an `<ol>` (no required order); the whole list item can then be a link, and it is "stylistically malleable and, therefore, responsive" (Ch. 5, pp. 220–225). Each row's checkbox needs a label that would duplicate the link text, so either the interface uses modes (read and manage), which are best when one mode dominates, or a visually hidden `<label>` carries a screen-reader-specific message such as "From John Oates, subject…", preferred over `aria-labelledby` on the first rule of ARIA (pp. 226–230). Highlighting a marked row is unnecessary, on MailChimp's example and the author's own research (pp. 230–231). Three submit buttons (Archive, Delete, Mark as spam) raise the implicit-submission problem, answered by putting the least destructive action first and offering undo; the menu sits statically above the list because sticky menus obscure content, mask pages that are too long, and are far away by keyboard, with `position: sticky` allowed as an enhancement where useful; buttons are shown at all times rather than hidden or disabled until a row is selected (pp. 231–237). Where the buttons do not fit, a select box is not a menu, because a select is for input, an `onchange` submit fails WCAG 3.2.2 "Changing the setting of any user interface component does not automatically cause a change of context", and Chrome on Windows fires `onchange` on the arrow key; adaptive design, which ships two layouts and hides one by device width, is "normally unnecessary and counter-productive" and "should always be a last resort", against responsive design that adds a media query only where something breaks, at a content breakpoint (pp. 238–245). Menus open on click, never hover, and "features should never be inferred from screen size" (pp. 245–246). The true menu uses `role="menu"` and `role="menuitem"`, a toggle button with `aria-haspopup` and `aria-expanded`, `matchMedia` on the same `em` query as the CSS, and arrow, Escape, Home and End keys (pp. 246–253). "Select all" is a button that toggles to "Deselect all", not a checkbox, because a control "should look like what it does" (pp. 253–255). A success message is a green panel in the document flow with `role="alert"`; toasts obscure content and vanish before they are read; a Dismiss button is added only if research shows it is wanted, and only by JavaScript; for repeated tasks, confirmation dialogs are "speed bumps on the motorway", and undo beside the success message is the alternative (pp. 255–259).

### Chapter 6, A Search Form (pp. 262–276)

Search should "search everything", including the returns policy Spool's participant could not find, and where it cannot, the label says so ("Search products"); analytics on empty-result queries drive improvement (Ch. 6, pp. 263–264). The form is a label, a `type="search"` input and a submit button inside a `<div role="search">` landmark, because the role on the `<form>` itself would override its semantics (pp. 264–266). To fit the header, the submit button may go inline for a single-field form only; the label may be visually hidden but never removed; the button is kept, since without it implicit submission fails for multi-field forms and iOS may not submit; and the better answer is a toggle button that reveals the whole form, moving focus to the input (pp. 266–274). Results keep the search text, state the count in the `<title>`, offer sorting, and avoid infinite scrolling in favor of "Show more" or pagination (pp. 274–275).

### Chapter 7, A Filter Form (pp. 278–313)

Filters are only for large result sets, and let users navigate by their own mental model (Stephanie Lemieux's cookbook) (Ch. 7, pp. 278–279). Interactive filters apply on each click and reload the page; batch filters collect several and submit once, on Katie Sherwin's waiter analogy; links styled as checkboxes are "materially dishonest"; the chapter goes with batch filters made of real checkboxes and radios (pp. 279–284). The page keeps a heading hierarchy (`h1` "Wallets", `h2` "Filters" and "Products"), the filter is an `<aside>` and the form has `role="form"` and `method="get"`, so the URL carries the query and the page rebuilds without JavaScript (pp. 284–289). Gumtree's research (Dave House) found desktop users selecting filters without submitting and reporting "Your filters are broken", an expectation the author attributes to Jakob's law and to dishonest interfaces elsewhere; before submitting automatically, make the button prominent and near, collapse categories, duplicate the button at the top, or use `position: sticky` (pp. 289–291). Switching to links loses text inputs and hits query-string limits (p. 292). Automatic submission on `change` fails WCAG 3.2.2 and breaks arrow-key navigation of radios, so Ajax is the fix, which then needs a live region for loading state, `history.pushState` and `popstate` to keep the back button working (Baymard observed "confusion, disappointment, anger and even abandonments" when it broke), collapsed categories so results stay in view, and an awareness that Ajax drains battery and "is not necessarily faster" because it defeats progressive rendering (Jake Archibald) (pp. 293–302). Categories collapse behind a `<button>` inside the legend with `aria-expanded` and a plus/minus SVG (pp. 302–307). On small screens Gumtree "reluctantly" went adaptive with a full-screen panel; the book prefers Amazon's and eBay's tray, from Kathryn Whitenton's NN/g article, where filters overlay but do not cover the results (pp. 307–310). Selected filters stay in place and are also listed at the top under an `h3` "Current filters" with remove links (pp. 310–311).

### Chapter 8, An Upload Form (pp. 314–348)

The native file picker with `enctype="multipart/form-data"` is enough for one file (Ch. 8, pp. 314–315). Restyling it means hiding the input and styling the label as a button, which loses the label's meaning (screen readers hear "cv.doc"), the hint and error convention, the native drop zone, and room for long names: "pretty and useless is considerably worse than ugly and useful" (pp. 316–320). The `multiple` attribute selects from one folder only and is not universally supported, so the persistent form pattern lets a user upload repeatedly until done, with `multiple` as an enhancement on top (pp. 320–324). Drag and drop is an enhancement beside the picker, not a replacement, because mobile cannot drag and tremors make it hard; the drop zone uploads immediately by Ajax because a file input's value cannot be set by script, with per-file `<progress>` bars, success and error rows using the same error markup as validation, a visually hidden live region ("Uploading files. Please wait."), and feature detection for `ondrop`, `FormData` and the File API (pp. 324–341). Other considerations: convert formats server-side, help people find files (Ed Horsford's passport-photo research at GDS found desktop users lost imported photos and Android hid them under "Documents"), hand off to the phone by code or URL, offer Dropbox only with care, say "Upload" not "Attach" outside email, and use `accept` and `capture` knowing `capture` on Android removes the choice of an existing photo (pp. 341–347).

### Chapter 9, An Expense Form (pp. 350–371)

Three patterns for an unknown number of entries. The persistent form (GitHub's collaborators form, the to-do list) suits short forms but cannot branch, pushes the form down the page, has two calls to action, and costs a request per entry (Ch. 9, pp. 350–353). One thing per page with an "add another?" question at the end handles branching (pp. 353–354). The add another pattern clones a set of fields on one page with array-style names (`items[0][description]`), works without JavaScript by regenerating on the server, injects Remove buttons only once there is more than one item, renumbers names and `for` attributes on every change, focuses the first new field on add, and on remove focuses the `<h1>` with `tabindex="-1"` and no outline, because browsers "don't know where to place focus when it has been destroyed" (Heydon Pickering) (pp. 354–368). No notification bar is added, since focus movement is the feedback, and animation is not added, since "needless animation is jarring" and can harm users with ADHD or autism (pp. 368–369). Persistent and one-thing-per-page suit infrequent use and lower digital literacy; add another suits frequent use without branching (p. 370).

### Chapter 10, A Really Long and Complicated Form (pp. 372–381)

For tasks that take days or several people, first simplify or remove the process (Ch. 10, p. 373). "Check before you start", named by GDS, tells users what they need before they begin (HSBC's mortgage page; the Renew Your Passport eligibility questions) and lowers support costs (pp. 373–375). The GDS task list pattern breaks a long process into sub-tasks each marked complete, on Lauren Marchese's dopamine argument for small wins and Don Norman's checklists, which are "proven to increase the accuracy of behavior and to reduce error" under interruption; MailChimp's campaign checklist is the non-government example (pp. 375–378). Each status is clear, remaining effort is visible, progress is saved; the completed list stays visible for review with one clear call to action; tasks are named with verbs, sized alike, ordered in an `<ol>` if order matters, and marked with who must do them (pp. 378–380).

## 5. The book's own exceptions

These are the places the author limits his own claim, admits a counter-case, or says a rule does not apply.

- **Rules in general.** "Rules can be broken — occasionally", which is why the book is problems rather than principles (Introduction, p. x). "There's no right or wrong here. It's about choosing the most appropriate pattern for your users" (Ch. 9, p. 370). "Prioritizing best practice above user needs" is listed among things to avoid (Ch. 7, p. 313).
- **Convention.** No-password sign-in and passphrases are alternatives, but "it's not that one technique is always better than the other", and a move away from convention comes "only once we've conducted thorough and diverse user research" (Ch. 1, pp. 29–30). The password reveal's changing label is the default, and the checkbox or `aria-pressed` alternatives apply "if your research shows this to be a problem" (Ch. 1, p. 43).
- **Hints.** The hint inside the label is preferred, and `aria-describedby` is offered as the other way, with the trade-off stated as hit area and Internet Explorer 11 support rather than correctness (Ch. 1, pp. 24–25). A placeholder is "the lesser of two evils" for a date format hint when both the native date input and JavaScript are unavailable (Ch. 3, p. 178).
- **Live validation.** "There's some evidence to show that live inline validation improves accuracy and decreases completion times in long forms"; the objection is to timing, and the checklist affirmation pattern is "less invasive" though "not suited to every type of field" (Ch. 1, pp. 61–62). If errors are frequent "there's probably something wrong elsewhere" (p. 62).
- **Error copy.** Instructions and descriptions take different shapes: "Enter your first name" is natural, but a length rule reads better as "First name must be 35 characters or less" (Ch. 1, p. 65). Some repetition is unavoidable and only looks bad when testers submit an empty form (p. 65).
- **One thing per page.** "It doesn't mean you'll always end up with one question per page"; research "will quickly show you that some questions will be best grouped into a longer page" (Ch. 2, p. 71). Some forms are longer than a checkout and may need an indication of progress (p. 115).
- **Extra steps.** A review page adds a step and still reduces effort; "completion time is not the only useful metric for success" (Ch. 2, pp. 107–108, 122).
- **Progress.** A step number in the heading is the first thing to try, and a visual bar is added "if research shows that a more prominent progress bar is useful" (Ch. 2, p. 116). A back link inside the page is added if "research might show you that it's useful" (p. 120).
- **Fieldsets.** Wrapping a plain address in a fieldset is "technically valid" but adds nothing (Ch. 2, p. 85). Nested fieldsets are avoided "where possible" (Ch. 3, p. 192).
- **Number input.** It is right when incrementing makes sense, there is no leading zero, no letters, slashes, minus signs or decimal points, and the number is not very large; passenger counts qualify (Ch. 2, p. 102; Ch. 3, p. 180). `type="tel"` as a workaround has no practical downside the author has met, only a standards one (Ch. 2, p. 103).
- **Select boxes.** Discouraged, yet the autocomplete is built on one because it is the only native control that fits, and `<datalist>` "might work for you" on a locked-down set of browsers (Ch. 3, pp. 128, 130).
- **Custom components.** Native first, but "it became apparent that custom components were necessary to give users the best experience" in four places: autocomplete, date picker, stepper, seat chooser (Ch. 3, p. 198). The custom date picker is removed once browser support makes it redundant (p. 179).
- **Disabling.** Unavailable seats are disabled, "one of the few use cases where disabling elements is appropriate" (Ch. 3, p. 194).
- **Many options.** Four hundred seats on one page break Miller's rule deliberately; "choosing a seat is quite a unique interaction" (Ch. 3, p. 194).
- **Icons.** Plus and minus pass because they are equally weighted and "users can type a number if they want" (Ch. 3, p. 185); the magnifying glass may go inside a submit button (Ch. 6, p. 270).
- **Sign in versus log in.** Industry decides: banks say "Log in"; the rule is consistency, not the word (Ch. 4, p. 208).
- **Choice.** "Offer choice" is a principle, and social login is the case where more choice "may also be a burden" (Ch. 4, pp. 217–218).
- **Modes.** Best "when one mode is used more frequently than the other"; if both are frequent, switching "may be undesirable" (Ch. 5, p. 228).
- **Highlighting rows.** Do it "only if user research shows this will add value" (Ch. 5, p. 231).
- **Sticky menus.** "Where sticky menus are useful, you can use `position: sticky` as a progressive enhancement" (Ch. 5, p. 235); duplicating or sticking the filter's submit button is offered before automatic submission (Ch. 7, p. 291).
- **Menu role.** `role="menu"` "isn't commonly used in web applications. Only ones that mimic desktop applications" (Ch. 5, p. 248).
- **Dismiss and confirm.** A Dismiss button is added if users stay on the page long and "research might show that dismissing a message is valuable"; confirmation "is fine for infrequent tasks" and undo is for frequent ones (Ch. 5, pp. 257–258).
- **Search.** The submit button may sit inline for a one-field search form (Ch. 6, p. 268); the label may be visually hidden (p. 268); "technological and resource limitations may come into play" against searching everything (p. 269).
- **Automatic submission.** If users share Gumtree's expectation "then you may have no choice"; the chapter builds the Ajax filter for that case while calling it a departure from convention (Ch. 7, pp. 291, 312).
- **Ajax.** "This is not to say Ajax is bad", only that it is for "smaller page updates" and settled by research "with a diverse group of people" (Ch. 7, p. 302).
- **Adaptive design.** Gumtree "reluctantly had to use an adaptive approach" on mobile, reported without condemnation before the tray alternative (Ch. 7, p. 309).
- **Label as button.** The "ill-advised technique" of styling a file input's label as a button is used inside the drop zone because a file input's value cannot be set by script (Ch. 8, p. 326).
- **Drag and drop.** "The humble file picker may be all that users need"; the enhancement is optional and always beside a picker (Ch. 8, p. 325).
- **Older browsers.** Uploading on `change` "might be confusing" and does not work in some older browsers; "whether you need to support such browsers depends on your situation" (Ch. 8, pp. 340–341).
- **Which entry pattern.** Persistent and one-thing-per-page for infrequent use and lower digital literacy; add another for frequent use without branching, at the cost of an interface "a little more complicated to operate" (Ch. 9, p. 370).
- **Long forms.** "If you can simplify the process so it's short — or even remove it altogether — do that" before any of chapter 10's patterns (Ch. 10, p. 373). Marking who completes a task is "only useful if the tasks are performed by different people" (p. 380).

## 7. What has changed since publication

Each figure, tool, company or browser fact the book leans on, checked in September 2026 against a later source. "Unchecked" means no source could be reached that confirms or refutes it. The session's web search budget was exhausted before this section was written, so every check below is a direct fetch of a primary page, and several pages that could not be fetched leave their item unchecked rather than guessed.

- **The book itself.** Smashing's product page lists the print edition as sold out and the ebook at $19, published October 2018, 384 pages, with no second edition or update note (smashingmagazine.com/printed-books/form-design-patterns). Chapter 1 is published as a free excerpt dated 2018-10-10.
- **The demos at `smashed.by/*demo` (every chapter's closing Demos list), built on Heroku per the acknowledgements (PDF p. 7).** Heroku ended free dynos and free Postgres on 2022-11-28 (heroku.com/blog/next-chapter). `smashed.by/regformdemo` now redirects to `nostyle.onrender.com/examples/registration-form`, so the demos moved to Render; that host returned HTTP 503 on two fetches, so whether they are live is unchecked.
- **Internet Explorer as the reason for several choices: IE11 not supporting `aria-describedby` (Ch. 1, p. 25), `::-ms-reveal` (p. 40), IE submitting every `<button type="submit">` (p. 46), IE8 ignoring radio size changes (Ch. 2, p. 86), SVG being focusable in IE (Ch. 3, pp. 133, 169), and the `hidden` attribute having "less support" than a class (Ch. 1, p. 56).** Microsoft ended support for the IE11 desktop application on 2022-06-15 (learn.microsoft.com/en-us/lifecycle/faq/internet-explorer-microsoft-edge). The `hidden` attribute and `aria-describedby` are baseline in every current browser; caniuse puts `position: sticky`, which the book offers "as a progressive enhancement" (Ch. 5, p. 235), at 96.67% global support with IE11 the only desktop holdout.
- **`<datalist>` "too buggy for use on the open web" (Ch. 3, p. 128).** caniuse now lists 96.76% global support, full in Chrome 69+, Edge 79+ and Safari 12.1+, still partial in every Firefox version and in Safari on iOS (caniuse.com/datalist). The bugs the book cites are no longer the whole story; the partial-support rows are.
- **`<input type="date">` unsupported on desktop Safari and Internet Explorer "at time of writing" (Ch. 3, p. 159), which is the reason the custom date picker exists.** caniuse lists desktop Safari as partial from 14.1 (2021), Chrome from 25 and Edge from 13 as full, Firefox as partial, iOS Safari full from 18.2, at 97% global (caniuse.com/input-datetime). The author's own "future support" note said the custom picker could be removed once support improved (p. 179).
- **The hint inside the `<label>` as the preferred markup, with `aria-describedby` as the alternative (Ch. 1, pp. 24–25).** The GOV.UK Design System's Text input now renders the hint as a separate `<div class="govuk-hint">` joined to the input by `aria-describedby`, not inside the label (design-system.service.gov.uk/components/text-input). Their Error message is likewise a separate `<p>` after the hint and before the input, with a visually hidden "Error:" prefix, joined by `aria-describedby` (design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-message). The book's inline error inside the label (Ch. 1, p. 57) is the older shape.
- **The error summary: heading "There's a problem", `role="group"`, `tabindex="-1"`, focused on submit (Ch. 1, pp. 53–55).** GOV.UK's component fixes the heading as "There is a problem", puts `role="alert"` on the inner container, and moves focus to the summary by script, linking a group error to the first radio or checkbox (design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-summary). The govuk-frontend changelog fetched for this read-back covered only v5.7.0 onward, so the version at which the markup diverged from the book is unchecked.
- **A password reveal and no confirm field (Ch. 1, pp. 39–41).** GOV.UK published a Password input component in March 2024 (govuk-frontend v5.3.0) with a Show/Hide toggle, hidden by default "because users might not be in a private space", the statement that a confirm field "is not necessary", and "Always allow users to copy and paste in password fields" (design-system.service.gov.uk/components/password-input). The book's `aria-pressed` alternative (p. 44) is not what GOV.UK shipped; theirs uses `aria-controls` and a spoken "Your password is visible".
- **"Sign in" is "perhaps more human" and either word may be used consistently (Ch. 4, p. 208).** The GOV.UK style guide now rules: "Use sign in rather than log in (verb) for calls-to-action", and "Do not use login as a noun" except in a product name (guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk, A to Z style guide).
- **Select boxes as a last resort (Ch. 3, pp. 124–125).** GOV.UK's Select guidance now says the same in its own words: "should only be used as a last resort in public-facing services because research shows that some users find selects very difficult to use" (design-system.service.gov.uk/components/select).
- **Character countdown without `maxlength`, announced when the user pauses (Ch. 2, pp. 89–93).** GOV.UK's Character count matches: it "does not restrict user input", and "screen reader users will hear the count announcement when they stop typing" (design-system.service.gov.uk/components/character-count).
- **Memorable dates as three boxes, a picker only for chosen dates (Ch. 3, pp. 155–159).** GOV.UK's Dates pattern still says "Ask for memorable dates, like dates of birth, using the Date input component", match a document's format for a document date, and "Never make a calendar control that depends on JavaScript as the only input option" (design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/dates).
- **Start with one thing per page and merge on evidence (Ch. 2, p. 71).** GOV.UK's Question pages pattern keeps it, keeps "Start by testing your form without a progress indicator", and offers "Question 3 of 9" as a caption (design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/question-pages). The author's own post of 2025-11-16, "End with one thing per page", keeps the rule and renames it, on the ground that in ten years of testing multi-field pages failed "countless" times and single-field pages once (adamsilver.io/blog/end-with-one-thing-per-page).
- **Check before you start (Ch. 10, pp. 373–375).** The GOV.UK pattern is now "Check a service is suitable" and its page states "this pattern used to be called 'Check before you start'" (design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/check-a-service-is-suitable).
- **The GDS task list pattern (Ch. 10, pp. 377–379).** Now a published GOV.UK component, with status tags in sentence case and "Completed" in plain text because uppercase statuses tested "harder to read" (design-system.service.gov.uk/components/task-list); the page carries no publication date.
- **The author's later positions.** Posts on adamsilver.io restate, with new detail, the book's rules on disabled buttons (2023-05-14, "Just enable the button", naming no exception), sticky menus (2023-05-07), nested fieldsets (2023-05-28, which now allows conditionally revealed day/month/year boxes as an acceptable nesting), and optional markers (2023-06-11, "add '(optional)' to the label of any optional field"). A 2023-06-04 post argues against automatically focusing the first input on page load; the book's focus moves are after a user's click (Ch. 6, p. 274; Ch. 9, p. 363), a different case. A 2025-02-03 post holds that multi-column forms have no exception that survives scrutiny; the book's three-box date and inline stepper are not discussed there.
- **The Carer's Allowance 12-step progress bar removed with no effect (Ch. 2, p. 113).** Confirmed: Ben Holliday, GDS design notes, 2014-07-07, "completion rates stayed exactly the same while other key metrics, like time to completion and the total amount of online applications, were also unaffected" (designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2014/07/07/do-less-problems-as-shared-spaces).
- **Baymard on breaking the back button (Ch. 7, p. 297).** Confirmed live and dated 2015-07-01, wording "confusion, disappointment, anger and even abandonments" (baymard.com/blog/macys-filtering-experience). The field-width study (Ch. 2, p. 82) and the inline-scroll finding (Ch. 7, p. 300) were not fetched and are unchecked.
- **Luke Wroblewski's inline validation evidence (Ch. 1, p. 61).** The source is his 2009-09-01 A List Apart article: 22 participants, six variants, 42% faster completion, 22% fewer errors, 31% higher satisfaction, with the benefit concentrated in fields whose answers users could not predict (alistapart.com/article/inline-validation-in-web-forms). The book cites it in one clause and argues against it on reasoning rather than counter-evidence; see section 8.
- **Matteo Penzo's eye-tracking on label placement (Ch. 1, p. 31).** The source is a 2006-07-12 UXmatters article reporting saccade times of about 50ms for labels above against 500ms beside, with no stated sample size (uxmatters.com, "Label Placement in Forms"). The quoted sentence is verbatim.
- **Spool's "$300 Million Button" (Ch. 2, p. 74).** Confirmed: 2009-01-14, a Register button replaced by Continue, purchases up 45%, $15 million the first month, $300 million the first year (articles.centercentre.com/three_hund_million_button).
- **Whitenton's tray pattern for mobile filters (Ch. 7, p. 310).** Confirmed live, NN/g, 2015-07-26 (nngroup.com/articles/mobile-faceted-search).
- **The inclusive design principles (Introduction, p. xv).** Live at inclusivedesignprinciples.info, credited to Henny Swan, Ian Pouncey, Heydon Pickering and Léonie Watson; the book credits the Paciello Group, their employer at the time.
- **GOV.UK Verify as the site of Jarrett's username/password test (Ch. 2, p. 71).** Verify closed in April 2023 and was replaced by GOV.UK One Login (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOV.UK_Verify). The finding stands as history.
- **Kidly, the author's client for the checkout and social-login examples (Ch. 2, p. 94; Ch. 4, p. 216).** kidly.co.uk now redirects to babymori.com, where "KIDLY by MORI" is a brand section of the MORI baby-clothing shop; the site the book describes no longer exists as its own store.
- **Stripe's "bare minimum" of card number, CVV and expiration date (Ch. 2, p. 96).** Stripe's current guidance routes card collection through the Payment Element, an embeddable component that "validates input, and handles errors" and renders the card fields itself, with address collected by a separate Address Element (docs.stripe.com/payments/payment-element). The three-field minimum is no longer something an integrator lays out by hand.
- **The WAI-ARIA combobox: `aria-owns`, focus moved into the options, `aria-activedescendant` set aside (Ch. 3, pp. 131, 138).** The current ARIA Authoring Practices combobox pattern keeps DOM focus on the textbox and moves assistive-technology focus with `aria-activedescendant`, and notes that ARIA 1.0's `aria-owns` was replaced by `aria-controls` (w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/combobox).
- **Medium's passwordless sign-in and Facebook login (Ch. 1, p. 28; Ch. 4, pp. 215–217).** medium.com/m/signin returned HTTP 403 to the fetch; unchecked.
- **The jam study (24 jams, 3%; 6 jams, 30%) and Vanguard's 2% per ten funds (Ch. 4, p. 218).** The 2010 meta-analysis of choice overload (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd, *Journal of Consumer Research*) could not be fetched (HTTP 403), and Wikipedia's Overchoice article cites it without stating its effect size; the replication record of these figures is unchecked.
- **Capture+ as the address-lookup plugin (Ch. 2, pp. 82–83).** Loqate's Address Capture page does not name Capture+ or PCA Predict, so the succession is unchecked.

Each is a screenshot, an interview, or a browser behavior of its year.

## 8. Where the book is weakest

- **Correlation presented as cause.** The book's headline number, Just Eat's "whopping 5% increase in conversion" from one thing per page (Ch. 2, p. 69), came from a redesign of the whole checkout, with no control and no separation of the page split from everything else that changed; the Boots result before it is offered with "I can't remember the exact numbers" (p. 69). The $300 million button (p. 74) is Spool's secondhand account of one unnamed retailer. The chapter's list of reasons the pattern works (pp. 72–73) is argued, not measured.
- **Evidence cited and then set aside.** The one study the book names on live validation found forms 42% faster and errors 22% fewer (Ch. 1, p. 61; section 7), and the book rejects live validation on reasoning about timing without a study of its own. The reverse also happens: MailChimp's row-highlighting decision is read as "we can assume their research showed this to be enough" (Ch. 5, p. 230), which is assumption presented as evidence.
- **Reasons that have expired.** A large share of the markup decisions rest on browsers of 2018: the hint inside the label because IE11 lacked `aria-describedby` (Ch. 1, p. 25), the `hidden` class over the attribute (p. 56), the `datalist` verdict (Ch. 3, p. 128), the custom date picker for desktop Safari and IE (p. 159), the iOS 10 blur bug that shaped the autocomplete (p. 140), and `focusable="false"` on every SVG (pp. 133, 169). Section 7 has the later state of each. The eighteen pages of date-picker code (pp. 161–178) build a fallback whose reason the author himself expected to lapse (p. 179), and it has.
- **Markup slips in the code the patterns are meant to be copied from.** The stepper's minus button carries `aria-label="Add"` and the plus button `aria-label="Remove"` (Ch. 3, p. 183). The inline error example prints the message twice, once with the icon and once without (Ch. 1, p. 57). The autocomplete's prose says `role="list"` where its markup uses `listbox` (Ch. 3, p. 133), and its `aria-owns` is the ARIA 1.0 form the current authoring practices replaced with `aria-controls` (section 7). The group error puts `aria-invalid` on the `<fieldset>` (p. 188); this repo's `RadioGroup` records that the attribute "is not supported on `role="group"`".
- **A law borrowed for a different finding.** The jam and Vanguard figures are about how many people buy or enroll; the book says the phenomenon "is actually called Hick's law", which is a law about the time a decision takes (Ch. 4, p. 218). The two are not the same claim, and the book's own point, that more sign-in methods may burden users, rests on the first.
- **Examples from the author's own work and from research he reports secondhand.** Boots, Just Eat and Kidly are the author's clients (Ch. 2, pp. 68–69, 94; Ch. 4, p. 216); the GDS findings on select boxes, radio labels, progress bars, dates and file pickers are a talk, a blog post, and interviews rather than published studies (Ch. 3, p. 125; Ch. 2, pp. 87, 113; Ch. 3, pp. 155–156; Ch. 8, p. 342); Gumtree's research is one designer's recollection (Ch. 7, pp. 290, 309). The book discloses each source; none can be re-run from what it prints.
- **Internal tension.** Styling a file input's label as a button is judged not to "justify the downgrade in usability and utility" on p. 320 and used, as "the ill-advised technique from earlier", on p. 326 (Ch. 8), with the reason given. Modes are to be avoided (Ch. 5, p. 228) and then designed. The hint and the error both go inside the `<label>` so that the label's hit area grows (Ch. 1, pp. 24, 57), which also makes the error text part of the control's accessible name; GOV.UK has since moved both out (section 7). The responsive menu is switched between "small mode" and "big mode" on a viewport `em` query (Ch. 5, pp. 248–250), the kind of width-based switch the same chapter calls a last resort three pages earlier (p. 245), and the book has no answer for a component that has to adapt to the space its container gives it rather than to the window.
- **Numbers without a source a reader can check.** "9 billion times/month" is a tweet (Ch. 2, p. 97); the 0.5% and £250,000 come from a 2009 blog post relaying a conference talk (Ch. 1, p. 64); "a font size of at least 16 pixels" and "at least 44px" are stated as probabilities ("this probably means") with links to secondary posts (p. 32); "Airlines, however, fly to a finite set of destinations" is the whole argument against a search box (Ch. 3, p. 127).
- **The jQuery-and-prototype code.** Every component is written in 2018 jQuery with constructor functions and `$.proxy` (Ch. 1, pp. 41–43), which the author flags as a convenience; a reader in 2026 has to translate every listing, and the book's claim that its patterns are "reusable and transferable" (Introduction, p. x) is truer of the decisions than of the code that carries them.
- **What the chapters do not reach.** No chapter covers a form filled in on behalf of someone else, a form edited long after it was first saved, a settings screen, or a screen that is both a record and a form; the closest is chapter 10's task list, which is about a single long submission. Nothing addresses right-to-left text, localization of the three-box date order it recommends, or what a form does when the server rejects a value the client accepted, beyond "not all errors can be caught this way" (Ch. 1, p. 52).
